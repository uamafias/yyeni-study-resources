# YYeni Business Subject Profile

**Version:** 0.1.0-draft  
**Machine-readable companion:** `business_subject_profile.yaml`

## 1. Purpose of the profile

This profile tells the universal YYeni generation engine what high-quality Business teaching material looks like. It does not define a particular syllabus. The qualification profile defines the board, level, papers, assessment objectives, and topic scope. This profile defines the subject-specific treatment of Business concepts, contexts, calculations, analysis, evaluation, and learning items.

The intended outcome is a learner who can:

- use precise business vocabulary;
- understand how business decisions, functions, stakeholders, and external conditions interact;
- apply concepts to unfamiliar organisations and situations;
- build valid cause-and-effect reasoning;
- interpret quantitative and qualitative information;
- compare alternatives and trade-offs;
- make justified, contextual recommendations; and
- explain implementation conditions and risks.

## 2. Foundational Business concepts

The following ideas should recur across the qualification and remain consistent in the global glossary:

- **Context:** the same decision may produce different results in different businesses.
- **Decision-making:** businesses choose between alternatives under constraints and uncertainty.
- **Objectives:** a decision should be judged partly by whether it helps achieve the relevant objective.
- **Stakeholders:** decisions create different benefits, costs, risks, and reactions for different groups.
- **Change:** internal and external conditions alter the suitability of business decisions.
- **Trade-offs:** improving one outcome may weaken another.
- **Risk and uncertainty:** future outcomes are not guaranteed.
- **Time horizon:** short-term and long-term effects may differ.
- **Implementation:** a theoretically suitable decision may fail if execution is weak.
- **Evidence:** qualitative and quantitative information should support reasoning and judgement.

These concepts should not appear only in an introductory page. They should shape examples, explanations, questions, and evaluations throughout the resources.

## 3. Business objective types

### 3.1 Concept or definition

Use for terms such as labour turnover, market share, working capital, or capacity utilisation.

A complete treatment normally includes:

1. one canonical definition;
2. a plain-language explanation;
3. essential features or components;
4. boundaries and related concepts;
5. one example and, where useful, a non-example;
6. why the concept matters to a business;
7. common misconceptions;
8. retrieval questions that test more than word recognition.

### 3.2 Business process

Use for recruitment, workforce planning, market research, decision-making, or product development.

A complete treatment normally includes:

1. purpose;
2. starting information or inputs;
3. ordered stages;
4. decisions made at each stage;
5. outputs;
6. reasons the order matters;
7. consequences of missing or weakening a stage;
8. contextual variations;
9. a worked example and process-sequencing practice.

### 3.3 Method, policy, or strategy

Use for internal recruitment, penetration pricing, just-in-time inventory, or bank loans.

A complete treatment normally includes:

1. definition;
2. how it operates;
3. purpose;
4. developed benefits;
5. developed limitations;
6. stakeholder effects;
7. short-term and long-term consequences;
8. contexts in which it is more or less suitable;
9. implementation conditions;
10. a reasoned comparison with relevant alternatives.

### 3.4 Comparison or distinction

Use for redundancy versus dismissal, internal versus external finance, or primary versus secondary research.

The comparison must use shared dimensions. Do not place unrelated facts opposite one another. Useful dimensions include purpose, source, cost, speed, risk, control, flexibility, stakeholders, information quality, and suitable context.

### 3.5 Relationship or impact

Use for the impact of training on a business, the relationship between price and demand, or the effects of a stakeholder decision.

A complete treatment should identify:

- the variables involved;
- positive and negative pathways;
- the mechanism linking cause and consequence;
- stakeholder effects;
- indicators or measures;
- contextual factors that strengthen, weaken, or reverse the effect;
- possible feedback loops.

### 3.6 Theory or model

Use for motivation theories, Boston Matrix, or decision trees.

A complete treatment should include:

- central proposition;
- assumptions;
- components;
- mechanism;
- application to business decisions;
- strengths;
- limitations;
- comparison with alternatives;
- contexts in which the model is more or less useful;
- risks of treating the model as universally predictive.

### 3.7 Quantitative measure or technique

Use for labour turnover, market growth, contribution, break-even, or capacity utilisation.

A complete treatment must include:

- precise meaning;
- exact formula;
- definition of every variable;
- units;
- calculation steps;
- simple worked example;
- contextual worked example;
- interpretation;
- appropriate comparisons;
- decision use;
- limitations;
- common calculation and interpretation errors.

### 3.8 Business decision or recommendation

Use when the learner must advise, assess, evaluate, justify, or recommend.

A complete treatment should include:

- options;
- criteria;
- evidence;
- trade-offs;
- stakeholder effects;
- short-term and long-term considerations;
- risk and uncertainty;
- decisive factor;
- strongest counterargument;
- implementation conditions;
- clear conclusion.

### 3.9 Case and data interpretation

Use for written, numerical, tabular, graphical, or diagrammatic stimulus.

Learners should be taught to:

- select relevant case facts;
- distinguish useful evidence from background detail;
- perform calculations accurately;
- interpret results in the case;
- connect facts to mechanisms and consequences;
- identify information limitations;
- reach a contextual conclusion.

## 4. Knowledge and understanding standard

A Business definition must be precise enough to distinguish the concept from close alternatives. It should not be circular, overbroad, or dependent on unstated context.

The notes should:

- introduce technical terms before using them;
- use one canonical definition across topics;
- preserve official syllabus terminology;
- explain rather than merely list;
- distinguish concept meaning from examples;
- teach common confusions directly;
- use British English consistently.

A learner should be able to explain the concept in their own words without changing its meaning.

## 5. Application standard

### 5.1 What counts as application

Application occurs when a fact about a particular business or situation changes, sharpens, or limits the reasoning.

Weak application:

> Internal recruitment may help the hotel.

Strong application:

> Internal recruitment may help the hotel fill a reception-manager vacancy quickly because an existing supervisor already understands the booking system, room categories, and service standards. This may reduce induction time during the peak tourism season.

The strong version uses context to explain why the method matters.

### 5.2 Required context dimensions

Across a topic, use a balanced range of:

- primary, secondary, tertiary, and where relevant quaternary sectors;
- goods, services, and digital products;
- small owner-managed, medium, large, and multinational businesses;
- private-sector, public-sector, and social-enterprise organisations;
- low-skilled, specialist, technical, professional, and customer-facing work;
- labour-intensive and capital-intensive operations;
- stable, growth, decline, crisis, and technological-change conditions;
- different financial positions and stakeholder pressures.

A topic does not need every context. It should use enough contrast to show why context affects decisions.

### 5.3 Worked application policy

Every major objective should normally have at least one fully worked application vignette. Every major decision or method comparison should include contrasting contexts when the conclusion changes between them.

An industry label alone does not satisfy the requirement.

## 6. Analysis standard

### 6.1 Core chain

A Business analysis chain should normally follow:

```text
decision or condition
    -> immediate effect
    -> operational, behavioural, financial, or market mechanism
    -> measurable consequence
    -> effect on an objective or stakeholder
```

Example:

> Effective induction training may help new employees understand safety procedures sooner. This can reduce early mistakes and accidents, lowering disruption and supervision requirements. If the savings exceed the training cost, average costs may fall and service reliability may improve.

### 6.2 Analysis rules

- Include a real mechanism.
- Use conditional language unless the result is certain.
- Do not jump directly from a decision to higher profit.
- Name the affected objective or stakeholder.
- Develop the most relevant chain rather than adding links for length.
- Include a material counter-effect where it could reverse the conclusion.
- Distinguish evidence from opinion.
- Do not treat correlation as proof of causation.

## 7. Evaluation standard

### 7.1 Core judgement structure

A high-quality judgement normally contains:

1. a clear answer;
2. the relevant business context;
3. the decisive criterion and why it matters;
4. the strongest counterargument;
5. a condition for success or implementation.

Example:

> External recruitment is likely to be more suitable when the business requires a technical skill that does not exist internally, despite its higher cost and longer process. The decisive issue is whether the new capability is essential to the expansion. Internal development may be preferable only if a suitable employee can be trained before the expansion deadline.

### 7.2 Evaluation dimensions

Relevant dimensions include:

- objectives;
- cost and available finance;
- urgency;
- size and organisational complexity;
- sector and nature of output;
- employee skill and motivation;
- stakeholder reactions;
- short-term versus long-term effects;
- risk and uncertainty;
- reversibility;
- reliability of data;
- external environment;
- implementation quality.

### 7.3 Evaluation rules

- “It depends” is not evaluation unless the factor and its effect are explained.
- Do not force equal weight onto unequal arguments.
- Identify the most important factor.
- Answer the precise question.
- State which option is preferable, for whom, over what period, and under what conditions.
- Where appropriate, recommend implementation actions.

## 8. Quantitative Business content

For every formula or quantitative technique:

1. use the exact approved formula;
2. define variables and units;
3. show working;
4. interpret the result in words;
5. compare it with a target, prior period, competitor, or benchmark when possible;
6. explain how a manager might use the result;
7. explain limitations and missing information;
8. provide calculation and interpretation practice.

A numerical answer without interpretation is incomplete.

## 9. Stakeholder and cross-functional reasoning

Business decisions should not be analysed only from the perspective of profit.

Where relevant, consider:

- owners or shareholders;
- employees and managers;
- customers;
- suppliers;
- lenders;
- government;
- local communities;
- pressure groups;
- the environment.

Connect functional areas where appropriate. For example, an HR training decision may affect operations quality, marketing reputation, finance costs, and customer satisfaction.

## 10. Legal and jurisdiction rules

For an international qualification:

- teach the general principle;
- state that exact law varies by country;
- avoid universal thresholds or procedures unless the syllabus specifies them;
- verify jurisdiction-specific factual claims;
- treat employment law, taxation, consumer protection, health and safety, equality, and trade-union rights as high-risk areas.

## 11. Learning-item architecture

### 11.1 Item types

Business resources should use a mix of:

- flashcards;
- short-answer questions;
- calculations;
- worked examples;
- case-analysis tasks;
- data-response questions;
- essay plans;
- full essay questions;
- source or evidence interpretation where relevant.

Flashcards cannot replace extended-response practice.

### 11.2 Flashcard subtypes

- `DEF` — definition
- `FEATURE` — component or feature
- `DIST` — distinction
- `PROC` — process
- `WHY` — purpose or reason
- `MECH` — mechanism
- `BEN` — benefit
- `LIM` — limitation
- `APP` — application
- `CHAIN` — analysis chain
- `EVAL` — evaluation
- `CALC` — calculation
- `INTERP` — interpretation
- `MISCON` — misconception correction
- `SYNTH` — connection across concepts

### 11.3 Flashcard rules

Each flashcard must:

- test one main target;
- be understandable without the previous card;
- map to approved claims;
- use a complete canonical answer;
- avoid revealing the answer in the question;
- avoid unnecessary list memorisation;
- avoid semantic duplication;
- form part of one unified topic bank.

Use difficulty and extension as metadata, not as separate incompatible card structures.

## 12. Depth allocation

Use the machine-readable tiers in the YAML profile. The word and item ranges are planning guides, not quotas.

Depth should depend on:

- conceptual complexity;
- mark tariff;
- observed assessment evidence;
- misconception risk;
- prerequisite importance;
- cross-topic reuse;
- calculation or procedural demands;
- need for contextual evaluation.

Do not lengthen a section after mastery requirements are met. Replace repetition with stronger examples, practice, or cross-links.

## 13. Business-specific failure patterns

Reject or revise content that contains:

- undeveloped advantage and disadvantage lists;
- a chain that jumps straight to profit;
- context that consists only of a company name;
- a generic conclusion that says only “it depends”;
- absolute claims about uncertain business outcomes;
- one-country law presented as universal;
- fabricated facts about real companies;
- repeated definitions with different wording;
- fixed flashcard quotas that create duplicates;
- enrichment mixed into the examinable core;
- one-sided treatment of stakeholder effects;
- a calculation with no interpretation;
- evaluation that repeats earlier points without weighing them.

## 14. Business semantic-review checklist

A reviewer should ask:

1. Is every definition precise and consistent?
2. Does each major objective include genuine application?
3. Do examples span meaningfully different business contexts?
4. Are analysis chains causally defensible?
5. Do chains stop at the most relevant objective or stakeholder outcome rather than defaulting to profit?
6. Does every major decision contain a decisive contextual judgement?
7. Are quantitative results correct, interpreted, compared, and limited?
8. Are stakeholder trade-offs represented where relevant?
9. Is the content proportionate to the syllabus and likely assessment demand?
10. Could the learner transfer the knowledge to an unfamiliar case?
11. Are recurring terms aligned with the global glossary?
12. Is any A Level, optional, or out-of-route content presented as core?
13. Are legal claims appropriately qualified?
14. Do flashcards and practice tasks cover all required cognitive levels without duplication?

## 15. Required output layers for Business

A complete Business topic should normally produce:

1. **Core Mastery Notes** — precise and proportionate understanding.
2. **Worked Application and Analysis** — contextual examples and chains.
3. **Examination Practice** — command-word and data-response practice.
4. **Retrieval Bank** — comprehensive, non-duplicative cards and short retrieval.
5. **Teacher Audit** — coverage, evidence, repairs, exclusions, and QA.

All five layers must be generated from the same approved objective and claim base.
