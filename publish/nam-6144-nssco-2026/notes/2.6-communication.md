## Effective communication

### Effective communication

Effective communication occurs when the intended message reaches the intended receiver and is understood as intended. Sending a message is not sufficient evidence that this happened. Business communication coordinates work, explains objectives, supports decisions and maintains relationships with staff, customers and suppliers. An accurate production instruction can still fail if it arrives after the work is completed or uses language the receiver does not understand. Feedback helps test understanding, especially where the message requires action. Communication should therefore fit the audience, purpose, urgency and need for a record. Effectiveness does not require the receiver to agree with every proposal: a worker can understand an unwelcome change clearly and still object. Managers should distinguish disagreement from misunderstanding and allow appropriate questions.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Owa Bakery changes tomorrow’s start time but sends the notice to an account some staff cannot access. Writing the correct time has not ensured effective communication. The manager must reach the affected staff before the change and confirm understanding, reducing late arrivals and disrupted preparation.

## Classifying communication

### Classifying communication

Internal communication takes place within the business; external communication crosses its boundary to customers, suppliers or other outside parties. One-way communication sends information without a response as part of that exchange; two-way communication includes feedback between sender and receiver. These distinctions describe different features and can be combined. An internal staff discussion is internal and two-way; a supplier conversation is external and two-way; a notice to staff can be internal and one-way. Written does not automatically mean one-way, since an email exchange can include replies, and speaking is not automatically two-way if no response is allowed. Identify the parties and the opportunity for feedback instead of assuming the medium settles every classification.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nali Goods posts a staff notice that allows no replies, then its manager phones a supplier and discusses a delayed delivery. The notice is internal and one-way. The supplier discussion is external and two-way. The classifications depend on the business boundary and feedback, not whether one channel is electronic.

### Internal communication

Internal communication is communication between people or groups within the same business. It can move downward as instructions, upward as reports or horizontally between departments. The direction does not change its internal boundary. Suitable internal communication helps staff coordinate and raise problems before commitments are missed.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Kira Stores’ sales team asks its warehouse team whether a product is available. Both teams are inside Kira, so the exchange is internal. A later reply to a customer would be external.

### External communication

External communication is communication between a business and parties outside it, such as suppliers, customers, lenders or government bodies. It can inform, negotiate, request or respond. Confidentiality and accuracy matter because misleading information can damage relationships or decisions. Electronic contact can be either internal or external; identify the participants.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nura Print asks its paper supplier to confirm a delivery date. The supplier is outside Nura’s organisation, so the exchange is external even if the two firms work closely together.

### One-way communication

One-way communication transmits a message without a return response as part of that communication. A notice or announcement can deliver consistent information quickly to many people, but the sender cannot immediately use questions to check understanding. It may suit simple information, while complex or disputed changes often need an opportunity for feedback.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Hila Goods posts a closing-date announcement and offers no response channel in that notice. The notice is one-way in the described exchange. A later discussion would be a separate feedback opportunity rather than proof the original notice checked understanding.

### Two-way communication

Two-way communication includes feedback or exchange between sender and receiver. Questions, replies and clarification can expose misunderstandings and improve decisions. It may take more time than a simple announcement, and it works only if people can respond meaningfully. It need not be face to face: a written exchange can also be two-way.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Omi Foods’ manager emails a proposed rota and staff reply with questions that the manager answers. The written exchange is two-way because responses and clarification are included. Written does not automatically mean one-way.

## Choosing a way and a method

### Choosing a way and a method

Verbal communication uses spoken words, written communication uses text and visual communication uses images, symbols or graphical displays. Letters and meetings are methods, while these ways describe how meaning is expressed. A meeting can combine spoken discussion, written handouts and a visual diagram. Verbal discussion can give fast clarification and personal contact, but may leave no reliable record unless recorded appropriately. Written text provides something to consult and can specify details, but the receiver must access and understand it; replies may not be immediate. Visuals can make a layout or trend clearer, but unfamiliar symbols or missing labels can mislead. Choose using urgency, audience access and language, complexity, confidentiality, cost and the need for feedback or proof. Combining methods can be more suitable than claiming one is best for every message.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Uda Repairs must explain a new machine layout to workers and retain the agreed operating instructions. A spoken demonstration with a labelled layout can clarify positions and allow questions; a concise written instruction provides a later reference. An unlabelled picture or a long letter alone may not resolve practical misunderstandings.

### Verbal communication

Verbal communication uses spoken words, for example conversation, a telephone call or a spoken presentation. It can suit urgent clarification and sensitive discussion where tone helps meaning. Without a retained record, details may be forgotten or disputed. Noise, unfamiliar terms or not allowing questions can make spoken communication ineffective.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Veya Repairs phones a customer to clarify an unusual repair request. The call can resolve questions quickly. The final agreed work and price should be confirmed in a suitable record if needed, rather than assuming memory will preserve every detail.

### Written communication

Written communication conveys meaning through text, such as letters, emails, instructions or reports. It can preserve details for later reference and reach people who cannot meet at once. It depends on access and reading comprehension and may delay clarification. A written record demonstrates the content supplied, but not automatically that every intended person read or understood it.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Ema Clothing sends detailed order specifications by an accessible email. The specifications can be consulted during production. Confirming the supplier’s understanding helps prevent an unnoticed ambiguity from becoming an incorrect order.

### Visual communication

Visual communication conveys information through images, symbols, diagrams, charts or other graphical representations. It can make spatial layouts or patterns easier to understand than a long verbal description. Clear labels, an appropriate scale and audience familiarity matter; a symbol that nobody recognises does not communicate effectively. Visuals often work best with a short explanation.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Tola Stores uses a labelled floor plan to show where stock should be placed. The plan can make the required location clear. Missing labels or an unclear orientation would limit its usefulness even if the drawing looks attractive.

### Letters

A business letter is a written message addressed to a particular recipient, commonly used for formal detailed communication. It can provide a record, explain terms and present information in an organised form. Delivery and response can take time, and the reader must understand the wording. Choose an appropriately secure and timely delivery method instead of assuming a letter suits an immediate emergency.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Owa Supply writes to a customer confirming agreed delivery terms. A retained letter can record the agreement. It is less suitable as the sole method for an urgent change if the customer will receive it after dispatch.

### Meetings

A meeting brings participants together, physically or through a suitable communication channel, to share information, discuss issues or make decisions. It can allow questions and draw on several perspectives, but consumes time and may be dominated by a few voices. An agenda can focus the discussion and minutes can record decisions, responsibilities and deadlines. Holding a meeting does not itself ensure everyone understands or agrees.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Iha Foods brings production and sales staff together to coordinate a major order. The meeting can resolve conflicting promises through immediate discussion. Recording the agreed quantity, date and responsible people helps prevent different memories afterwards.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why “sender and feedback” does not answer a request for two ways of communication. Fictional Kena Goods uses a spoken briefing and a written reminder for a rota change.

Sender and feedback are elements of the communication process: who originates the message and the response. Spoken or verbal and written are ways in which the message is expressed. Internal/external is another classification about the business boundary. In Kena’s case the briefing is verbal and the reminder written; naming process elements would not identify those ways.

## The communication process

### The communication process

The sender originates the message, the message is the information intended, the medium is the channel used, timing concerns when it is sent and received, the receiver is the intended recipient, and feedback is a response indicating receipt or understanding. The elements must work together. A correct message sent through an inaccessible channel fails to reach its receiver. A clear instruction received too late cannot guide the intended action. Feedback can reveal a misunderstanding so the sender can clarify it; merely observing that a system delivered a notification does not prove comprehension. Good communication checks who needs to know, what they need to understand, how to reach them and by when. Use plain wording and relevant detail rather than overload the receiver with unrelated information.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Tuli Foods’ supervisor tells the evening shift that tomorrow’s dispatch deadline is noon, uses an accessible staff channel before the shift ends and asks the shift leader to repeat the arrangement. The supervisor is sender, the changed deadline is the message, the staff channel is the medium, the pre-departure contact is timing, the evening shift is receiver and the repeated arrangement is feedback. An incorrect repetition would identify a need for clarification before the error affects delivery.

### Sender

The sender is the person or group originating the message. The sender should identify the intended receiver, purpose and essential information, then choose accessible wording and a suitable medium. Responsibility for clear communication is not fulfilled simply by saying that a notice was sent. The sender can use feedback to identify the need for clarification.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Olina Works’ supervisor informs staff of a revised safety procedure. The supervisor is the sender. Choosing familiar terms and asking staff to explain the change can reveal whether the intended meaning was conveyed.

### Message

The message is the information or meaning the sender intends to convey. It needs sufficient accuracy and detail for the purpose, such as the task, responsible person and deadline. Ambiguous phrases can let different receivers infer different actions. More words do not necessarily improve a message if important instructions are buried in unrelated detail.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Tenga Stores replaces “bring the stock soon” with “move these boxes to shelf B before 15:00 today”. The more precise message identifies what, where and when, reducing the scope for conflicting interpretations. The shelf location should still be understandable to the receiver.

### Media

Media are the channels or means used to carry messages; medium is the singular. A phone call, email or noticeboard can carry information in different circumstances. Select a channel using access, urgency, confidentiality, complexity and the need for feedback or a record. The channel is distinct from the message’s actual content.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Kara Repairs uses a phone call to tell an absent technician about an urgent schedule change. The call is the medium, while the revised schedule is the message. An email account the technician cannot access would be a poor alternative for the stated urgency.

### Timing

Timing concerns when communication is sent, received and available for action. A clear and accurate instruction can fail if it reaches the receiver after the decision or task. Too many messages at a busy moment can also reduce attention. Allow time for access, clarification and the required response rather than treating the sending time as the entire process.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Hano Foods’ workers need a revised start time before leaving the previous shift. Giving the update after they arrive the next day is too late to change that arrival. Send and confirm it while they can still adjust their plans.

### Receiver

The receiver is the intended person or group to whom the message is directed. Effective communication depends on reaching the right receiver and on their ability to understand and act. The receiver may need clarification and should provide feedback where appropriate. Sending the information to someone with no relevant responsibility may not achieve the intended action.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Tuli Metals needs its purchasing officer to change an order but sends the request only to a salesperson. The purchasing officer is the intended action receiver. Unless the salesperson reliably passes it on, the required decision may not happen.

### Feedback

Feedback is the receiver’s response to a message, which helps the sender assess receipt, understanding or reaction. A question can reveal ambiguity; repeating an instruction in the receiver’s own words can reveal a mistaken detail. An electronic delivery notification confirms transmission to a system, not necessarily human understanding. Feedback can be verbal, written or observable action appropriate to the message.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nela Foods’ worker repeats the new deadline as Friday although the supervisor intended Thursday. The incorrect repetition is useful feedback: it reveals a misunderstanding while the sender can still clarify. Silence would not provide the same evidence of understanding.

## Barriers and matching remedies

### Barriers and matching remedies

Communication barriers include noise, unfamiliar language or jargon, ambiguous wording, inaccessible channels, poor timing, information overload, long reporting chains and attitudes that discourage listening or honest feedback. Match the remedy to the cause. Move a safety briefing away from loud machinery; use plain language and check meaning where terms are unfamiliar; give dates and responsibilities instead of vague instructions; use a channel the receiver can access. Send information early enough to act and prioritise the essential message instead of burying it in many notices. Shorter communication routes can reduce distortion, while respectful opportunities for questions can reveal hidden misunderstanding. Repeating the same unclear message more loudly does not necessarily solve a language problem. A method is effective in its circumstances, not because it is modern or widely used.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Osa Workshop announces a changed shift beside a running machine. Several workers hear different times and do not ask because they fear criticism. Pause for a quiet briefing, state the exact day and time, provide an accessible written reminder and invite workers to confirm the arrangement without blame. This addresses noise, ambiguity and fear of feedback rather than assuming a notice sent later will repair tomorrow’s missed start.
