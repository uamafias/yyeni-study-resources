## Ownership sectors and organisation

### Ownership sectors and organisation

Public and private sectors classify activity by ownership and control. A public-sector enterprise is owned and controlled by the state, whereas private-sector activity is owned and controlled by private individuals or organisations. A government-owned bus operator and an individually owned bus operator can provide the same service while belonging to different ownership sectors. Their activity does not settle their ownership. A mixed economy combines public and private activity. Public provision can pursue access to essential services or employment as well as financial sustainability. Private owners commonly seek profit and business survival, although a private organisation may also pursue social aims. Neither label proves that the organisation is efficient or that every service is free. Public enterprises may charge customers and private providers may supply services under government contracts. Organising privately can involve a sole trader, partnership or company; public activity can be organised through government departments or state-owned enterprises. Ownership forms are developed in topic 2.1. A company described as public limited has shares available to the public; that name does not by itself make it state owned. Ask who owns and controls it before assigning its ownership sector.

### Putting the idea to work

A municipal workshop, Ombili Services, is controlled by its local authority; nearby Ekuva Repairs is controlled by its individual owner. Both repair vehicles, but Ombili is public sector and Ekuva is private sector. Charging a repair fee does not change Ombili’s owner, and serving government vehicles does not make Ekuva a public enterprise.

### Public sector

The public sector comprises organisations owned and controlled by the state. State ownership, rather than whether the public uses a service, is the defining feature. Public provision may aim to extend access to essential services, while still needing to manage costs and finance its operations. A public enterprise may sell its output; charging customers does not turn state ownership into private ownership.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume the regional authority owns and controls Tano Passenger Services. Tano belongs to the public sector even when passengers pay fares. The fare is a source of revenue; the authority’s ownership is the classification test.

### Private sector

The private sector comprises activity owned and controlled by private individuals or organisations rather than the state. It includes individually owned businesses and companies, and can include organisations with social aims. Supplying a government customer does not by itself transfer ownership to government. A private company can grow very large while remaining private sector.

### Putting the idea to work

Amina owns Eteno Copy Shop and supplies printed worksheets to several state schools. The shop is private sector because Amina owns and controls it. The schools’ public status describes the customers, not the shop’s owner.

## Nationalisation and privatisation

### Nationalisation and privatisation

Nationalisation transfers a privately owned business or industry into state ownership and control. Privatisation transfers a state-owned business or activity into private ownership and control. The directions are opposite: private to state, or state to private. A new owner may change objectives, investment and decisions, but a change in owner is distinct from a guaranteed improvement in performance. Nationalisation may protect essential service access but creates state financing demands. Privatisation may introduce commercial incentives, but service conditions may be needed to protect unprofitable users. A sale to investors within Namibia is still privatisation if state ownership transfers to private owners. Nationalisation does not mean selling abroad or banning imports; those concern international trade, not a transfer of ownership. Identify the enterprise whose ownership changes.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume a state-owned coach company is sold entirely to private investors. In a separate event the state buys a privately owned water supplier. The coach sale is privatisation because ownership moves from state to private investors. The water purchase is nationalisation because it moves in the reverse direction. The buyers’ nationality does not determine either classification.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why selling shares to the public does not by itself nationalise a business. Assume all shares of Hano Ltd remain owned by private investors, although anyone eligible can buy them.

Nationalisation transfers a privately owned enterprise into state ownership and control. Here private investors still own the shares, so it remains private sector. Public availability of shares concerns a company’s ownership form, whereas public-sector status concerns state ownership; the word public is being used in two different ways.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain whether either event is nationalisation: a Namibian state authority buys a privately owned local coach firm, while a different private firm begins selling cushions to Botswana.

The state purchase is nationalisation because control and ownership move from private owners to the state. Selling cushions across a border is exporting, a form of international trade. No ownership transfer is stated for the cushion business. Nationalisation is tested by the change of owner, not the country where products are sold.

## Primary, secondary and tertiary activity

### Primary, secondary and tertiary activity

Activity sectors classify what is done. Primary businesses obtain natural resources; secondary businesses transform materials into products or carry out construction; tertiary businesses supply services. Follow the activity rather than the owner or product’s final user. Growing mahangu is primary, milling it into flour is secondary, and transporting or selling the flour is tertiary. Transport does not physically manufacture the flour even though it adds value by making it available where needed. A business can operate in more than one activity sector. An enterprise growing fruit, making juice and running its own shop has primary, secondary and tertiary operations. If asked to classify one operation, classify that operation. If describing the whole enterprise, explain its combination rather than assuming a single label covers every stage. Public/private ownership is a separate axis: a privately owned farm is both private sector and primary sector. Development of services does not remove the economy’s need for materials or manufactured goods; the sectors supply inputs and customers to one another.

### Putting the idea to work

Osha Clay extracts clay near Otjiwarongo, Imba Pots shapes and fires it, and Hela Couriers delivers the pots to buyers. Extraction is primary; changing clay into pots is secondary; delivery is tertiary. A privately owned courier remains tertiary even if a public school buys its service. Classifying the process avoids confusing a customer’s ownership with a supplier’s activity.

### Primary sector

The primary sector obtains natural resources from the land or sea. Farming, fishing, forestry and mineral extraction are examples. The classification concerns extraction or harvesting, before manufacturing transforms the resource into another product. Selling what a farm grows does not make the growing activity tertiary.

### Putting the idea to work

Luma Farm grows vegetables near Rundu. A separate factory cans them. Growing the vegetables is primary. The later canning is secondary because the factory processes the resource. Distinguish stages instead of giving all food-related activity one label.

### Secondary sector

The secondary sector transforms materials into manufactured products and includes construction. It takes inputs such as timber, flour or metal and changes them into an output such as a desk, loaf or gate. Being secondary does not mean less important; the term identifies the stage of activity. Delivery of a finished product is a separate service activity.

### Putting the idea to work

Ondira Frames purchases timber and makes window frames. The transformation from timber to frames is secondary. If another business merely sells the frames unchanged, its retail activity is tertiary.

### Tertiary sector

The tertiary sector provides services to consumers or other businesses. Retailing, transport, banking and repair services are examples. A service may support physical goods without manufacturing them: a carrier moves an existing product and a shop makes it available to buyers. A business’s classification follows its service activity rather than whether its customer is public or private.

### Putting the idea to work

Kina Deliveries transports parcels between shops and households. Moving the parcels is a tertiary activity. Kina is not a primary producer simply because one parcel contains farm produce.

## A set of business-size indicators

### A set of business-size indicators

Business size can be described through legal form of ownership, nature of business, nature of product, capital employed, total assets, number of employees, level of technology, market share, turnover, profitability and value of output. Two straightforward numerical measures are the number of employees and annual turnover. No single indicator ranks every business fairly. A highly automated producer may employ fewer people than a labour-intensive workshop but produce much more. Some indicators are descriptive clues, such as ownership form or technology, rather than a numerical scale. Others measure different things: turnover is sales revenue, profit is the surplus after costs, and total assets is the value of resources owned. Select a measure suited to the comparison and state the time period or valuation basis. Compare similar activities where possible and use several indicators if they disagree. Large does not necessarily mean successful, profitable or well managed. A small specialist may earn a high profit relative to its investment, while a large producer can make a loss.

### Putting the idea to work

Two furniture businesses each employ ten people. Tuli Furnishings has automated machinery and much higher annual sales than hand-finishing workshop Tameka Joinery. Employee count alone makes them look equal. Turnover and assets reveal differences in the scale of their operations, while neither measure by itself tells us whose customers are more satisfied.

### Legal form of ownership

Legal form identifies how a business is owned and organised, such as a sole trader, partnership or company. It is a descriptive clue to size because some company forms may make larger funding possible. Its limitation is that the form does not specify actual sales, employees or assets: a company can be small and a long-established individually owned business can be substantial.

### Putting the idea to work

Eha Bakery is a company employing two people; a nearby sole trader employs eight. The company label alone does not make Eha larger. Compare employee numbers or sales for the same period as well.

### Nature of business

Nature of business identifies the activity undertaken, such as mining, retailing or personal services. It helps choose meaningful size comparisons: mining often needs extensive equipment, while a personal service may depend mainly on labour. Its limitation is that activity is a category, not a measured quantity. A retailer is not automatically larger or smaller than a manufacturer.

### Putting the idea to work

A sewing workshop and a mineral-extraction site each employ twenty people. Equal employment can hide very different investment needs. The activity helps interpret asset figures but cannot itself give an exact size ranking.

### Nature of product

Nature of product describes what is supplied and its characteristics, such as a low-priced standard item or a high-value customised item. It helps explain why units of output can be misleading. Its limitation is that the kind or price of product does not alone establish the business’s total scale. One expensive item and many inexpensive items require a comparable monetary measure.

### Putting the idea to work

A boat builder completes three custom boats while a bakery makes thousands of rolls. Counting physical items alone would heavily favour the bakery. Compare annual value of output and investment alongside the nature of the products.

### Capital employed

Capital employed is the long-term finance used in a business, commonly measured as owners’ capital plus non-current liabilities, or total assets less current liabilities. A larger amount can indicate a greater scale of resources committed. Its limitation is that capital-intensive and labour-intensive activities need different investment, so it does not translate directly into jobs or sales. Use a consistent accounting basis.

### Putting the idea to work

Oruvatu Milling uses substantial machinery, while a tutoring enterprise works mainly through its staff. More capital employed at the mill would indicate more finance tied into its operation, not necessarily more employees or a higher return. Capital employed is a money amount, not the count of workers.

### Total assets

Total assets is the recorded value of all assets controlled by the business, including non-current and current assets. It indicates the resource base used to operate. Its limitation is that valuation dates and methods, the age of equipment and renting rather than owning premises can distort comparisons. A business with expensive assets may use them poorly or sell little output.

### Putting the idea to work

One transport firm owns its vehicles; another rents an equally capable fleet. The owner may record more assets even when both carry similar loads. Asset values should therefore be interpreted with capacity and turnover, rather than treated as a complete measure of activity.

### Number of employees

Number of employees measures the size of the workforce. It is straightforward and useful for comparing labour use in similar businesses. Its limitation is that automation and differing part-time hours make headcounts less comparable. A highly mechanised producer can have fewer workers but far more output. Where hours differ, compare equivalent working time rather than ignoring the difference.

### Putting the idea to work

A machine-equipped packaging business has six full-time workers and a manual packer has twelve. The second has the larger workforce. That does not establish larger output without knowing working hours and productivity. Do not change the question from employment size to overall success.

### Level of technology

Level of technology describes how advanced or extensive equipment and methods are. It can help explain productive capacity and why output is high with relatively few workers. Its limitation is that sophisticated equipment is a qualitative clue rather than a complete size measure: one advanced machine can belong to a small specialist. Underused technology need not produce large sales.

### Putting the idea to work

A small engraver owns a computer-controlled cutting machine but completes only a few orders each week. Its advanced equipment does not make it larger than every manual workshop. Check capacity used and annual output before drawing that conclusion.

### Market share

Market share is a business’s sales as a proportion of total sales in a defined market over a stated period. Use the same basis, such as sales value for both business and market. It indicates relative presence among competitors. Its limitation is that the market boundary matters: a large share of a tiny town market can represent fewer sales than a small share of a national market.

### Putting the idea to work

Tula Bread has half of a small settlement’s bread sales; another bakery has one tenth of a much larger regional market. Tula has the higher percentage in its defined market, but the other bakery may sell more bread. State the market before using market share to infer scale.

### Turnover

Turnover is sales revenue over a period. It indicates the monetary volume of sales and makes comparison of unlike products more useful than a physical unit count. Its limitation is that prices and the period covered affect it, and high revenue does not guarantee profit. A firm buying expensive goods for resale can have high turnover but a thin surplus after costs.

### Putting the idea to work

Kavesa Shop has annual sales revenue of N$900,000; a repairer reports annual profit of N$100,000. These figures cannot rank turnover because the repairer’s figure is profit. Obtain its annual sales revenue first. Revenue and profit are different measures.

### Profitability

Profitability concerns the ability to generate profit relative to a relevant base such as sales or capital employed. It can help compare financial performance, but its limitation as a size indicator is that efficiency or a high margin is not the same as large operations. Absolute profit is the surplus after costs; a profitability ratio relates that surplus to another figure. Neither proves large employment or assets.

### Putting the idea to work

A small specialist keeps a greater proportion of each N$1 of sales as profit than a large discount store. The specialist may be more profitable relative to sales while remaining smaller in turnover and staff. State the basis of comparison instead of equating a high percentage with a large enterprise.

### Value of output

Value of output measures production in monetary terms over a period. It allows outputs of different kinds or sizes to be expressed on a common money basis. Its limitation is that changing prices and different valuation methods affect the result, and produced goods may remain unsold. Therefore value of output is not automatically the same as turnover, which relates to sales.

### Putting the idea to work

A workshop makes furniture in December but sells some of it in January. December’s production value includes furniture still held at the period end, while December turnover includes only sales made in that period. Use matching periods and distinguish output from sales.

### Worked practice: Small-firm survival and sector/size distinctions

Omila Shade near Outjo makes customised garden shades from purchased fabric. It has three employees and a small rented workshop. A large supplier sells standard shades. Local households choose Omila for unusual sizes, and orders are sufficient for its present three-person operation. Omila could rent a bigger factory and concentrate on identical shades, but there is no firm demand for those extra units. For part (b) only, a guesthouse group now commits to a two-year order for identical shades sufficient to keep the proposed larger factory busy. All other facts are unchanged.

a Evaluate remaining small and customised under the original facts. Classify Omila’s activity and explain why one size measure alone is insufficient. [12]
b Evaluate the same choice after the stated guesthouse order arrives. Explain whether the original recommendation should change. [12]

a Omila manufactures shades and is secondary. Three employees describe workforce size but omit productive equipment, sales and the high value of customised products. Its present demand fits a small operation; unusual sizes and personal attention distinguish it from the large standard supplier. Expanding might allow more output, yet the bigger factory would create commitments before demand is established. Remain small because the niche is proven for current scale whereas the standard market is not. This judgement would weaken if dependable standard orders emerged. b The two-year order changes demand certainty, not the fact that manufacturing is secondary. Expansion can now use the larger factory for a specified customer rather than speculative stock; standardisation may reduce unit costs if the order uses capacity efficiently and coordination is controlled. Against this, reliance on one guesthouse group creates risk at contract end and the larger operation could displace custom work. Shift towards the larger operation only after checking that the agreed order covers its extra commitments and can be delivered with the resources available. The recommendation becomes more favourable to expansion because dependable demand replaces the original uncertainty, while renewal and customer dependence remain reasons to limit longer commitments.

### Worked practice: Small-firm survival and sector/size distinctions

Nareta Repairs in Mariental employs four people and repairs customers’ bicycles. It buys replacement parts from a manufacturer. A large retailer nearby sells standard bicycles but offers no repairs. Nareta earns most of its revenue from individual repair jobs and can discuss each problem directly with its customer. Its owner is considering replacing that personal service with a much larger standard bicycle assembly operation. No firm order for assembled bicycles exists.

a Explain why turnover and employee numbers might rank the repair business and the parts manufacturer differently. In your answer distinguish their activity sectors. [8]
b Evaluate whether the small repair firm is likely to survive alongside the large retailer if it keeps its present focus. [12]

a Repairs provide a tertiary service while the parts manufacturer transforms inputs in the secondary sector. Employee numbers measure labour use, whereas turnover measures sales revenue over a period. Repair jobs require direct labour and discussion; an automated manufacturer may sell a large volume with few staff. The manufacturer could therefore have higher turnover despite similar or lower headcount. Different prices also influence turnover, so sales value alone cannot establish physical output. Compare the same accounting period and use both indicators with relevant information about activity. b A small firm can survive where a niche and personal service provide enough paid demand to cover costs. Nareta fills the retailer’s stated repair gap, understands individual faults and can encourage repeat custom. Remaining small avoids committing to unconfirmed assembly demand. However, four people limit the number of repairs that can be handled, and dependence on individual jobs may leave revenue variable; the retailer could later introduce a competing service. Survival is plausible, not assured. Retain and monitor the repair focus while demand covers operating costs; this recommendation rests on the service gap, not on an assumption that every small business can undercut a large one. Reassess if repair revenue falls or a larger service contract appears.

## Why classify and measure businesses?

### Why classify and measure businesses?

Classification groups businesses by a shared characteristic, such as activity sector or ownership. Measurement indicates scale through an appropriate size indicator. Governments can use sector and employment information to identify where support or infrastructure may be needed. A lender can compare an applicant with businesses of similar activity and scale instead of interpreting its turnover without context. Managers can monitor changes over time and compare competitors. Investors may use size data as background, but size alone cannot establish return or safety. A classification is useful only if its categories fit the question. Primary/secondary/tertiary helps describe economic activity; public/private helps describe ownership. Neither directly ranks business size. Measurement must also be consistent: compare annual turnover over the same length of time and avoid comparing one firm’s sales revenue with another’s profit. Two businesses may be similar in employment but different in assets, so describe that result instead of forcing a single ranking. The purpose of the decision determines the most useful classification and measure.

### Putting the idea to work

A local planning office needs to assess employment opportunities in food processing. It receives employment totals for food factories and sales totals for farms. Food processing is secondary while farming is primary. The employment totals for the food factories are the relevant measure of food-processing jobs. The farm figures are sales revenue for a different, primary activity, so they cannot be added to or compared with factory employment; if the office also wants farm jobs, it needs farm employment figures for the same period.

## Why small firms can survive

### Why small firms can survive

Small firms can survive by meeting needs that large suppliers cannot serve profitably or flexibly. A narrow local market may support a small repairer but not a large branch. Personal service can help an owner learn customer preferences, adjust an order quickly and win repeat business. A specialist may make customised goods that do not suit a standard production line. Close contact between owner, workers and customers can shorten decisions. Low overhead commitments may also help, although a small firm does not automatically have a lower cost per unit. Large rivals may obtain purchasing advantages if orders are sufficiently large and coordination remains effective. Small firms face disadvantages such as limited finance, dependence on a few customers and the owner’s limited time. Survival therefore requires enough demand and revenue to cover costs, not merely being friendly. Some owners deliberately remain small to preserve control and a manageable workload. Others are newly established businesses that may later grow. Judge survival against the actual customers and resources: a niche can protect a small supplier, but losing a major customer can make that niche too narrow.

### Putting the idea to work

Nela Stitch, a three-person workshop in Keetmanshoop, alters individual garments within a day. A large clothing outlet nearby sells standard sizes and does not offer alterations. Nela serves a distinct tertiary service need with flexible personal attention. Customers needing a specific fit may pay for that convenience. Survival still depends on sufficient alteration orders to cover rent and wages; large retail sales nearby do not guarantee Nela’s demand.
