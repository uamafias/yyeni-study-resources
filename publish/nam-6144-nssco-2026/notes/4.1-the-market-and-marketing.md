## Markets and marketing

### Markets and marketing

A market brings buyers and sellers into contact for exchange of a particular good or service; it need not be a physical place. Marketing is the management process which identifies consumer wants and anticipates their future wants. It guides decisions about how an offering meets those wants through product, price, promotion and distribution. Research collects information and advertising communicates promotional messages; both can contribute to the wider marketing role but neither is its complete definition. Good marketing connects understanding customers with a feasible offering, rather than merely persuading people to buy whatever has already been made.

### Putting the idea to work

Nera Bags interviews potential buyers, adjusts bag capacity, chooses a price and arranges where the bags can be bought. The interviews are research within a wider marketing process. Decisions about the offering and access show why marketing is broader than an advertisement.

### A market

A market is an arrangement bringing buyers and sellers into contact to exchange a particular product or service. Contact can occur at a stall, through a shop, by telephone or online. Define what is being traded and the relevant buyers or area when comparing markets. A market is not the activity of marketing or merely the physical building where sales occur.

### Putting the idea to work

Oru Repairs accepts bookings through phone calls and completes repairs at customers’ homes. There is still a market for its repair service even without customers meeting at a market stall.

### Marketing

Marketing is the management process which identifies consumer wants and anticipates their future wants. It supports decisions about the offering and how customers obtain it, not simply promotional communication. Research is one information-gathering activity within it. A business needs to connect identified wants with realistic product, price, promotion and access choices.

### Putting the idea to work

Nalu Meals investigates preferences and adapts its meal and collection arrangements. The customer-focused process is marketing; a poster announcing collection times is only one promotional element.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why Tano Meals’ customer survey and poster are not complete definitions of marketing.

The survey is market research, collecting information; the poster is advertising, communicating a promotional message. Marketing is the wider process identifying and anticipating wants and guiding how an offering meets them. Product, price and access decisions remain beyond those two activities.

## Identify needs, design and review

### Identify needs, design and review

A business identifies customer needs through relevant research, sales patterns, feedback and observation. It then decides which customers to serve and designs a suitable product or service, price and access. A stated preference is not a guaranteed sale: affordability, competing products and the buying situation matter. Test a feasible offering and use feedback to improve it. Customer focus does not require accepting every request at any cost. A change must fit resources and earn sufficient revenue for the enterprise’s purpose. Customer needs can change, so an initial survey does not remove the need to review actual behaviour.

### Putting the idea to work

Omi Lunches learns that workers need portable meals collected quickly during a short break. A suitable response could combine secure packaging, a collection time and an affordable price, then review actual orders and complaints. Advertising the old slow service would not address the stated need.

## Objectives of marketing

### Objectives of marketing

Marketing objectives can include increasing sales, increasing market share, attracting new customers, retaining existing customers, developing awareness and supporting a successful new-product launch. They should fit wider business aims and available resources. State a result, not just an activity: running an advertisement is an action, whereas increasing awareness among a defined target group is an objective. A measurable target and time period make review possible. More sales are not automatically more profit if the cost of achieving them exceeds the added return.

### Putting the idea to work

Eha Goods wants repeat purchases from existing local customers over the next three months. Retention is the objective; a reminder message or improved service is a possible action. Management should review repeat purchases and cost rather than regard sending the reminder as proof of success.

## Segmenting and choosing a target

### Segmenting and choosing a target

Market segmentation divides a market into groups of consumers with similar needs. The syllabus bases include product, region, purpose, price, age and gender. Segments can overlap: buyers in one region can also seek budget-priced products for a particular purpose. Use evidence that the chosen grouping affects needs rather than assuming all people in a demographic category behave alike. Assess potential demand, purchasing ability, competition, reach and whether the business can serve the segment with its resources. A small enterprise may focus on a niche instead of trying to offer everything to everyone, but excessive dependence on a narrow group creates risk. A supported target decision links a real need with an achievable offering and recognises the strongest alternative.

### Putting the idea to work

Tula Bags can make durable work bags in small batches. Interviews indicate mechanics need strong tool compartments, while fashion customers want frequent style changes that Tula cannot provide. Targeting work-purpose buyers fits its capability and the stated need better than the fashion alternative. Confirm sufficient paid demand and feasible price before committing; the word mechanic alone does not prove a profitable market.

### Product segmentation

Product segmentation groups a wider market by the type or category of product sought. It can help a supplier focus its range and expertise, but a product label alone does not explain the customer need. Buyers within a category may still differ in price, use and preferred features.

### Putting the idea to work

Uta Sports distinguishes buyers seeking footwear from buyers seeking sports equipment. The categories can guide separate ranges and promotion. Further evidence is needed to choose the footwear features and price.

### Regional segmentation

Regional segmentation groups customers by geographical area. Climate, access, travel distance or local demand can make needs differ between areas. The grouping is useful when those differences affect the offering or delivery; it does not prove everyone in one area wants the same thing.

### Putting the idea to work

Oha Clothing compares demand for warm outerwear in colder inland areas with demand in warmer locations. Regional evidence can guide stock and delivery, while actual customer data should check the assumed climate-related need.

### Segmentation by purpose

Purpose segmentation groups customers by the use they want from a product. The same broad product can serve school, work, travel or recreation, requiring different features. This can be more informative than a demographic label where function drives the purchase. A buyer can belong to different purpose segments for different occasions.

### Putting the idea to work

Teya Bags distinguishes tool-carrying bags from small everyday leisure bags. Strength and internal compartments may matter for tools, while size and style may matter for leisure. Research should confirm those priorities before design.

### Price segmentation

Price segmentation groups demand by price level or willingness and ability to pay, such as budget and premium ranges. The offering must deliver value the group recognises; a higher price label alone does not create superior quality or willing buyers. Low-price demand can require careful cost control, while a premium offer needs credible benefits.

### Putting the idea to work

Kira Furniture compares a simple low-priced desk with a more expensive customised range. The budget group may value affordability, but the desk must still meet its basic purpose. Premium customers need a reason to accept the extra price.

### Age segmentation

Age segmentation groups customers by age where life stage or practical needs affect demand. Children’s and adult sizes can differ, but age does not establish every preference or income level. Use a meaningful product link and evidence instead of stereotypes. A buyer may also purchase on behalf of another age group.

### Putting the idea to work

Ema Cycles makes smaller cycles for younger riders and larger sizes for adults. Size and safe fit provide a relevant age-related distinction, while price and style still need evidence from riders and purchasing adults.

### Gender segmentation

Gender segmentation groups demand by gender where evidence shows a relevant difference in needs or preferences. It is one possible basis, not an assumption that every product must have separate male and female versions. Many needs are shared and individuals vary within categories. Choose fit, intended use or actual preferences when those explain demand better.

### Putting the idea to work

Nela Tailoring tests demand for different clothing cuts, asking customers about fit rather than assuming all preferences from gender. Gender-related ranges may help where fit evidence supports them, but actual measurements and preferences remain important. For some clothing, intended use, such as workwear, may explain demand better than gender.

## Market and product orientation

### Market and product orientation

Market orientation means research identifies customer wants before the product is developed and produced. Product orientation starts from the product or the enterprise’s technical strengths and then seeks buyers for that offering. The distinction is what guides the offering first, not whether either business advertises. Market focus can reduce the risk of an unwanted offer but costs time and research can be imperfect. Product focus can support technical innovation and expertise, yet may miss what buyers can use or afford. A business can combine technical capability with customer evidence rather than ignoring one.

### Putting the idea to work

Hela Bottles surveys users about size before designing a bottle. Another producer builds its preferred technical design first and then looks for buyers. Hela’s sequence is market oriented; the other is product led. Advertising after either decision does not change which information guided the design first.

### Market orientation

Market orientation is an approach in which market research finds consumer wants before the product is developed and produced. Customer evidence guides the offer, although the business must also assess cost and capability. Research may be inaccurate or needs may change, so continue to test the response.

### Putting the idea to work

Ota Lunches asks workers about meal size and collection time before designing its menu. The customer findings lead the offering, so the sequence is market oriented.

### Product orientation

Product orientation starts from the product, technical capability or producer’s preferred design and seeks a market for it. It can support innovation and technical quality but risks producing something buyers do not want or cannot afford. It is not simply making a poor product or doing no promotion.

### Putting the idea to work

Hila Gear develops a technically preferred design, then looks for customers willing to buy it. The product leads the sequence. Advertising and later sales do not make the original design decision customer-research led.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain the orientation: Ula Bags asks customers about carrying problems before developing a range; Eha Cases designs its preferred case first and then seeks buyers.

Ula is market oriented because identified wants guide development. Eha is product oriented because its design focus precedes the search for buyers. Either may advertise later; the distinguishing fact is what guided the offering first.
