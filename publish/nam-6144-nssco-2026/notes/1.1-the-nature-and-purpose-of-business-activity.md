## The purpose of business activity

### The purpose of business activity

Business activity organises resources to provide goods or services that satisfy wants and needs. A need is something essential for living, while a want is a particular way someone would like a need or desire satisfied. Producing something is not sufficient for commercial success: the offer must reach customers at a price and quality they accept. A private enterprise normally seeks enough revenue to cover costs and provide a return; other organisations may prioritise a service or social objective.

### Putting the idea to work

Omuti Foods is a small bakery supplying bread to families in a Namibian town. The bakery combines ingredients, equipment and people to make bread available locally. Customers obtain food without baking it themselves, while sales give the bakery revenue with which to pay its costs. Revenue is not the same as profit: costs must be deducted. A shortage of customers or rising costs can prevent an otherwise useful business from surviving.

## Responding to community needs

### Responding to community needs

A community includes customers, workers, nearby residents and other organisations. Businesses can respond by supplying needed goods and services, creating work, purchasing from local suppliers and supporting useful local projects. A response is stronger when the business first identifies a specific need rather than assuming that everyone wants the same thing. Community benefit and business benefit may coincide, but a useful service can still be costly to provide.

### Putting the idea to work

Families near Omuti Foods need bread before the early bus leaves, and the bakery has an unused early shift. Opening earlier could make breakfast purchases more convenient and increase sales if enough families use the service. Hiring local workers can bring household income into the area. The bakery must compare the extra demand with wages, electricity and transport costs: a community response is more sustainable when it can be maintained without exhausting the business’s resources.

## Types of business activity

### Types of business activity

The five basic activity types are primary, manufacturing, wholesale, retail and service. They describe what a business does, rather than its legal ownership. Primary activity extracts or obtains natural resources. Manufacturing changes materials into products. Wholesaling buys goods in bulk and supplies other businesses; retailing supplies final consumers. Service activity provides an activity, skill or assistance rather than principally transferring ownership of a physical product.

### Putting the idea to work

A grower supplies grain, a mill processes it, and Omuti Foods makes bread for households. The grower’s activity is primary. Milling grain and making bread involve manufacturing. A distributor buying large consignments for shops is a wholesaler; a shop selling loaves to households is a retailer. A vehicle-repair workshop supporting deliveries supplies a service. One enterprise can carry out more than one activity, so classification should identify the particular operation being described.

### A primary business

A primary business obtains natural resources, such as agricultural produce or fish. Its activity supplies inputs that other businesses may process.

### Putting the idea to work

A grower harvests grain near a Namibian town. Growing the grain is primary activity; milling it later would be a separate manufacturing stage.

### A manufacturing business

A manufacturing business transforms materials into products. It may produce finished goods or inputs that another manufacturer will process further.

### Putting the idea to work

A workshop turns timber into school desks. The transformation of timber into desks is manufacturing, irrespective of who owns the workshop.

### A wholesaler

A wholesaler buys goods in relatively large quantities and resells to other businesses, often in smaller quantities. The buyer’s business use distinguishes this role from retailing.

### Putting the idea to work

A distributor buys cartons of soap and supplies independent shops. Its customers are shops that will resell the soap, so the distributor is operating as a wholesaler.

### A retailer

A retailer sells goods to final consumers for their own use. It may provide convenience through location, smaller quantities and a range of products.

### Putting the idea to work

A shop sells one packet of flour to a household. The household is the final consumer, so this sale is retailing rather than wholesaling.

### A service business

A service business provides an activity, skill or assistance to customers or other businesses. Its principal offer may be transport, repair, advice or personal care.

### Putting the idea to work

A mechanic repairs a bakery’s delivery vehicle. The repair is a service that helps the bakery operate; it is not the manufacture of a new vehicle.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why calling a furniture maker a partnership does not identify its activity type.

Partnership describes an ownership arrangement, while manufacturing describes transforming materials into furniture. Two partners can own a manufacturer, retailer or service firm. In this case, what happens to the timber identifies manufacturing; the number of owners does not. The categories can both be stated without treating them as alternatives.

## Distinguishing activity types

### Distinguishing activity types

To distinguish activities, follow what happens to the resource or product and who receives the output. Extraction differs from transformation; selling to another business differs from selling to the final consumer. A service can support a physical product without itself being manufacturing. These distinctions prevent classifying an enterprise solely from its size, its name or the fact that it earns money.

### Putting the idea to work

A fishing operation lands fish; a processor freezes packaged portions; a shop sells them to families. The fishing operation obtains a natural resource, while the processor transforms or prepares it for sale. The shop’s transaction with a family is retailing. A firm buying cartons from the processor and reselling them to shops performs wholesaling. The ownership form could be the same at several stages without changing the activity performed.

## Government in the economy

### Government in the economy

Government can provide services and infrastructure, regulate business activity, raise revenue and influence economic conditions. Public services may support business by improving skills, transport or health. Regulation can address harms that private decisions impose on others. Fiscal policy concerns government spending and taxation; monetary policy concerns monetary and credit conditions, including interest rates. The tools differ, and their effects depend on the situation rather than being guaranteed.

### Putting the idea to work

In a policy example, a road improvement shortens Omuti Foods’ delivery route while borrowing becomes more expensive. The road can reduce delivery delays and transport expense, helping the bakery reach customers. A higher borrowing cost can make a new oven less affordable, so expansion may be postponed. Government expenditure on the road is a fiscal action; the change in credit conditions belongs to monetary policy. Calling both tools taxation would hide the different mechanisms.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain the difference between a government road-building programme and a change in borrowing interest rates affecting a bakery.

Road-building expenditure is a fiscal tool, while interest-rate or credit conditions belong to monetary policy. Better roads may improve deliveries; higher borrowing costs may discourage an oven purchase. The different tools work through different mechanisms, and neither effect should be treated as guaranteed regardless of the bakery’s circumstances.

## Market, planned and mixed economies

### Market, planned and mixed economies

Economic systems concern how resources are allocated and how production decisions are made. In a market model, private decisions and prices have a major coordinating role. In a planned model, central public decisions direct a substantial part of production and allocation. A mixed economy combines private enterprise with public ownership, provision and regulation. These are models: actual economies differ in the extent and form of intervention. Market economy: advantages are wide consumer choice and a profit motive that encourages efficiency and new products; disadvantages are that people with low incomes may not afford essentials, some services such as street lighting may not be provided, and firms may pollute or gain monopoly power. Planned economy: advantages are that the government can make sure basic needs are met, keep unemployment low and make incomes more equal; disadvantages are little consumer choice, weak incentives to work hard or innovate, and shortages or surpluses when planners misjudge needs. Mixed economy: advantages are that private firms provide choice and efficiency while the government provides essential services and controls harmful activity; disadvantages are that the taxes needed for public services can discourage enterprise and state-run activities may be inefficient.

### Putting the idea to work

A town has privately owned bakeries and publicly provided roads and schools. The town illustrates a mixed arrangement: private producers decide what bread to offer, while public provision supports the community. Market competition may encourage responsiveness and cost control, but people with little purchasing power may not obtain all the services they need. Planning can pursue collective priorities, but planners may lack detailed local information. A comparison should connect a feature to its consequence rather than label one system good in every situation.

### A market economy

In a market economy, resources are mainly privately owned and are allocated through prices (supply and demand), with little government involvement. Producers respond to customers’ willingness and ability to pay.

### Putting the idea to work

Two bakers compete for customers in the same town. Competition may encourage a better offer, but people unable to pay may still lack access to what they need.

### A mixed economy

A mixed economy combines private enterprise with public ownership, services and regulation. The balance and form of involvement can differ between places and activities.

### Putting the idea to work

A private bakery uses publicly provided roads. The combination illustrates private production supported by public provision; mixed does not mean exactly equal ownership.

### A planned economy

In a planned economy, the government owns most resources and decides centrally what is produced, how it is produced and who receives it; prices play only a small part. Collective priorities guide what is produced and how resources are used.

### Putting the idea to work

A public plan assigns output priorities to food producers. The plan can direct resources towards a chosen need, but its usefulness depends on information about local conditions and capacity.

## Specialisation and division of labour

### Specialisation and division of labour

Specialisation means concentrating on a particular activity, product or skill. Division of labour is a form of specialisation in which production is split into tasks and workers concentrate on particular tasks. The ideas are related but operate at different scales: a business may specialise in bread, and its workers may divide mixing, shaping and baking between them.

### Putting the idea to work

Omuti Foods produces bread, with different workers mixing dough, shaping loaves and operating an oven. The firm specialises in a narrow product range. Its workers’ separate tasks illustrate division of labour. Repetition can build skill and reduce time lost switching tasks, but the stages depend on one another. If the oven is unavailable, faster shaping alone does not produce finished loaves. Specialisation therefore needs coordination as well as individual skill.

### Specialisation

Specialisation is concentration on a particular activity, product or skill. Concentration allows experience and resources to be developed around that activity.

### Putting the idea to work

A repair workshop concentrates on refrigeration. It may develop useful expertise, but will need to trade for other goods and services.

### Division of labour

Division of labour splits production into separate tasks, with workers concentrating on particular tasks. Coordination links those tasks into a finished output.

### Putting the idea to work

One bakery worker mixes dough while another shapes loaves. The tasks are divided, but a delay in mixing can leave the next worker waiting.

### Worked practice: Specialisation, trade and stakeholder effects

Etango Bakes is a small bakery serving cafés. Most orders are standard rolls. It buys flour and packaging from other businesses and is considering dividing its work into narrower tasks. For part (b), the stated constraint changes: most customers now order small quantities of individually shaped celebration breads rather than standard rolls.

(a) Evaluate the suitability of division of labour for Etango Bakes under its original order pattern. [12]
(b) Evaluate whether your decision should change under the new celebration-bread order pattern. [12]

(a) Standard rolls allow mixing, shaping and baking to be organised as repeated stages. This may build skill and save switching time, provided the stages match demand and one does not constrain the rest. The bakery still needs trade for flour and packaging and outlets for its rolls. A narrow system creates dependence and can be repetitive, so it should be combined with feasible cover rather than an assumption that every worker must remain at one task. A coordinated division of labour is suitable for the original stable pattern. (b) Individually shaped orders create greater variety and smaller runs. Broader skills now help workers respond to different specifications, so the earlier degree of specialisation may be too rigid. Some repeated preparation can remain specialised, but flexible roles around shaping and finishing become more useful. The trade links remain necessary; what changes is the internal balance between repetition and flexibility. This conditional change is stronger than claiming that division of labour is either suitable or unsuitable for every bakery.

### Worked practice: Specialisation, trade and stakeholder effects

Nawa Rolls is a bakery in Otjiwarongo. It sells standard rolls to shops, buys flour from a mill and uses separate workers for mixing, shaping and baking. Orders are steady, but an absent oven operator can delay output.

(a)(i) Define specialisation. [2]
(a)(ii) State two transactions that link this specialised bakery to other businesses. [2]
(b) State two tasks that could be allocated to different bakery workers. [2]
(c) Explain one benefit and one limitation of division of labour for Nawa Rolls. [6]
(d) Evaluate whether Nawa Rolls should retain narrow specialised tasks. [8]

(a)(i) Concentrating on a particular product, activity or skill. Here the firm concentrates on baking. (a)(ii) Buying flour from the mill and selling rolls to shops are two trade transactions. (b) Mixing dough and operating the oven are distinct tasks. (c) Repetition can improve skill in shaping standard rolls, reducing avoidable mistakes if workers are trained. However, dependence on the oven operator can leave other stages waiting when that person is absent. (d) Retaining some specialised tasks suits steady standard orders, where repeated work can be organised efficiently. The absence problem means the bakery should also cross-train a worker to cover the oven. If training and cover are feasible, a coordinated division of labour is preferable to making each worker complete every stage; if reliable cover is impossible, broader roles may protect continuity. Specialisation still requires trade for flour and outlets for finished rolls.

### Worked practice: Specialisation, trade and stakeholder effects

Nawa Rolls is a bakery in Otjiwarongo. It sells standard rolls to shops, buys flour from a mill and uses separate workers for mixing, shaping and baking. Orders are steady, but an absent oven operator can delay output.

(a) Explain how specialisation creates both a need for trade and dependence on other businesses at Nawa Rolls. [8]
(b) Evaluate whether the benefits of division of labour outweigh its limitations for the bakery. [12]

(a) Concentrating on rolls means the bakery buys flour rather than growing and milling grain itself. A dependable mill allows attention to be focused on baking, but interrupted supply can stop output. Selling to shops gives access to customers without the bakery performing every retail transaction; dependence on those shops’ orders means a lost outlet can reduce demand. Division of labour also links the internal stages, so coordination is needed as well as external trade. (b) Repeated standard orders favour specialised tasks: workers can develop skill and arrange work efficiently. Against that, the absent oven operator exposes a vulnerable stage and repeated work may reduce interest. Trade allows access to inputs and markets but does not remove those internal risks. Retaining the system with feasible cross-training and coordinated scheduling is a reasoned choice because it keeps the repetition benefit while addressing the specific disruption. If orders become varied or cover cannot be organised, flexibility becomes more valuable and the judgement should change.

## Levels of specialisation

### Levels of specialisation

Specialisation can be considered between countries, between regions, between industries and between individual businesses. At each level a unit concentrates on activities suited to its resources or capabilities. A level describes the scale of concentration; it is not a rank in a management hierarchy. Different levels can exist at the same time.

### Putting the idea to work

A coastal region concentrates on fishing, while a particular business concentrates on repairing fishing equipment. The region’s concentration is regional specialisation. The repair firm’s focus is specialisation between businesses. The examples do not identify a managing director or supervisor: those are management levels, not levels of specialisation. Concentration creates reasons to exchange with other regions or businesses for goods and services not produced locally.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why a coastal region concentrating on fishing illustrates a different classification from a supervisor managing a team.

The region illustrates a level of specialisation: concentration of activity across an area. A supervisor’s position belongs to management hierarchy. The test is whether the classification describes a focus of economic activity or a rank of authority; neither can be substituted for the other.

## Benefits and limits of specialisation

### Benefits and limits of specialisation

Specialisation can improve skill through repetition, reduce time spent changing tasks and allow equipment to be used intensively. These gains may raise output or reduce unit costs if the stages are coordinated and demand supports the output. Limitations include repetitive work, dependence on other stages and a loss of flexibility. A specialised skill or product may become less useful when demand changes. Skills for cover can be developed through on-the-job or off-the-job training; the choice of training method is explained in Topic 3.5, while the point here is continuity between specialised tasks.

### Putting the idea to work

Omuti Foods has steady orders for standard loaves but is considering accepting many individually decorated celebration breads. A task-based line suits repeated standard loaves because workers can practise a narrow activity and organise batches efficiently. It may be less suitable when orders differ substantially or one absent worker stops a stage. A sensible judgement is to specialise the repeated standard work while retaining flexible skills for varied orders. If most demand becomes individual orders, that balance should change.

## Specialisation and trade

### Specialisation and trade

Trade is the buying and selling or exchange of goods and services. Specialisation and trade support each other: concentrating on some activities leaves other needs to be met through exchange, while access to a wider market can make specialisation worthwhile. Selling output can provide the income needed to buy inputs or other products. Trade still depends on reliable supply, demand and acceptable terms.

### Putting the idea to work

Omuti Foods specialises in bread and purchases flour and repair services from other local suppliers. The bakery does not need to grow grain and repair every machine itself. It can sell bread and use revenue to buy those inputs and services. This allows attention to be focused on baking, but creates dependence on suppliers. If flour deliveries become unreliable, the benefits of concentration may be reduced. Trade is the exchange linking specialised activities; it is not another name for the specialisation itself.

### Worked practice: Specialisation, trade and stakeholder effects

Etango Bakes is a small bakery serving cafés. Most orders are standard rolls. It buys flour and packaging from other businesses and is considering dividing its work into narrower tasks.

Evaluate whether Etango Bakes should introduce narrower specialised tasks. Present a decision plan using specialisation, trade and the dependence between production stages. [12]

Knowledge: specialisation concentrates activity; division of labour divides production tasks; trade exchanges output and inputs. Application: standard café orders give repeated tasks, while flour and packaging come from other firms. Analysis: repetition may improve speed and skill, but late inputs or a stalled stage can interrupt output. Alternative: broader worker roles offer flexibility but involve more switching and a wider range of skills. Judgement: introduce a limited division of labour where the repeated work is sufficient, with planned cover and reliable suppliers. A narrow task should not be adopted merely because it looks efficient if it creates an uncovered bottleneck.

## Groups involved in business

### Groups involved in business

Stakeholders are groups or individuals with an interest in a business’s activities or outcomes. They include consumers, workers, managers, owners, finance providers and the wider community. A person can belong to more than one group: an employee may also buy the product or own part of the enterprise. Their interests should be identified from the business relationship, not from an unrelated category.

### Putting the idea to work

Omuti Foods employs workers, sells to households and has borrowed money for equipment. Workers have an employment interest, households have a customer interest and the lender has a repayment interest. A customer is a stakeholder even without working inside the bakery. A production sector such as manufacturing is an activity classification, not a stakeholder group. Informal friendships among employees are workplace groups, which is another classification again.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why workers and customers of a bread maker are stakeholder groups, while manufacturing is not a stakeholder group.

Workers have an employment interest and customers have a purchasing interest. Manufacturing describes what the business does, not a group with an interest in its outcomes. Informal friendship groups inside the bakery are another classification and do not replace identifying the relevant stakeholders.

## Stakeholder objectives

### Stakeholder objectives

An objective is a result a person or group wants to achieve. Consumers may seek value and dependable quality; workers may seek pay, safety and security; managers may seek effective performance and career progress. Owners may seek profit and long-term survival. Finance providers may seek repayment or a return consistent with risk. The community may seek jobs and useful services without excessive disturbance. These objectives can overlap but do not necessarily have equal priority.

### Putting the idea to work

The owners of Omuti Foods are considering a later opening time, higher wages and a price increase. Higher pay may support workers but increase costs. A higher price could help cover those costs, yet reduce value for some customers and weaken demand. The lender is interested in whether the bakery can continue meeting repayments. Residents may care about delivery noise as well as employment. A decision should identify who gains or loses, rather than assume the owners’ objective represents every stakeholder.

### Consumer objectives

Consumers may seek value for money and reliable product quality. Convenient access and a safe, dependable product can also matter.

### Putting the idea to work

A household buys bread before school. A low price has limited value to that household if the shop is closed when bread is needed.

### Worker objectives

Workers may seek fair pay and safe working conditions. Security, development and satisfaction can also influence their priorities.

### Putting the idea to work

A bakery worker is learning to operate new equipment. Training and a safe procedure can matter alongside the wage because the worker needs to perform the task confidently.

### Manager objectives

Managers may seek to achieve business targets and use resources effectively. They may also value recognition, responsibility and career development.

### Putting the idea to work

A manager is responsible for a Friday delivery. The manager needs enough resources and cooperation to meet that target, rather than merely being given a title.

### Owner objectives

Owners may seek a return on their investment and the continued survival of the enterprise. Some also prioritise growth, independence or a social purpose.

### Putting the idea to work

A bakery owner chooses between withdrawing cash and replacing an unreliable oven. Taking more cash now may conflict with maintaining the equipment needed for future sales.

### Finance-provider objectives

A lender may seek repayment and interest consistent with the risk. An investor may seek returns and information about how the business performs.

### Putting the idea to work

A lender considers financing a bakery oven. The lender’s interest is not the same as a customer’s: expected repayment and risk matter, and approval is not guaranteed.

### Community objectives

The wider community may seek employment and useful local services. It may also want less pollution, congestion and disturbance.

### Putting the idea to work

A bakery creates local jobs but receives noisy deliveries early in the morning. Residents can welcome employment while asking for a less disruptive delivery arrangement.

## Conflicting objectives

### Conflicting objectives

Objectives conflict when pursuing one makes another harder to achieve. Conflict can occur between groups or within one group’s priorities. Raising wages can reduce short-term profit if other conditions are unchanged; reducing prices may help customers but leave less money to cover costs. A conflict is not proof that cooperation is impossible: changes in productivity or a negotiated compromise may help several groups.

### Putting the idea to work

Workers at Omuti Foods request higher pay while customers resist a price rise and the owners need to maintain cash for repairs. Paying more without extra revenue increases costs and can reduce the funds available for repairs. Passing the full increase to customers may reduce sales. A phased wage change combined with feasible waste reduction could ease the conflict, but the savings must be achievable. The explanation must connect each objective to a constraint rather than merely list that groups want different things.

## Changing priorities

### Changing priorities

Business priorities can change with survival pressures, resources, demand and stakeholder concerns. A new enterprise may emphasise establishing customers; a business facing a cash shortage may prioritise paying immediate bills. Once stable, it may give more attention to expansion, product improvement or staff development. Changing priorities does not necessarily mean abandoning the longer-term objective: it may change the sequence in which objectives are pursued.

### Putting the idea to work

Omuti Foods planned a larger shop, but a delivery vehicle now needs urgent repair. Reliable deliveries may become the immediate priority because existing sales depend on them. Delaying expansion can preserve cash for repairs and protect current customers. If the vehicle is repaired and cash improves, expansion can be reconsidered. A stronger explanation identifies the changed circumstance and why it alters the relative urgency of the objectives.
