## Needs for finance and matching their duration

### Needs for finance and matching their duration

A need for finance states why money is required; a source states where it comes from. Businesses need funds to start operations, buy or replace equipment, expand and pay operating expenses before customer receipts arrive. A new machine is an investment need, while wages and materials can create a working-capital need. Long-lived assets are normally better matched to longer-term finance so that repayment does not fall due before the asset generates sufficient receipts. A temporary cash gap may suit an agreed overdraft or supplier credit if its duration and cost fit. Short-term finance is not automatically cheaper or available. Long-term borrowing commits the firm to repayments and interest; equity avoids scheduled loan repayments but shares ownership and potential returns. Match amount, purpose, duration, affordability and risk. A profitable business can still need finance because sales revenue may not yet have been collected. Conversely, borrowing provides cash but is not profit from trading.

### Putting the idea to work

Nera Bricks needs a kiln expected to last several years and materials to fill a confirmed order whose customer pays after delivery. The kiln is a long-term asset need; materials create a shorter operating gap. A medium- or long-term loan may match the kiln, while supplier credit could help the materials timing if agreed terms fit the expected receipts. The word loan names a source, not the reason money is needed.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why “bank loan” does not identify Nalu Bakery’s need for finance. Nalu needs money to replace its broken oven and is considering borrowing.

Replacing the oven is the need: it states the intended use of the money. A bank loan is a possible source: it states where the money would come from and how it would be financed. The same oven need could be met from retained cash, if available, so the purpose and source cannot be substituted for one another.

## Sources in private and public enterprises

### Sources in private and public enterprises

Internal finance arises within an existing business, especially retained profit available for use and proceeds from selling surplus assets. External finance comes from outside it: owner contributions, new share capital where the form permits, loans, overdrafts, supplier credit, leasing or grants subject to eligibility and conditions. For a sole trader or partnership, the owners’ savings are usually classed as internal finance because the owners and the business are not legally separate; for a limited company, new share capital from shareholders is external finance. Sources are also grouped by how long the money is needed. Short-term sources, repaid within about a year, include a bank overdraft and trade credit from suppliers. Medium-term sources, for perhaps one to five years, include bank loans, hire purchase and leasing, which suit buying a machine or vehicle. Long-term sources include long-term loans and mortgages, new share capital (for companies) and retained profit, which suit buildings or major expansion. Match the term of the finance to the life of what it buys. A loan gives funds now with repayment and interest obligations; an overdraft allows an agreed temporary negative bank balance; trade credit postpones payment to suppliers. Leasing provides use of an asset in return for payments rather than ownership through an outright purchase. A grant may support a specified purpose without ordinary loan repayment, but conditions and availability matter. Private businesses can use sources appropriate to their form. Public enterprises can retain earnings, borrow where permitted or receive state capital and allocations. The state itself obtains revenue through taxation and other public receipts and may borrow to fund expenditure. Public/private describes ownership sector; internal/external describes the origin of funds relative to the particular enterprise.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume state-owned Temo Water retains a cash surplus and also receives an additional state capital allocation. A privately owned workshop obtains a bank loan. Temo’s retained funds are internal to its enterprise, while the new state contribution comes from outside it. The workshop’s loan is external private-enterprise finance. A public organisation can therefore have both internal and external sources; private does not mean internal.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain the two classifications. Assume state-owned Oru Water finances repairs partly from retained earnings and partly from a new bank loan.

Oru is public sector because the state owns it. Retained earnings are an internal source relative to Oru, whereas the new bank loan is external to it. Both sources finance a public enterprise. Internal/external identifies where money originates relative to that enterprise; public/private identifies ownership. Neither classification replaces the other.

## How to seek finance

### How to seek finance

Begin by identifying the amount needed, its purpose, when it is required and when the business can repay or provide a return. Prepare a business plan and realistic cash-flow forecast, supported by accounts or sales evidence where available. Approach a suitable provider: a lender for a loan, shareholders for equity, a supplier for trade credit, an asset provider for leasing or an institution offering eligible support. Present the proposal and information that the provider needs rather than simply requesting money. A lender may request security, repayment evidence and information about existing borrowing. An equity investor needs to understand ownership rights, risks and expected returns. Compare the total cost, duration, conditions and consequences for control before accepting an offer. Obtain agreed terms and monitor the commitments after funds are supplied. The process does not guarantee acceptance; an attractive idea can still have weak repayment prospects or fail a provider’s eligibility criteria.

### Putting the idea to work

Sola Repairs seeks finance for a diagnostic machine. It has records of paid repair work but has not estimated the timing of future receipts. The owner should prepare the machine cost, expected additional receipts and a cash forecast before approaching a bank. The bank can then assess whether scheduled repayments fit likely cash inflows. Sola should compare the offered loan with leasing rather than choosing whichever quote mentions the smallest monthly payment without checking duration and total cost.

## Using funds generated within the enterprise

### Using funds generated within the enterprise

Retained profit is profit kept in the enterprise rather than distributed to owners. If corresponding cash is available, it can finance equipment, stock or other needs without new outside ownership or loan interest. Retained profit is not automatically a cash balance: money can be tied up in debtors or assets. Selling a surplus asset releases funds, but selling essential equipment can damage capacity. Reducing unnecessary inventory and collecting receivables sooner can release cash already tied up in operations; these actions improve liquidity rather than create fresh sales profit by themselves. Internal sources preserve control and avoid a new finance application, but amounts can be limited and using reserves removes protection against later difficulties. Retained cash has an opportunity cost because it cannot simultaneously finance another project or provide a reserve. Choose the use that best matches the firm’s needs without weakening necessary operations.

### Putting the idea to work

Omu Craft has an unused delivery trailer and retained cash, but also needs a reserve for seasonal rent. Selling the unused trailer can release cash without reducing stated current capacity. Using every retained dollar for new tools would leave no rent reserve. Use genuinely spare funds and protect operating needs instead of assuming that internal finance is unlimited or costless.

### Worked practice: Finance needs, sources and provider criteria

Tawa Repairs needs N$60,000 for a machine. It has N$65,000 retained cash after its essential operating reserve and forecasts stable receipts. It compares using that spare cash with a bank loan carrying interest. No alternative use is identified for these spare funds.

Evaluate using Tawa’s spare retained cash instead of borrowing for its machine. Give a concise plan. [12]

Need: long-lived equipment costing N$60,000. Internal option: N$65,000 is genuinely spare after protecting the operating reserve, so cash can meet the stated need without interest or new outside rights. Remaining spare cash would be N$5,000. Borrowing option: preserves additional liquidity and could match equipment benefits, but brings interest and repayment commitments. Provider view: assess repayment cash and risk. Judgement: prefer retained cash on the original facts because funds are available, the essential reserve is already protected and no competing project is identified. Reassess if the supposedly spare funds become necessary elsewhere or cash forecasts deteriorate.

## What providers assess

### What providers assess

A finance provider examines the proposal from its own risk and return perspective. Banks and other lenders assess ability to repay principal and interest, using expected cash flows, trading history, existing debt, management and any security offered. Security may reduce loss if repayment fails but does not prove the business can make instalments. Shareholders consider potential dividends and growth in the value of their investment, risks, management quality and the rights or influence offered. Institutional lenders or development bodies can also apply mandate and eligibility conditions, such as the project purpose, beneficiaries or environmental requirements stated in a programme. A borrower’s wish for low interest does not explain why a provider would accept the risk. Distinguish the requested amount from evidence that it is sustainable. Strong evidence can improve the likelihood of funding, but the final decision also depends on the provider’s criteria and available funds.

### Putting the idea to work

Eha Panels asks a bank for a machine loan. Its customer orders look promising, but existing repayments already absorb much of its cash. The bank should examine the timing and reliability of receipts against both existing and proposed commitments. Promising sales alone may not leave enough cash for the new instalments. Eha’s desire for a low rate is a borrower preference, not evidence of repayment capacity.

### Banks as providers

Banks supplying loans examine repayment capacity, expected cash flows, existing debt, the purpose and term of the loan, management reliability and security where relevant. Interest compensates the bank for supplying funds and bearing risk, but a higher offered rate does not automatically make a weak borrower acceptable. Collateral is a possible fallback if the borrower defaults, not a substitute for reliable operating cash. If the borrower is a limited company or close corporation, limited liability restricts the owners’ personal exposure, which can leave the lender exposed, so a bank may ask for personal guarantees or other security; a sole trader’s unlimited liability instead gives the lender a claim on the owner’s personal assets. Assess the enterprise and any expressly agreed additional security.

### Putting the idea to work

Mavo Joinery has assets available as security but forecasts a cash shortage during the proposed repayment months. The security could reduce loss in default, yet the forecast raises doubt about on-time repayment. The bank may require a revised schedule, more evidence or decline the proposal; possessing an asset alone does not establish approval.

### Shareholders as providers

Shareholders supply equity in exchange for an ownership interest. They assess potential dividends, longer-term growth in value, the chance of losing their investment, management quality and the voting or other rights offered. Equity does not promise the scheduled interest and capital repayments of a loan. Expected profit may support future dividends, but dividends depend on available resources and decisions, not a guaranteed fixed payment. An investor may reject an attractive sales story if costs, risk or ownership terms are unclear.

### Putting the idea to work

Ota Foods offers an investor a minority share interest to fund equipment. Sales are forecast to rise, but proposed distributions and voting rights have not been explained. The investor needs expected profit and risk information as well as clear ownership rights. Increased revenue does not establish the dividend return, because costs and retention decisions intervene.

### Institutions as providers

Institutions providing finance assess whether a proposal fits the type of funding and their stated mandate. A development institution may examine eligibility, project viability, likely benefits and the applicant’s contribution; an institutional lender also examines repayment and risk. Grants can require spending on a specified activity and evidence of compliance. Conditions differ, so use the programme facts provided instead of assuming every institution supplies free or interest-free funds. A socially useful proposal can still be ineligible or financially unviable.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume an enterprise-support institution offers equipment funding to eligible new manufacturers, with a required applicant contribution. A retailer applies without that contribution. The retailer’s application does not fit the stated activity and contribution conditions. Its need for equipment is not enough to establish eligibility. The institution must assess its own rules as well as the applicant’s enthusiasm.

Nali Milling needs N$120,000 for equipment lasting several years and N$15,000 for materials before customers pay. It has N$20,000 genuinely spare retained cash after protecting its operating reserve. A bank is considering a term loan for equipment and a supplier may allow short credit for materials. Repayment capacity and agreed terms must still be assessed.

a The equipment is a long-term investment need and the materials are a short operating-cash need before customers pay. A bank offering a term loan needs evidence that instalments and interest fit the cash flows, including existing commitments. The supplier needs confidence that payment will arrive on its credit deadline; customer receipts delayed beyond that date would create risk. The owner’s desire for a cheap source is different from either provider’s assessment. Internal funds avoid new loan interest and preserve ownership, but draining its essential reserve would create operating risk. It adds repayment obligations and depends on agreement and cash affordability. Choose the combination if the bank and supplier terms fit credible receipts and the operating reserve remains protected. If finance is not affordable, postpone or reduce the investment rather than treating borrowing as guaranteed.

### Worked practice: Finance needs, sources and provider criteria

Tawa Repairs needs N$60,000 for a machine. It has N$65,000 retained cash after its essential operating reserve and forecasts stable receipts. It compares using that spare cash with a bank loan carrying interest. No alternative use is identified for these spare funds. For part (b) only, most retained cash has now been committed to an essential roof repair, leaving N$10,000 spare after the operating reserve. The N$60,000 machine need and expected receipts are unchanged.

It avoids interest and preserves control, though it reduces funds available for unexpected events. A loan could preserve spare liquidity but adds commitments; the bank would assess expected cash, existing debts and risk. Prefer internal cash given stable receipts and no stated competing use, while reviewing the forecast before spending. b N$10,000 spare cannot fund a N$60,000 machine. The change removes the sufficiency condition behind the original recommendation. A suitable term loan or mixed funding becomes more relevant if machine benefits support repayment and the bank accepts the evidence. Avoid using cash needed for the essential roof or operating reserve. Change towards external or mixed finance subject to affordable terms; if those terms are unavailable, postpone the machine. Need alone does not establish repayment capacity.
