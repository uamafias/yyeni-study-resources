## Production and productivity

### Production and productivity

Production is the activity of creating goods or services to meet needs. Productivity relates output to an input, such as acceptable units per labour hour. More total output is not necessarily higher productivity if inputs rise faster. State the input and time period and compare like quality. A larger staff producing more items can still produce fewer items per worker. Product is the offering; production is making it; productivity measures how efficiently the stated input generates output.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Tula Packs makes 400 packs with ten workers in one week and later 480 with twelve workers working equal hours. Output rises, but output per worker remains forty packs in each period. That is increased production without improved labour productivity under the stated comparable hours.

### Production

Production is creating goods or services from inputs to satisfy needs. It can involve manufacturing, farming or service activity and is not restricted to a factory making objects. A production total records output over a period, while productivity relates output to input. The distinction matters when judging whether extra resources are used more efficiently.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Eya Repairs completes more repairs after hiring another technician. The service output has increased. Productivity requires comparing repairs with technician time, not merely counting the extra repairs.

### Productivity

Productivity is output per unit of input, such as acceptable products per labour hour. Higher productivity means more output from a given input or the same output with less input, using comparable quality and periods. It differs from production, the activity or total output. A busier workplace can have unchanged or lower productivity if resources rise as quickly as output.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Kena Packs makes 200 packs in 40 labour hours. Labour productivity is five packs per labour hour. More hours alone would not establish an improvement in this rate.

## Improving productivity

### Improving productivity

Productivity can improve through relevant skills, suitable technology, better layout, maintenance, reliable inputs, motivation and effective coordination. Trace the mechanism: less time waiting for materials can increase acceptable output per hour; fewer defects can increase usable output from the same input. Training can be on-the-job or off-the-job according to need and available expertise. An improvement requires actual use and suitable conditions, not simply buying a machine or announcing a target. Extra output achieved solely by proportionately extra hours is not a productivity gain.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nera Assembly loses time walking between distant storage and workstations. A practical layout change can reduce handling time and increase acceptable units per labour hour without asking for longer shifts. Check safety and quality so the faster process does not merely create defects.

## HIV, AIDS and workplace productivity

### HIV, AIDS and workplace productivity

Illness associated with HIV and AIDS can affect attendance, working capacity, skills continuity and household care demands, potentially disrupting output and raising replacement costs. Effects vary; HIV status alone does not establish that a person cannot work. Support, confidentiality, non-discrimination and access to appropriate prevention, care and treatment services matter. Stigma can damage cooperation and discourage seeking support. Discuss the business mechanisms conditionally rather than label all affected people unproductive. This is a workplace-impact lesson, not medical advice or a reason to exclude workers. The ILO emphasises care, support and non-discrimination in its [workplace code](https://www.ilo.org/publications/ilo-code-practice-hivaids-and-world-work).

### Putting the idea to work

A fictional workshop faces periods of illness-related absence and care demands affecting some staff; no assumption is made about any individual’s diagnosis or capability. Absences can interrupt a task sequence and create a need for cover. Cross-skilling and supportive arrangements can reduce disruption while protecting dignity; blaming or excluding people based on perceived status can worsen relationships.

## Inclusive participation in production

### Inclusive participation in production

Women and people living with impairments contribute skills, knowledge, enterprise and labour to production. Capabilities vary among individuals, so allocate work using relevant abilities and role needs rather than assumptions based on gender or impairment. Inaccessible workplaces, unsuitable equipment, exclusion from learning or stereotypes can waste available talent. Accessible layouts, suitable tools, fair development opportunities and appropriate task organisation can enable contribution. Inclusion is not a claim that every person performs every task identically; it means assessing the work and removing avoidable barriers rather than excluding a group.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Eha Print has a capable applicant who uses a wheelchair, while the record desk is inaccessible. An accessible desk and route can remove the stated barrier to administrative work. The applicant’s relevant skill should be assessed instead of treating the inaccessible design as proof of inability.

## Job, batch and flow production

### Job, batch and flow production

Job production makes a single product at a time, usually to the customer’s exact specification. Batch production makes a group of similar units before changing to another group. Flow production moves standardised units through a continuous sequence of stages. Compare volume, variety and sequence: customised one-off work differs from repeated groups and from a continuously operating standard line. Job production can offer flexibility with high skill and time per unit; batches balance variety and repetition but require changeovers; flow can support high volume but needs dependable demand, coordination and equipment. A flow line does not guarantee lower unit cost if capacity is underused or coordination and breakdown costs offset savings.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Omi Furniture makes one customised cabinet; fictional Kena Bakery makes groups of different loaf types; fictional Hila Bottles operates a continuous standard bottling line. These respectively illustrate job, batch and flow. The product’s name alone does not determine the method; describe how the stated process handles units.

### Job production

Job production makes a single product at a time, usually to the customer’s exact specifications. It suits custom variety and skilled flexible work, with close attention to individual requirements. It can take longer and have a high cost per unit compared with suitable repeated work. Batch production instead groups similar units; flow runs standardised units through an ongoing sequence. A business can use different methods for different orders.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Teya Joinery makes a cabinet measured for one unusual room. Skilled individual work fits the exact dimensions, but time and price must cover that effort. A standard line would need adaptation to deliver the same variety.

### Batch production

Batch production makes a group of similar items before changing to another group. It can combine repeated work within a batch with variety between batches. Setup and cleaning between groups take time and work may wait between stages. Job production focuses on individual specifications; flow focuses on a continuous standard sequence. Suitable batch size depends on demand, equipment and the cost of holding stock.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Omi Bakery makes a batch of one loaf type, changes its setup and then makes a different loaf type. Repeated groups with a changeover identify batch production, not a continuously unchanged flow. It can supply variety but needs planned timing to avoid excessive waiting.

### Flow production

Flow production moves standardised units continuously through a sequence of operations. It can support high output and consistent processes, but usually needs substantial equipment, dependable demand and coordination between stages. A breakdown can disrupt the line, and limited variety may not suit customised orders. Lower unit cost depends on capacity use and on coordination and other diseconomies not offsetting potential savings. Job and batch production provide different flexibility and volume patterns.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Hila Bottles has continuing demand for identical filled bottles and a coordinated line. Flow may fit its standard volume. If demand falls and the line sits idle, fixed commitments and coordination costs can undermine the expected unit-cost benefit.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain the methods at three fictional workshops: one makes a unique measured gate; another makes a group of one design then changes design; a third runs identical units through a continuing line.

The unique specification is job production; repeated groups with changeovers are batch; the ongoing standard sequence is flow. The material or business size alone does not identify the method.

## Choosing a production method

### Choosing a production method

Choose by demand volume, required variety, available skills and equipment, reliability, cost and quality. Job production fits individual specifications, batch can fit recurring groups with variety and flow can fit large dependable standard demand. The cheapest-looking method is unsuitable if it cannot meet customers’ needs or its capacity sits idle. Job production is not on-the-job training: one makes an output and the other develops a worker. On-the-job and off-the-job learning are methods explained in topic 3.5.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nela Joinery receives different custom dimensions for each order and has skilled flexible workers but no large standard contract. Job production fits the variety and skill. A flow line could restrict customisation and commit funds before standard demand exists. Prefer job production here and review if large repeat orders emerge.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why fictional Nela Joinery making one custom cabinet is different from an experienced worker teaching a new worker at the bench.

Making the one-off cabinet describes job production. Teaching while working is on-the-job training, a method of skill development. Off-the-job training is the alternative learning setting. Production method and learning method answer different questions.

## Technology changes production

### Technology changes production

Technology can automate repetitive tasks, improve precision, support design and coordinate inventory or schedules. It can increase acceptable output per input or allow new products, but benefits depend on demand, skills, reliable support and integration. Initial investment, maintenance, energy, downtime and staff development create costs. Some tasks change or disappear while others require new skills. Training may be on-the-job or off-the-job; staff involvement can help implementation. Assess actual productivity and quality after adoption instead of assuming advanced equipment automatically improves performance.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Ota Cutting acquires computer-controlled equipment but staff cannot yet set it accurately. Precision and repeatability are potential benefits, while setup mistakes and downtime can offset them. Suitable instruction and maintenance must accompany the purchase.

## Scale economies and diseconomies

### Scale economies and diseconomies

Scale of operation concerns the size or capacity of business activity. Economies of scale are causes of average-cost reductions as scale increases, such as purchasing advantages, use of specialist equipment or management and spreading suitable fixed costs across more output. They require conditions: demand and capacity use must support the investment, and coordination costs or diseconomies must not offset savings. Diseconomies are causes of average-cost increases beyond a scale, for example communication difficulties, slower decisions or reduced coordination. Total cost can rise while average cost falls, so keep the measures distinct. This concerns business costs, not the economy’s system of ownership and resource allocation.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Hena Packs can negotiate a material discount for larger orders, but rapid expansion also creates stock-control errors. The discount can reduce unit cost if useful sales justify the larger purchase and storage and coordination costs do not offset it. The errors can create diseconomies, so growth is not automatic evidence of lower average cost.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why fictional Hela Packs negotiating a bulk material discount concerns economies of scale rather than a market or mixed economic system.

The discount can reduce unit cost if higher scale and useful sales justify the purchase and coordination or storage diseconomies do not offset savings. Economic systems concern how an economy allocates resources and ownership. The enterprise cost mechanism is a different concept.

## Location depends on the activity

### Location depends on the activity

Location factors include access to customers, suppliers, labour and skills, transport, premises, utilities, communications, land cost and relevant public conditions. Explain why a factor matters to the particular activity instead of listing inputs alone. Heavy materials may make transport cost decisive for a manufacturer, while a convenience retailer needs customer access. A service may need either face-to-face footfall or reliable remote communication. Cheaper rent can be outweighed by lost sales or unreliable supplies. Rank the most consequential constraints and compare realistic alternatives.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Tula Fresh Foods compares a cheap isolated site with a more expensive site near customers and reliable delivery routes. The cheaper site may raise transport time and spoilage risk and reduce customer access. Compare total practical consequences rather than rent alone.

### Manufacturing location

A manufacturer transforms inputs and often needs appropriate premises, utilities, labour skills and transport links to suppliers and markets. Bulky inputs or products can make freight important; perishability can make reliable quick access decisive. The relative importance depends on the product and process. A cheap site without sufficient power or delivery access may not support reliable production.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nela Blocks makes heavy building blocks for nearby construction sites. Road access and distance to customers affect delivered cost because the output is bulky. An attractive rent alone cannot settle the location.

### Retail location

A retailer supplies final customers and often depends on convenient access, suitable footfall, parking or delivery links, nearby competitors and premises cost. High footfall is useful only if it includes likely buyers and the extra sales can justify the rent. Online retail changes access needs but still requires fulfilment and suitable communication.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Ota Snacks compares a busy commuter route with a cheap site far from its intended buyers. Relevant passing customers can support convenience sales, while the remote rent saving may be outweighed by lower demand. Check actual target footfall rather than total pedestrians alone.

### Service location

A service business’s location needs depend on how it serves customers. A walk-in repairer may need accessible local premises, while a remote administrative service may depend more on connectivity and skilled staff. Urgency, face-to-face contact, transport and trust can be decisive. Do not apply the same ranking to every service or assume online delivery removes all location costs.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Eya Emergency Repairs must reach local customers quickly with suitable equipment. Nearby skills and short journeys support its promised response. A distant cheap office would not provide that capability by itself.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why “labour and land” alone is not enough to justify fictional Omi Repairs choosing a site near customers.

Labour and land are factor inputs. A location explanation identifies why this site provides suitable skills, premises or customer access at an acceptable cost. For urgent repairs, proximity can shorten response time; naming inputs without linking them to the site does not explain the choice.

## Locating at home or abroad

### Locating at home or abroad

An overseas location may offer access to customers, inputs, skills or lower specified operating costs. It can also create transport, communication, currency, coordination and unfamiliar-market risks. A home location can preserve known suppliers, management access and customer understanding while sometimes limiting reach. Compare verified total relevant costs and service reliability; neither a foreign nor a home label establishes the better option. Separate one-time relocation costs from ongoing costs and check whether potential incentives are actually available rather than treating them as guaranteed.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nara Goods compares remaining near its established Namibian customers with a foreign site that has cheaper stated rent but longer delivery and coordination demands. The rent saving is one benefit, not the whole decision. Lost responsiveness and extra delivery costs may outweigh it, especially for urgent customised orders.

## Choosing between locations

### Choosing between locations

A supported location choice identifies the business’s needs, compares the relevant alternatives and gives weight to the decisive constraint. Use the stated customer, input, labour, reliability and cost facts. A location can suit one enterprise and not another; low wages do not establish low unit labour cost without productivity evidence. Recognise uncertainty and the condition that would change the choice. Do not invent sales, subsidies or infrastructure just to justify a preferred place.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Oru Repairs serves urgent local customers. Its current town has suitable skills and short response times. An alternative foreign site offers lower rent but makes urgent visits impossible; no remote service substitute is available. Remain near the customers because response access is essential to this service and the rent saving does not replace it. A standard non-urgent product with dependable delivery could justify another balance.
