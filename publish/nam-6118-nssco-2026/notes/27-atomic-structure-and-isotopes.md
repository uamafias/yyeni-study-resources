## Atomic structure and isotopes — 6.1.1

### The atomic nucleus

An atomic nucleus contains protons and, for most nuclides, neutrons. Protons have positive charge and neutrons have no net electric charge.

Electrons occupy the surrounding atomic region rather than being ordinary constituent nucleons. The common hydrogen-1 nucleus is a proton without a neutron, so avoid saying every nucleus must contain both. Nearly all atomic mass is concentrated in the nucleus, while the atom’s size is much larger than the nucleus. A nuclide is a type of nucleus identified by its proton and neutron numbers, or equivalently A and Z. Relative charge/mass are proton +1/about 1, neutron 0/about 1 and electron −1/about 1/1840. A neutral atom has Z electrons.

### use the term proton number (atomic number), denoted by Z

Proton number Z counts the protons in a nucleus. It identifies the element.

Changing neutron number can produce another isotope of the same element, but changing proton number changes element identity. In a neutral atom, electron number equals Z; an ion can have a different electron number without changing its nucleus or element. Do not define Z by electrons without the neutrality condition.

### Nucleon number A

Nucleon number A counts protons plus neutrons in a nucleus. Neutron number is A − Z.

A nucleus with 15 protons and 16 neutrons has A = 31. The mass number is an integer count, not a measured atomic mass in kilograms. Electrons are not included in A. A minus Z subtracts the proton count from the total, leaving the neutron count.

### Use nuclide notation with mass number A and atomic number Z

Nuclide notation places A at the upper left and Z at the lower left of the element symbol. For phosphorus-31 the notation is ³¹₁₅P, with 15 protons and 16 neutrons.

Read the two positions independently: the upper number is the total nucleon count, while the lower is proton number. In plain text, phosphorus-31 with A = 31 and Z = 15 is an unambiguous accessible equivalent. Isotope notation does not itself specify how many electrons an ion has unless charge is also given.

### use the nuclide notation in equations to show alpha and beta decay

Nuclear equations conserve total nucleon number and total electric charge. Alpha emission reduces daughter A by four and Z by two; beta-minus emission leaves A unchanged and increases daughter Z by one.

An alpha particle is ⁴₂He. A beta-minus electron is represented ⁰₋₁e in nuclear-number accounting. For example, ²¹⁰₈₄Po → ²⁰⁶₈₂Pb + ⁴₂He, while ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e gives the school beta-number balance.

### Represent alpha decay using nuclear numbers

Alpha emission removes two protons and two neutrons from the parent nucleus. The daughter has Aparent − 4 and Zparent − 2.

Using the original practice choice ²²⁶₈₈Ra, the daughter is ²²²₈₆Rn. Check both balances: 226 equals 222 + 4 and 88 equals 86 + 2. The element symbol follows the new proton number; it is not unchanged merely because the same atom started the process.

### Represent beta-minus decay using nuclear numbers

Beta-minus decay leaves the nucleon number unchanged and increases the daughter proton number by one. The nucleus’s nucleon count is unchanged while its proton number rises by one.

A phosphorus-32 nucleus can become sulfur-32: ³²₁₅P → ³²₁₆S + ⁰₋₁e in the simplified school number display. The emitted electron is produced in the decay, not an ordinary atomic-shell electron falling out.

### Distinguishing the ideas

Nucleon number includes both protons and neutrons. Subtract proton number to obtain neutron number.

A nucleus with A = 39 and Z = 19 has 19 protons and 20 neutrons, not 39 neutrons. Treating A as neutron count adds the protons twice when reconstructing the nucleus. Reading the definitions before arithmetic keeps the particle roles clear.

## Atomic structure and isotopes — 6.1.2

### define isotopes as atoms with the same proton number but different nucleon number

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different nucleon numbers. Their neutron numbers differ.

Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have six protons, but have six and eight neutrons respectively. Neutral atoms of these isotopes have the same electron count, which supports similar chemical behaviour. Their nuclear stability can differ, so isotope does not mean automatically radioactive: some isotopes are stable.

### give and explain examples of practical applications of isotopes for example in carbon-14 dating; nuclear medicine, radiology, biochemical tracers; radiotherapy, detection of leaks in pipes; determination of thickness of material; power generation; sterilisation

Radioisotope applications use detectable emission, penetration, ionisation or energy release. The radiation type and half-life must suit the intended task.

A tracer should remain detectable for its measurement but need not persist far longer than required. A thickness monitor needs partial transmission that changes measurably with thickness. Sterilisation uses damage to microorganisms, while nuclear power commonly uses controlled fission of suitable fuel rather than merely detecting emissions. Name the actual mechanism for each application.

### Explain carbon-14 dating

Carbon-14 dating uses the decrease of radioactive carbon after an organism stops exchanging carbon with its surroundings. The remaining activity or isotope ratio can be compared with an appropriate initial model.

Living material exchanges carbon; after death that exchange largely stops and the carbon-14 decays. More elapsed half-lives leave a smaller remaining fraction. Calibration and contamination matter in real dating, so the school exponential model is an approximation rather than an exact age for any arbitrary object. It does not date an unconnected metal sample merely because all objects contain atoms. Using a given half-life of 5730 years, a corrected carbon-14 activity one quarter of its appropriate original value indicates two half-lives, or about 11 460 years, in the simple model.

### Explain nuclear-medicine diagnosis and biochemical tracing

A radioactive tracer labels a substance so its location or movement can be detected. Suitable penetrating emission can be measured by a detector outside the studied region.

In nuclear-medicine or biochemical examples, a selected labelled substance follows a process and its radiation provides information. Choice depends on chemical behaviour, emission and half-life. This describes the physical principle rather than selecting a patient’s tracer or dose. The tracer is the material introduced; the detector does not become the tracer.

### Explain radiotherapy

Radiotherapy uses controlled radiation to damage targeted cells. Absorption and ionisation can affect both intended targets and healthy tissue.

Specialist planning aims to concentrate the intended effect while limiting unwanted exposure. The physics link is radiation interaction with cells, not a rule that radiation recognises disease automatically. This syllabus example supplies no individual treatment or dose advice.

### Explain leak tracing

A detectable radioactive tracer in a fluid can help locate a pipe leak. A local change or peak in detector response can indicate where labelled fluid leaves its intended path.

Measure a suitable background and compare positions along the route. The isotope must follow the fluid and its emission must reach the detector under the stated conditions. Real use needs appropriate containment and authorised handling; the learning task interprets a supplied detector record rather than introducing material into public pipes.

### Explain thickness monitoring

A thickness monitor measures radiation transmitted through a sheet. For a suitable radiation and range, a thicker sheet gives a smaller transmitted count under fixed source and geometry.

Beta radiation is a common school example for thin-sheet monitoring because partial absorption can respond usefully to thickness. An alpha source may be stopped too readily and a very penetrating gamma beam may change too little for that particular task. Match the radiation to the actual material rather than memorising a fixed choice for every sheet.

### Explain nuclear power use

Suitable nuclear fuel can release energy by controlled fission. Heating can drive a turbine and generator to give electrical output.

The isotope’s nuclear properties permit the intended chain reaction; not every radioactive source is a practical power-reactor fuel. Connect released energy to the engineering stages and include cooling and radioactive-waste management as consequences. This application links to the earlier energy-source explanation rather than replacing the nuclear equations taught in this atomic topic.

### Explain sterilisation

Radiation can sterilise suitable materials by damaging microorganisms. Penetrating radiation can reach some packaged materials without opening the package.

The absorbed radiation produces the biological effect. The suitability of packaging, exposure and material must be controlled by the intended process. Sterilisation does not mean the package itself becomes an active gamma source merely because radiation passed through it in this model.

### Distinguishing the ideas

Isotopes share proton number and differ in neutron number. Same neutron count with different proton counts does not define isotopes of one element.

Two atoms with eight protons and eight or ten neutrons are oxygen isotopes. An atom with nine protons is a different element even if its neutron count matches one of them. The proton count fixes element identity, so exchanging the roles changes the classification.
