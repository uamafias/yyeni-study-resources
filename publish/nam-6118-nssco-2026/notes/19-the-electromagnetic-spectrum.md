## The electromagnetic spectrum — 4.3.4

### state the components of the electromagnetic spectrum and place them in the correct order of frequency and wavelength

In increasing frequency, the school spectrum order is radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma. Wavelength decreases along that order for a common vacuum speed.

These names divide a continuous spectrum into useful regions. Since c = fλ, a higher frequency at fixed speed requires a shorter wavelength. Learn the order with an explicit direction rather than memorising a sequence without knowing which end is higher. Boundaries between adjacent named bands are conventions rather than physical gaps.

### Locate radio waves in the spectrum

Radio waves occupy the low-frequency, long-wavelength end relative to the other named bands. Microwaves lie next in the stated increasing-frequency order.

A radio transmitter uses a selected frequency rather than emitting an undifferentiated whole spectrum. A longer wavelength does not make it a sound wave.

### Locate microwaves in the spectrum

Microwaves occupy between radio and infrared in the school sequence. They have higher frequency and shorter wavelength than the radio region listed before them.

Microwaves are often treated as a radio-frequency subregion in wider classifications. In this course sequence they have a separate label for communication and heating applications.

### Locate infrared in the spectrum

Infrared lies between microwaves and visible light. Its frequency is below visible red and its wavelength is longer.

Infrared is invisible to the ordinary human eye. A warm object can emit infrared without glowing visibly red. Its thermal applications depend on absorption and emission.

### Locate visible light in the spectrum

Visible light lies between infrared and ultraviolet. Within visible light, red has lower frequency and longer wavelength than violet.

The eye responds to a limited part of the electromagnetic spectrum. Visible is a response-based region, not the only radiation capable of transferring energy.

### Locate ultraviolet in the spectrum

Ultraviolet lies between visible light and X-rays. It has higher frequency and shorter wavelength than visible violet.

Ultraviolet is outside ordinary vision. Different UV subregions have different absorption and biological effects; the school ordering is the broad comparison needed here.

### Locate X-rays in the spectrum

X-rays occupy between ultraviolet and gamma in the school sequence. They have short wavelengths and high frequencies.

X-ray and gamma energy ranges can overlap in detailed classifications, where origin distinguishes the names. The syllabus ordering describes their typical regions and should not be treated as a claim of perfectly sharp universal boundaries.

### Locate gamma rays in the spectrum

Gamma rays occupy the high-frequency, short-wavelength end of the school sequence. They are electromagnetic radiation, not helium nuclei or electrons.

Gamma radiation is associated with nuclear transitions in the basic nuclear account. Its high penetration is useful for certain imaging and industrial tasks but creates exposure concerns.

### Shared electromagnetic properties

The named electromagnetic waves in this spectrum are transverse and travel in vacuum at approximately 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s. Their frequencies and wavelengths differ despite this common vacuum speed.

They can cross a vacuum and do not require air. In a material their speeds can depend on the material and wavelength; the vacuum statement must not be copied unqualified to every substance. A radio signal and a visible-light signal travelling the same vacuum distance take equal propagation times in this model.

### describe the role of electromagnetic waves in: - radio and television communications (radio waves) - satellite television and telephones (microwaves) - electrical appliances, remote controllers for televisions and intruder alarms (infrared) - sterilisation (ultra violet) - medicine and security (X-rays) - optical fibres (visible light and infrared) - killing cancerous cells and detecting cracks in metals (gamma rays)

Electromagnetic applications depend on propagation, detection, absorption and penetration. The useful interaction determines the selected band rather than frequency alone being a universal measure of suitability.

Communication uses a controlled signal that can be transmitted and received. Imaging can exploit different absorption through a material. Heating uses absorbed energy, while sterilisation or treatment uses radiation’s biological effects under controlled conditions. Match the named application to its mechanism rather than giving only a band name.

### Explain radio-wave communication

Radio communication transmits information by controlled changes in an electromagnetic carrier. A receiver detects the signal and recovers its information.

An aerial emits and receives the selected radio signal. Sound from a broadcast microphone is converted into an electrical information signal; it does not itself travel through space as a sound wave to the receiving radio. The receiver converts the recovered information into sound if required.

### Explain microwave satellite and telephone links

Microwaves can carry information between suitable transmitters, receivers and satellites. A directed beam and suitable receiving equipment support a communication link.

The link sends encoded information electromagnetically, rather than transporting an object through the air. Line of sight and alignment can matter. The same broad band has other applications, so describing microwave heating alone does not explain its communication role.

### Explain infrared applications

Infrared can transfer heating energy or carry a coded control signal. Warm-body emission can also be detected by suitable sensors.

A remote control emits pulses detected by its receiver. A thermal intruder detector senses changes in incoming infrared associated with a warm body. A heater instead relies on absorption that raises internal energy. These uses share a wave region but differ in whether the desired outcome is information or heating.

### Explain ultraviolet sterilisation

Suitable ultraviolet radiation can damage microorganisms and is used in controlled sterilisation systems. The same interaction can harm living tissue.

The educational link is between radiation absorption and damage to cellular material. Effective equipment must manage exposure, shielding and the treated region; this example does not supply a do-it-yourself dose or procedure. Do not confuse germ reduction by UV with merely warming a surface.

### Explain X-ray medicine and security

X-ray imaging exploits different transmission through materials. A detector records the spatial pattern of transmitted radiation.

In the simple bone-radiograph example, bone absorbs more strongly than surrounding soft tissue, so the detector receives different signals. Security imaging uses related material-dependent transmission. Radiograph contrast does not mean X-rays reflect from bones like light from a mirror. The examples describe physics rather than decisions about individual medical examinations.

### Explain visible and infrared optical fibres

Visible and infrared signals can be guided along optical fibres. Total internal reflection in a suitable core-cladding system helps confine the light.

The core has higher refractive index than its surrounding cladding. Rays meeting the boundary under the required conditions remain guided. Coded pulses carry information; the glass is not moved along with the message. Real signal loss means repeaters or other system design may be needed over long paths.

### Explain gamma-ray treatment and industrial use

Gamma radiation can affect cells and penetrate materials sufficiently for some industrial inspections. Controlled treatment uses cell damage, while crack detection uses transmission differences.

In industrial radiography a defect changes the radiation reaching a detector, making internal structure visible. The same penetration that permits inspection requires exposure control. Cancer-treatment use is a syllabus example of a specialist application; it is not an instruction about individual treatment choice or dose.

### describe the main dangers of the electromagnetic spectrum

Electromagnetic hazards depend on frequency, intensity, duration and the material exposed. Heating can damage tissue, while sufficiently energetic radiation can cause ionisation and cellular damage.

Intense visible light can harm eyes; infrared and microwaves can cause excessive heating. Ultraviolet can damage skin and eyes, and X-rays and gamma rays are ionising. A useful answer names a mechanism and affected tissue rather than calling every band equally dangerous under every exposure. Lower photon energy does not imply unlimited intensity is harmless. Named effects include microwave heating of body tissue, infrared skin burns, ultraviolet skin and eye damage and increased skin-cancer risk, and cell damage or increased cancer risk from ionising X-rays and gamma radiation.

### outline the safety issues regarding the use of a microwave oven and X-rays

Microwave ovens use shielding and interlocks to contain operation, while X-ray systems control exposure with shielding and restricted access. Equipment protection should not be bypassed.

A metal enclosure and designed door structure limit escaping microwave radiation. X-ray work uses appropriate barriers and exposure management because the beam can penetrate and ionise tissue. These are the intended safety principles in the syllabus, not repair or operating instructions for high-energy equipment.

### Further practice: State the oscillation type shared by electromagnetic waves in the named spectrum.

They are transverse.

### Further practice: Explain the physical reason gamma radiation can be used to treat cancerous tissue and why the beam must be controlled.

Gamma radiation can ionise and damage cells. Healthy cells can also be damaged, so the radiation must be directed and controlled to limit unwanted exposure.

### Further practice: State one exposure-control precaution used with X-ray equipment.

An appropriate barrier shields people who do not need to be in the beam.

### Further practice: Explain one harmful effect of ultraviolet radiation and one harmful effect of infrared radiation.

Ultraviolet can damage skin cells or eyes and increase skin-cancer risk. Infrared absorption can cause excessive heating and skin burns.

### Further practice: Explain how an infrared intruder detector can respond to a person entering its monitored area.

A person emits infrared radiation because their body is warm. The detector senses a change in received infrared and triggers its circuit.
