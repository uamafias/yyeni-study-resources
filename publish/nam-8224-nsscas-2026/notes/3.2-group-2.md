## Group 2 — 3.2.1

### Physical properties and the limits of a trend

Group 2 contains beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium and barium among the familiar non-radioactive members. They are metals with two outer electrons and commonly form 2+ ions. Metallic bonding supplies cohesion, and delocalised electrons explain ordinary electrical and thermal conduction. A metallic appearance or conductivity alone does not identify which member is present.

Down the group, atomic radius and the radius of comparable 2+ ions generally increase as occupied shells are added. First ionisation energy generally decreases because outer electrons are farther from the nucleus and more shielded. These electronic trends were established in Topic 3.1. Increased nuclear charge is present but does not reverse the additional-shell effect in the general ionisation comparison.

Melting points do not follow a smooth one-way sequence. The general fall from the very high value for beryllium has departures, including calcium melting at a higher temperature than magnesium. Metallic attraction depends on ion size and the delocalised electron population, while different solid arrangements also matter. An explanation based only on steadily increasing radius cannot justify ignoring an observed rise in a supplied table.

When describing physical-property data, distinguish a broad tendency from the actual neighbouring comparisons. If the question gives two measured melting points, quote the higher and lower correctly before relating them to a pattern. A numerical trend is evidence to interpret, not a line to overwrite with a memorised rule. Do not infer that the member with the lowest melting point must be the most reactive in water: melting and electron-loss reaction rates are different properties. Use radius and shielding for the reactivity argument, and structure for melting behaviour.

### Reactions of the metals

For a Group 2 metal M forming its simple oxide, 2M + O2 → 2MO. Magnesium gives the familiar bright white light on burning and a white magnesium oxide product. In air, other products can also form, so a simple oxygen equation describes the named oxide rather than guaranteeing a pure product from an open-air burn. The metal loses electrons and oxygen is reduced.

Calcium, strontium and barium react with cold water to give the hydroxide and hydrogen: M(s) + 2H2O(l) → M(OH)2 + H2(g). The hydroxide's dissolved amount depends on its solubility and the water quantity. Calcium can give a cloudy mixture when solid calcium hydroxide is present. Reaction generally becomes more vigorous down this sequence as removal of outer electrons becomes easier.

Magnesium is much slower with cold water, partly because its surface layer affects access. The cold-water product is Mg(OH)2, whereas heated magnesium with steam gives MgO(s) + H2(g). Beryllium does not show the same ready cold-water behaviour. State the member and water temperature rather than applying the most vigorous example to the whole group.

With dilute hydrochloric acid, M(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MCl2(aq) + H2(g) for the usual reactive Group 2 examples. Effervescence accompanies gas formation and the metal is consumed. Use a suitable non-oxidising dilute acid for this simple hydrogen account; acid identity can alter the products. Dilute sulphuric acid can form a low-solubility sulphate coating on some lower members, reducing further reaction. Observed vigour therefore depends on both intrinsic electron-loss tendency and the surface/solution setting. Keep surface area and acid concentration comparable when testing a group trend.

### Oxides, hydroxides and carbonates

The oxide and hydroxide of a typical Group 2 metal show basic behaviour. Calcium oxide reacts with water to form calcium hydroxide, while magnesium oxide reacts much less readily and forms sparingly soluble magnesium hydroxide. An insoluble or sparingly soluble solid can still react with acid: the acid consumes basic species at the solid surface and in solution.

The acid reaction of an oxide is MO(s) + 2H+(aq) → M2+(aq) + H2O(l). For a hydroxide, M(OH)2(s) + 2H+(aq) → M2+(aq) + 2H2O(l). With hydrochloric acid the corresponding chloride remains in the products. These are acid–base reactions; they do not produce hydrogen simply because the compound contains a metal. The metal in the oxide is already in its positive oxidation state.

Carbonates are generally low-solubility solids and do not simply turn into hydroxide on adding ordinary water. With dilute acid, MCO3(s) + 2H+(aq) → M2+(aq) + CO2(g) + H2O(l). Effervescence is therefore expected. Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky under the appropriate test, whereas hydrogen from a metal–acid reaction has a different identity. Distinguish metal, oxide and carbonate before deciding which gas, if any, forms.

As an original amount example, 0.0250 mol of calcium carbonate needs 0.0500 mol H+ for complete neutralisation and can produce 0.0250 mol CO2. The one-to-two acid ratio follows the balanced ionic equation. A carbonate may remain if acid is insufficient, so unchanged solid does not prove it cannot react. Compare acid amount, concentration, surface area and any product coating before attributing a slow observation to one cause. This prepares the agricultural neutralisation explanation later in the topic.

### Heating carbonates and nitrates

Thermal decomposition uses heat to break a compound into simpler products. A Group 2 carbonate forms the oxide and carbon dioxide on sufficient heating: MCO3(s) → MO(s) + CO2(g). For calcium carbonate this is the lime-making change, CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). Carbon dioxide loss reduces the mass remaining in the heated container.

For the Group 2 nitrates, the school decomposition pattern is 2M(NO3)2 → 2MO + 4NO2 + O2 on heating. Nitrogen dioxide gives brown fumes and oxygen is also produced. Do not write the carbonate products for a nitrate, or treat nitrogen dioxide's brown appearance as the identity of both gases. The gas amounts follow the coefficients: four moles of NO2 accompany one mole O2 in this equation. Check the oxygen balance explicitly: two M(NO3)2 units supply twelve oxygen atoms. Two remain in the oxide, eight enter the four NO2 units and two form O2. The metal amount is conserved as well. This atom count rejects a proposed nitrate equation that omits oxygen or uses an unbalanced gas coefficient.

Thermal stability generally increases down the group for these salts. A smaller 2+ cation has a higher charge density and more strongly polarises the large carbonate or nitrate anion, making decomposition easier in the qualitative model. Larger cations lower down distort the anion less, so stronger heating is needed. This explanation concerns anion distortion and stability rather than a claim that the nitrate ion's formal charge changes down the group.

For original arithmetic, 10.0 g of pure CaCO3 with molar mass 100 g mol⁻¹ contains 0.100 mol. Complete heating yields 0.100 mol CaO, or 5.60 g using 56.0 g mol⁻¹, and 0.100 mol CO2. The mass decrease is 4.40 g. This assumes complete decomposition and no loss of solid. A short heating period may leave carbonate, giving a larger final residue than the complete-decomposition prediction. Topic 5.1 develops mass measurements and the use of repeated heating to assess completion.

### Using a trend to make a prediction

The word “reactive” needs a process. Group 2 metals generally react more readily down the group in the water comparison, while their carbonates generally resist thermal decomposition more strongly. These trends are not contradictory. One concerns electron removal from metal atoms; the other concerns stability of a compound containing M2+ and a large anion.

For a metal below calcium, predict easier outer-electron removal from its larger, more shielded atom and generally more vigorous cold-water reaction. For its carbonate, predict greater thermal stability because the larger cation has lower polarising power. Do not carry one trend's arrow directly into the other explanation.

Physical observations can give further evidence, but should be kept distinct. Metallic conductivity is a shared feature; irregular melting points need their own structural interpretation. If a supplied table shows a local rise, describe that rise rather than replacing it by the broad downward tendency. Solubility of hydroxides and sulphates also changes in opposite general directions, so “more soluble down the group” is incomplete without naming the compound family.

When interpreting an experiment, check that the measurements compare the same temperature, acid concentration, water amount, surface condition and reaction time where these matter. A sulphate coating may slow metal–acid reaction even for a metal expected to lose electrons readily. An incomplete thermal decomposition may reflect insufficient heating rather than an incorrect final-product equation. Make the prediction from the appropriate chemical model, then identify the experimental limitation before drawing a stronger conclusion. This reasoning applies the attraction, shielding and bonding ideas from Topics 3.1 and 1.3 to unfamiliar group members without inventing exact numerical results.

### Opposite solubility trends

The hydroxides and sulphates show opposite general solubility trends down Group 2. Hydroxides become more soluble from magnesium towards barium, whereas sulphates become less soluble. State the compound family in the answer. A group-position prediction without naming hydroxide or sulphate can give the wrong result.

Magnesium hydroxide is sparingly soluble. Calcium hydroxide has limited solubility but supplies enough dissolved hydroxide to give alkaline limewater. The lower Group 2 hydroxides dissolve more readily in the general trend. More dissolved hydroxide in a saturated solution can give greater alkalinity, but compare at the same temperature and recognise that adding extra solid beyond saturation does not simply keep increasing the dissolved concentration.

Magnesium sulphate is readily soluble, while barium sulphate is very low-solubility. Calcium sulphate is much less soluble than magnesium sulphate. Precipitation depends on the concentrations of the ions mixed, so a very dilute solution can fail to give a visible solid even when a salt is described as low-solubility. Conversely, a precipitate is an observation about a particular mixture, not a measurement of an element's metallic reactivity.

For a qualitative prediction between calcium and barium, expect the barium hydroxide to be more soluble but the barium sulphate to be less soluble under comparable aqueous settings. Solubility reflects the balance between separating a lattice and stabilising ions in water; changing cation size influences those contributions differently for the two anions. The syllabus requires the observed directions and their use in predictions, rather than a numerical dissolution calculation. Keep the trends separate from acid reactions: low-solubility magnesium hydroxide can still neutralise acid, and a sulphate coating can influence how much metal remains accessible.

## Group 2 — 3.2.2

### Slaking lime

Calcium oxide, CaO, is called lime or quicklime. Adding water produces calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2, called slaked lime: CaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(s). The reaction releases heat and the mixture can become hot. Its temperature rise indicates an exothermic process, linking to Topic 2.1's system-and-surroundings account.

The product is not calcium carbonate. Water supplies the hydrogen and oxygen needed for hydroxide formation; carbon is absent from the reactants. To form carbonate from the hydroxide, carbon dioxide must be involved in a later reaction. This atom check is a useful way to reject an incorrectly remembered equation.

Because calcium hydroxide is only moderately soluble, a practical mixture may contain solid and liquid together. A suspension is not the same as clear limewater, which contains dissolved calcium and hydroxide ions at saturation. Extra undissolved solid does not give an unlimited dissolved concentration. Use the product form requested in a question and distinguish a slaked-lime paste from the clear solution used in gas tests.

For 0.100 mol CaO reacting completely, the equation produces 0.100 mol Ca(OH)2. The one-to-one amount ratio is independent of how much excess water remains. Using molar masses 56.0 g mol⁻¹ for CaO and 74.0 g mol⁻¹ for Ca(OH)2, this corresponds to 5.60 g oxide forming 7.40 g hydroxide. The extra product mass comes from water incorporated in the reaction, rather than creation of calcium. A larger volume of water can change the measured temperature rise by increasing the mass warmed without changing the basic reaction equation. The exothermic description therefore needs no invented fixed temperature rise. In building uses, slaking is one stage of preparing lime-based materials; later hardening and cement manufacture involve further chemistry and must not be collapsed into the water-addition step alone.

### Agriculture and building materials

Powdered limestone contains calcium carbonate. In acidic soil it consumes acid through CaCO3(s) + 2H+(aq) → Ca2+(aq) + CO2(g) + H2O(l). Calcium hydroxide can also neutralise acidity: Ca(OH)2 + 2H+ → Ca2+ + 2H2O. Neutralisation can raise soil pH towards a suitable range for a crop. It does not imply that an arbitrary large addition improves any soil; the required amount depends on measured acidity and the material's neutralising capacity.

Powdering limestone exposes more surface area and can make acid reaction faster. It changes the accessibility and rate, not the number of moles of H+ neutralised by one mole of pure CaCO3. For equal pure masses, hydroxide and carbonate also have different molar masses, so equal mass is not automatically equal neutralising amount. In a Namibian farm example, the appropriate comparison needs soil information rather than a guess based on a product name.

Limestone is also a raw material for lime and cement. Heating it releases CO2 and gives CaO. In cement manufacture, calcium-containing material reacts at high temperature with silica- and alumina-containing raw materials to form the cement compounds. Cement is therefore not simply ground limestone or pure calcium hydroxide. Its later setting with water involves hydration of that manufactured mixture.

In a lime-based mortar, calcium hydroxide can harden by reacting with carbon dioxide from air: Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3 + H2O. Distinguish this carbonation from slaking, which adds water to CaO, and from cement hydration. The three processes have different reactants and purposes. Explaining a building use requires the named material, its chemical change and the resulting solid binding material, rather than an unsupported claim that lime, limestone and cement are identical substances.
