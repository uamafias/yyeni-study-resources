## Measuring living standards

### Living standards and the indicators that measure them

Living standards are the level of material well-being of a person or a household: how well the household is housed, fed, clothed, schooled and cared for, together with the security of that level. The starting measure is Gross Domestic Product - the total value of the goods and services produced in a country in a given period - but for living standards the figure to use is real GDP per head: GDP adjusted for price changes, then divided by the population. It shows the average output available per person, so countries of different sizes compare.

The indicators that complete the picture: income per person; health measures such as life expectancy and infant mortality; education measures such as mean years of schooling and literacy; and access to basic services such as clean water and electricity. Together they capture what GDP per head alone may miss, and they travel in company: where output per person rises, health and schooling tend to rise with it.

### Real GDP per head: taking out the effect of prices

GDP measured at the prices of the year it is produced is nominal GDP. It can rise for two reasons: more goods and services are produced, or the same goods and services are sold at higher prices. Only the first makes people better off, so for living standards economists use real GDP - GDP measured at constant prices, adjusted for changes in the price level. Real GDP changes only when the quantity of output changes.

Real GDP per head therefore has two components: real GDP, the output side, and the population, the people who share that output.

Real GDP per head = real GDP / population

Worked example (figures invented for teaching). A country's nominal GDP rises from N$150 billion to N$165 billion in a year, a rise of 10%, while the price level also rises by 10%. Measured at the first year's prices, real GDP is N$165 billion / 1.10 = N$150 billion: no extra output was produced. If the population grows over the year from 2.5 million to 2.55 million, real GDP per head falls from N$150 000 million / 2.5 million = N$60 000 to N$150 000 million / 2.55 million = about N$58 824. A headline rise of N$15 billion in GDP has left the average person with slightly less real output, not more.

### The Human Development Index (HDI) and its components

The Human Development Index (HDI) is a measure of living standards published each year by the United Nations Development Programme. It starts from the idea that development means people living long and healthy lives, being educated, and having enough income to live decently, so it combines three components.

Health is measured by life expectancy at birth: the number of years a newborn baby would be expected to live if today's death rates stayed the same.

Education is measured in two ways: mean years of schooling, the average number of years of schooling received by adults aged 25 and over, and expected years of schooling, the number of years a child starting school can expect to receive if today's enrolment patterns continue.

Income is measured by GNI per head at purchasing power parity (PPP). Gross National Income is GDP plus the income residents earn from abroad, minus the income paid to people abroad; dividing it by the population gives GNI per head. PPP converts each country's figure at a rate that reflects what money buys there, so income in a country where food and rent are cheap is counted as buying more.

Each component is turned into a score between 0 and 1, and the scores are combined into one index between 0 and 1: the closer to 1, the higher the human development. Norway, for example, has sat near the top of the HDI rankings for many years, while several countries of Africa's Sahel region, such as Niger and Chad, sit near the bottom.

### Reading the figures for two countries

Namibia records GDP of N$150 billion and a population of 2.5 million: GDP per head is N$150 000m / 2.5m, which is N$60 000 a year. Vietnam, as a non-African example, records GDP of US$300 billion with 100 million people: GDP per head is US$3 000 a year, and at N$20 to US$1 that is N$60 000 as well, so the averages match. The other indicators: life expectancy 74 years in Vietnam against 60 in Namibia, household electricity 99% against about half. One figure is a doorway, not a verdict: the same GDP per head stands while health and access separate the two, because GDP per head is an average that may hide how income is shared.

### Reading the HDI beside income

Figures invented for teaching. Two countries each record GNI per head at PPP of US$10 000. In Country P, life expectancy at birth is 74 years, adults have 10 years of schooling on average and children starting school can expect 14 years; in Country Q, life expectancy is 62 years, adults have 6 years of schooling and children can expect 11 years. On income alone the two rank equal. The HDI scores Country P higher, because its people live 74 - 62 = 12 years longer on average and receive more schooling. The index picks up that P's income has been turned into longer and better-educated lives, which a money measure on its own does not show.

### Advantages and disadvantages of real GDP per head

Real GDP per head has real strengths as a measure. The figures are collected regularly in most countries using similar methods, so they are widely available. Adjusting for prices means a rise reflects more output rather than inflation, so years can be compared fairly, and dividing by population lets a small country such as Namibia be compared with a large one. A higher figure means more goods and services are available per person on average, which tends to go with better food, housing and services.

Its weaknesses come from what the number leaves out. It is an average, so where income is spread unevenly the typical household may earn well below it: a mean of N$60 000 can stand beside a majority earning a fraction of that. Own-consumption crops and self-built housing are included in GDP in principle, even when they are not sold. However, measuring subsistence and informal output is difficult, so some may be under-recorded and measured GDP may understate output. Unpaid household services, such as caring for relatives, are excluded and can improve living standards without raising GDP. It says nothing about how many hours people work, about the quality of goods, or about pollution and the using-up of resources that may lower the quality of life. It hides what the output is: spending on weapons counts the same as spending on clinics. Finally, comparing countries needs a common currency, and market exchange rates may not reflect what money buys locally, which is why comparisons are usually adjusted for the cost of living.

### Advantages and disadvantages of the HDI

The HDI's main advantage is breadth. Income matters to living standards because of what it can buy, but two countries with the same income can differ greatly in how long their people live and how much schooling they receive. By including life expectancy and schooling, the HDI measures outcomes people experience directly. Its income component is adjusted for the cost of living through PPP, so it avoids part of the exchange-rate problem of a plain money measure. A single index between 0 and 1 also makes countries easy to rank and progress easy to track over time.

Its disadvantages fall under two headings. First, it is still a national average: a country can post a rising HDI while one region or group lives shorter and poorer lives, so the HDI may hide inequality just as real GDP per head does. Second, it leaves things out: political freedom, safety, housing, working conditions, leisure and the state of the environment are all part of living standards, but none appears in the index. Years of schooling count time in a classroom, not how much is learned, and in some countries the data are estimates or several years old. A country can also lift its HDI through one component while another worsens, so the single number may hide which part of life changed.

## Comparing living standards and income distribution

### Comparing living standards honestly

To compare living standards between countries, GDP per head is first adjusted for the cost of living, because a money figure alone may mislead where prices differ: the same income buys more where food and rent are cheaper. The adjusted figure is then read alongside health, education and access indicators - life expectancy, schooling, clean water - which capture outcomes the money figure cannot.

The distribution of income is how a country's total income is shared among its households, and living standards depend not just on the average income a country produces but on where a household sits in that sharing. The Netherlands and Brazil illustrate: income is spread evenly in the Netherlands, while in Brazil a smaller group takes a larger share of a similar average, so the typical Dutch household tends to live better even where the averages sit close.

### Why living standards differ between countries

Countries differ in living standards mainly because they differ in how much each worker can produce, and in how widely the gains reach people.

Productivity: a worker with machinery, skills and modern technology produces far more in an hour than one working by hand. A wheat farmer in Canada with a combine harvester may harvest as much in a day as a farmer in the Zambezi region with a hoe harvests in several weeks. Higher output per worker supports higher real incomes per head.

Education and healthcare: a healthier, better-educated workforce tends to be more productive and lives longer, raising both income and the non-income parts of living standards.

Population growth: where population grows faster than output, real GDP per head falls even while total GDP rises.

Structure of the economy: where a large share of workers farms small plots for little output, average income tends to be lower than where more people work in manufacturing and services.

Natural resources: oil, minerals, fertile land and water can raise income, though resource wealth may be narrowly shared.

Saving, investment and stability: countries that save and invest more build up capital, and peace, stable government and good roads and power supplies encourage that investment.

These causes feed each other: low income limits saving, schooling and healthcare, which keeps productivity low. Topic 5.4 takes them further.

### Why living standards and income differ within a country

Inside one country, households can live very differently. Namibia is often listed among the countries with the most unequal sharing of income in the world, partly a legacy of unequal ownership of land and unequal access to schooling in the past. The main reasons for such gaps are these.

Skills and qualifications: a qualified engineer or doctor earns many times what an unskilled labourer earns, because the skills are scarcer and add more to output.

Wealth and property income: households that own land, buildings, shares or savings receive rent, interest, dividends and profit on top of wages, and that income can be saved to buy more wealth, so the gap may widen over time.

Unemployment and inactivity: households with no one in work, or whose members are too old or ill to work, depend on benefits, pensions or family support.

Region and location: well-paid jobs, schools, clinics, piped water and electricity cluster in towns such as Windhoek and Walvis Bay, while rural regions depend more on subsistence farming and seasonal work.

Gender and age: where women are paid less or carry more unpaid care, and where young people struggle to find a first job, incomes differ between groups.

The tax and benefit system: progressive taxes and benefits such as old-age pensions tend to narrow the gap in take-home income; regressive taxes tend to widen it. In Norway, for example, high taxes fund public services and benefits that help keep the gap between households narrow.

### Why the same average can house different lives

Figures invented for teaching. Two countries each record real GDP per head of N$60 000 a year. Typical disposable income per person is N$42 000 in one and N$24 000 in the other. These are separately supplied income figures, not household incomes calculated from GDP. With similar prices and public services, the person with N$42 000 can afford more goods. Output per head alone hides this difference: distribution, taxes and transfers influence how much disposable income people receive.

### Progressive does not mean regressive

The confusion: learners mix up a progressive income tax with a regressive income tax system, assuming both take a larger share from high earners, or judge a system by amounts paid rather than shares. The boundary: a progressive income tax takes a larger share of income from high earners, tending to narrow the gap in take-home pay; a regressive one takes a larger share from low earners, widening the gap. The test that tells them apart: which group pays the larger share of its own income - the shares, not the amounts.
