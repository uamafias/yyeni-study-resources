## The factors that affect population growth

### What population growth is

Population growth is the change in the number of people living in a country over a period. Three factors drive it. The birth rate is the number of live births per 1000 people in a country in a year, so a birth rate of 24 per 1000 in a district around Oshakati means 24 babies born for every 1000 residents in the year. The death rate is the number of deaths per 1000 people in a year. Net migration is the balance between immigration, people moving into a country to live, and emigration, people moving out to live elsewhere; the two are usually reported together as one net figure per 1000 people per year.

### Natural increase and total growth in figures

Natural increase is the birth rate minus the death rate. A country with a birth rate of 24 per 1000 people per year and a death rate of 8 per 1000 has a natural increase of 16 per 1000: 24 - 8 = 16, so its population is growing before migration is counted. Overall growth then adds net migration: where net migration is inward at 3 per 1000 people per year, total growth is 19 per 1000, because 16 + 3 = 19. Japan's figures run the other way: a birth rate of 7 per 1000 against a death rate of 12 per 1000 means deaths outnumber births by 5 per 1000, so unless immigration fills the gap the population shrinks.

### Migration changes the count too

Migration moves people across borders and so changes a country's population without a single birth or death. Workers have moved to Walvis Bay for port and fishing jobs, adding to the town's count, while Namibians have left to study in the Netherlands or to work in Germany, subtracting from the count at home. Because migration responds to jobs, wages and living conditions, it can change quickly: a new mine or a drought can shift the net figure within a few years, faster than birth and death rates move.

## Why population growth rates differ between countries

### Why birth and death rates differ

Birth rates tend to be higher in low-income countries, where children contribute to household income early and schooling for girls and family planning are harder to reach. In high-income countries such as Germany or Japan, children cost more to raise and educate, and families tend to be small. Death rates follow income and health care: where clinics, clean water and secure food are scarce, more people die young and life expectancy, the average years a newborn would live if the year's pattern of deaths continued, is shorter. Improving healthcare can lower mortality while births remain high, allowing rapid population growth. However, the crude death rate also depends on age structure: an ageing population can have a high rate despite good healthcare.

### Net migration between countries

People move towards work and away from hardship, so the migration side of growth differs widely. Germany draws in workers because its wages and openings are high, while conflict or drought pushes people out of their home countries. Within southern Africa, movement follows mines, ports and harvests: a worker leaving Oshakati for Windhoek shifts the count within the country, whereas leaving for Germany reduces the national count.

### Death rates and net migration: why they differ

Death rates differ between countries for three main reasons.

Health and living conditions: where clinics, doctors, vaccinations, clean water, sanitation and a secure food supply are within reach, fewer people die of infectious disease and fewer babies and young children die. At a given age, mortality is therefore likely to be higher where these services are scarce; the overall crude death rate also depends on age structure.

Disease and conflict: an epidemic or a war can raise the death rate sharply. In southern Africa, HIV and AIDS pushed death rates up and cut life expectancy in the 1990s and early 2000s, until treatment became widely available.

Age structure: the death rate counts deaths per 1000 of the whole population, so a country with many older people records more deaths even where its health care is excellent. That is why Japan or Germany can record a higher death rate than a poorer country whose population is mostly young.

Net migration differs because of pull and push factors. Pull factors draw people in: jobs, higher wages, safety, good schools and universities, and family already living there. Push factors drive people out: unemployment, low pay, conflict, drought and persecution. Germany, the United Kingdom and the United States record net immigration; within southern Africa, South Africa draws workers from neighbouring countries. Net migration also depends on what limits movement: immigration rules, the cost of travel, and language. A country can have immigrants and emigrants at once - skilled doctors may leave while construction workers arrive - and the net figure is the balance of the two.

### Birth rate or infant mortality rate?

Answers on this objective sometimes swap two measures that sound alike. The birth rate counts live births per 1000 people in a year. The infant mortality rate counts deaths of babies under one per 1000 live births in a year. The boundary: one measures new life against the whole population, the other early deaths against the babies born. The test: ask what is counted and out of what group. A country can have a high birth rate and a falling infant mortality rate together, because better care keeps babies alive without changing how many are born.

### Interest rates and tax rates do not answer it

Some answers drift into policy terms: interest rates, the price a borrower pays a lender for borrowed money, or tax rates, the compulsory share of income or spending taken by the government. The boundary: interest is a price agreed with a lender, whereas a tax is a payment required by law. The test: ask who receives the payment and whether it is compulsory. Neither drives population growth, which turns on the birth rate, the death rate and net migration, so an answer built on borrowing costs has left the question.

## The effects of changes in population size and structure

### Optimum population

The optimum population is the size of population that, with a country's existing resources and technology, gives the highest output per person. Below it, too few workers make full use of the land and equipment available, so output per person sits lower than it could. Above it, extra people stretch resources and capital thin, so output per person falls. The optimum is not fixed: better technology, more equipment or new resources move it, so a country can grow into a population that once seemed too large.

### When the population grows

A rising population changes what a country needs. More children mean more school places: a fast-growing district may need new classrooms at Oshakati while the taxpayers who fund them rise more slowly. In time the extra children reach working age, and where jobs exist the labour supply grows, which can raise the economy's output. Where jobs, water or land do not keep pace, the arrivals count as dependants rather than earners, and output per person tends to fall. Brazil's cities grew this quickly: industry expanded, but housing and services lagged behind the arrivals.

### When the population falls

A population falls where deaths exceed births and immigration does not fill the gap, or where emigration is heavy. Japan's population has been shrinking since around 2010, and Bulgaria's has fallen since 1990 as low birth rates combined with emigration.

The effects run mainly through the labour force and the market. Fewer people of working age means a smaller labour force, so firms may struggle to fill vacancies and output may grow more slowly or fall. Fewer consumers means a smaller home market, so firms selling at home face falling demand, and schools, houses and shops in shrinking towns stand empty. The government collects less tax from fewer workers, while the remaining population is often older, so spending on pensions and health care may rise as a share of the budget.

The effect on living standards depends on where the country stands against its optimum population. A country above its optimum, where land, water and capital are stretched, may see output per person rise as the pressure eases. A country at or below its optimum is likely to see output per person fall, because too few workers remain to make full use of its resources. Governments facing decline may encourage immigration, support families with children, or invest in machinery that lets fewer workers produce more.

### When the population ages and shifts

A population can change in structure rather than size. Where the birth rate falls and life expectancy lengthens, the share of older people rises, as in Japan: the workforce shrinks relative to the retired, pension and health costs borne by the government and by workers climb, and firms face labour shortages. Where the share under 15 is large, schooling is paid for first and the taxes those children pay come much later. Gender distribution matters too: where men migrate alone for mining work, some regions hold more women of working age, shifting who supports households.

### Changes in gender distribution

The gender distribution is the balance between males and females in a population. It changes mainly through migration that draws one sex more than the other, and through differences in life expectancy: women tend to live longer than men, so the oldest age groups hold more women.

Migration for work is a common cause of imbalance in southern Africa. Where men leave rural regions for mines, ports and building sites, the home region holds more women of working age. More households are headed by women, and where women earn less or carry the unpaid care of children and elderly relatives, household incomes there may fall; farms may also go short of labour at ploughing and harvest. The region receiving the men gains labour but may face pressure on housing. Some Gulf states, such as Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, have many more men than women because so many of their migrant workers are male.

An imbalance also changes demand, since the goods and services households buy differ with who lives in them, and, in time, the number of births, since fewer couples form where one sex is scarce. In China, a preference for sons over several decades left more young men than young women. A government may respond with policies such as childcare that let more women take paid work, adding to the labour force.

### Reading a population pyramid

A population pyramid sets the number or share of males and females at each age side by side, rising up the age groups. A wide base relative to the top indicates high birth rates and a young population. A narrow base with a broad upper section indicates low birth rates, longer lives and an ageing population. Reading it answers two questions: how fast the next generation of workers replaces the current one, and how heavy the dependent groups at the top and bottom are relative to the working ages between them.

### Reading two pyramids

Figures invented for teaching. Pyramid A: 40% of the population aged 0-14 (20% male, 20% female), 56% aged 15-64, and 4% aged 65 and over, with each age bar narrower than the one below it - a wide base tapering to a point. Pyramid B: 13% aged 0-14, 60% aged 15-64 and 27% aged 65 and over, with the bars for the oldest ages wider on the female side (16% female against 11% male aged 65 and over).

Reading A: the wide base shows a high birth rate, and the narrow top shows a small elderly share. Shorter life expectancy may contribute, but past births and migration matter too; this shape alone does not establish a high crude death rate. The large under-15 group needs schools now and jobs within 15 years. Every 100 people of working age support (40 + 4) / 56 x 100, about 79 dependants, mostly children.

Reading B: the narrow base shows a low birth rate, and the broad top shows long life expectancy: an ageing population. Every 100 people of working age support (13 + 27) / 60 x 100, about 67 dependants, mostly older people. The working-age share is large today, but the small under-15 group means fewer workers will replace those retiring, so pension and health spending will weigh on a shrinking workforce. The wider female side at the top shows women living longer than men.

One more pattern to look for: a bar cut in on one side at working ages - fewer men aged 20 to 40, say - points to men emigrating for work.
