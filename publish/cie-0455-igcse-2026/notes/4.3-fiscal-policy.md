## The government budget

### The government budget, defined

The government budget is the government's plan, usually set out once a year, of the revenue it expects to raise - most of it from taxation - and the spending it intends to carry out on services such as schooling, clinics and roads. In Japan the yearly budget is presented to the Diet in the same spirit: one statement pairing what the state expects to collect with what it plans to spend. The budget is a plan, not a record: it states what the government intends for the year ahead, and the outturn may differ from it.

### A budget worked out in numbers

Where planned spending exceeds expected revenue, the budget is in deficit; where revenue exceeds spending, it is in surplus. Work an invented plan through: N$72 billion of spending set against N$64 billion of revenue leaves a gap of N$8 billion that the government plans to borrow, since 72 - 64 = 8; a revenue forecast of N$70 billion against spending of N$65 billion would instead leave a N$5 billion surplus that can repay past borrowing. A balanced budget expects the two sides to match. The calculation has one shape: budget balance = total government revenue - total government spending. A negative answer is a deficit of that size, a positive answer a surplus, and zero a balanced budget. Where revenue comes from several sources, add them first. An invented year: N$58 billion of taxes plus N$4 billion of fees and other revenue gives 58 + 4 = 62, N$62 billion of revenue; against spending of N$67 billion the balance is 62 - 67 = -5, a budget deficit of N$5 billion.

### A budget deficit is not a current account deficit

Learners who hear the word deficit often treat the budget and the current account as one balance. They are different balances: the budget balance compares government spending with government revenue, while the current account compares the value of a country's exports of goods and services with the value of its imports. To tell them apart, ask which two things the balance compares - the state's books, or the country's trade.

## Why governments spend

### Goods the market under-provides

Governments spend to provide goods and services that the market left alone tends to under-provide. Public goods such as defence, policing and street lighting over Otavi's taxi rank cannot be confined to those who pay, so private firms tend to build too little of them; merit goods such as schooling and vaccination are under-consumed where households undervalue their benefits when asked to pay directly. In Vietnam, coastal provinces fund sea dykes no single household would raise alone - the same reason, a public good provided by the state.

### Redistribution

Governments also spend to redistribute income. Pensions, child grants and subsidised housing transfer spending power from taxpayers towards households on low incomes, narrowing the gap in living standards that the market alone leaves wide. The grant a Katutura grandmother receives is not payment for a service: it is a deliberate transfer, judged fair because the aim is a floor under the poorest households.

### Spending to steady the economy

Spending is also used to pursue the macroeconomic aims. Where total spending in the economy is weak and workers sit idle, raising government spending tends to raise output and employment where there is spare capacity in the economy; near full capacity, extra spending tends instead to push prices up. Work the chain through an invented project: N$500 million committed to resurfacing the road to Etosha employs road crews, their wages are spent in the towns along the route, and that spending sustains further jobs - one public project raising incomes across the region.

## Why governments tax

### What a tax is, and why it exists

A tax is a compulsory payment to a government, levied on income, spending or wealth, owed whether or not the payer receives any specific service in return. Taxes exist first to raise the revenue that pays for public spending: without them the budget would fund nothing. In Portugal a household meets them twice over - income tax on the payslip, value-added tax on the shopping bill - and both feed the same public accounts.

### Taxation as the main source of revenue

Of all the ways a government raises money, taxation raises by far the largest amount, and the reason is its reach. A tax is compulsory and falls on the incomes, spending and profits of the whole economy, so even modest rates applied to so large a base yield large sums every year. In Namibia, income tax on individuals and companies, value-added tax and the country's share of the customs and excise duties pooled by the Southern African Customs Union together provide the great majority of the revenue in each budget. The other sources are smaller or less reliable. Fees and charges, such as those for passports and vehicle licences, cover the cost of particular services rather than funding the budget as a whole. Profits paid to the government by state-owned enterprises tend to be small and rise and fall with those firms' trading. Selling a state asset, such as a share in a state-owned company, raises money once. Grants from foreign governments vary from year to year. Borrowing fills gaps, but it is not revenue at all: it is a debt that must be repaid with interest out of later budgets. In the United Kingdom, too, taxes on income, spending and profits provide the great majority of government receipts. So a government that wants to spend more year after year is, in the end, deciding how much tax to raise.

### Beyond revenue

Taxation also redistributes: a tax that takes a larger share of high incomes than of low ones transfers spending power towards poorer households, and the grants it funds do the same. And it corrects prices: a duty on tobacco makes the price carry more of the harm smoking imposes on others - the clinic time, the chest cases - steering buying away from a demerit good whose market price leaves that harm uncounted.

### Direct is not indirect

A common mistake is to treat every tax as one undifferentiated charge. A direct tax is levied on income or profit and paid straight to the revenue authority by the person or firm taxed; an indirect tax is levied on spending and collected by the seller, who adds it to the price and passes it on. To sort a tax, ask what the charge falls on - income or spending - and who hands it over: the taxed party itself, or a trader collecting it from buyers.

## Sorting taxes

### Direct and indirect

Direct taxes fall on income or profit and are paid over by the taxed party itself: income tax withheld from wages at Oshakati Textiles, company tax on Marula Foods' profits. Indirect taxes fall on spending and are collected by traders: value-added tax added to a bill in a Gobabis shop, excise duty on fuel in Germany, each collected from buyers and remitted. What settles the sorting is what the charge is levied on and who hands it over.

### By the share of income

A progressive tax takes a rising share of income as income rises; a regressive tax takes a larger share of a low income than of a high one; a proportional tax takes the same share of every income. The share of income, not the amount paid, is what the classification turns on.

### The shares worked out

Work invented figures through: a worker paying N$1 500 of income tax on a N$10 000 wage pays 15 per cent of it, a manager paying N$6 000 on N$30 000 pays 20 per cent - the share rises with income, so the income tax is progressive. Value-added tax at a flat 15 per cent takes the same N$15 from a N$100 basket whatever the buyer earns, but a poorer household spends a larger part of its income on such baskets, so the tax takes a larger share of that household's income - regressive in effect. A proportional tax sits between the two. A flat income tax of 20 per cent takes N$2 000 from a N$10 000 income and N$6 000 from a N$30 000 income: the higher earner pays three times as much money, but the share of income is the same 20 per cent at both levels, so the tax is proportional, not progressive. A common slip is to call a tax progressive because richer people pay more money; the test is the share of income, not the amount. Estonia is one country that charges a single flat rate of income tax on most personal income, and a company tax charged at one rate on all profits works proportionally on profit.

### A consequence is not a cause

After a tax rises, some learners write the effects that follow as though they produced the change. The tax change is the cause; what follows - lower disposable income, dearer goods, changed buying - is its consequence. To check, ask which way the events run.

### Interest rates are not taxes

Asked about a tax measure, some learners reach for the interest rate. An interest rate is the price paid for borrowing money; a tax is a compulsory levy on income or spending. To tell them apart, ask who sets the charge and what it falls on.

## What makes a tax good

### The four principles

Adam Smith's principles still frame the judgement. Equity: those with a greater ability to pay bear a larger share. Certainty: the payer knows what is due, when it is due, and how it is to be paid. Convenience: payment falls due where and when it is easy to pay. Economy: the tax costs little to collect relative to what it raises. Each principle is a standard the system is held to, not a description of any one tax.

### The principles in a payslip

Pay-as-you-earn income tax shows three of them at once. A machinist at Oshakati Textiles has income tax taken from each month's wage by the employer and passed to the revenue authority: nothing further is owed later, no journey to a tax office - convenience; the rates and bands were published before the year began - certainty; the employer's payroll collects it with little extra cost - economy. In Japan, salary withholding works the same way, and for the same reasons.

### Where the principles pull apart

A market levy that takes the same N$20 from every trader in Katutura whatever her takings scores well on economy - one collector, one round - and badly on equity, since N$20 from a thin day is a heavier share than from a good one. The principles pull against one another, and a tax system trades among them: simplicity bought at the price of fairness is a choice, not an accident.

### Further qualities of a good tax

Smith's four principles are not the whole test; three further qualities are commonly added. Efficiency: a good tax raises its revenue without strongly discouraging what the economy needs - work, saving, investment and enterprise. An income tax so steep that a nurse in Rundu keeps little of her overtime pay may lead her to refuse extra shifts, and a company tax far above neighbouring countries' may lead a firm to build its new plant across the border; either way output and later revenue are lost. Flexibility: a good tax can be changed quickly and simply when the economy's needs change. A value-added tax rate can be raised or lowered in the next budget and applied at every till straight away, so it suits a government that needs to adjust its revenue or the spending power left in the economy. Difficulty of evasion: a good tax is hard to dodge. Pay-as-you-earn income tax is hard to evade because the employer deducts it before the wage is paid, whereas a tax that depends on traders declaring their own cash takings is easier to escape. Like the four principles, these qualities can pull against one another: a tax with many exemptions to protect the poor may be fairer but harder to collect and easier to evade.

## What taxation does

### Through disposable income

A higher income tax takes more from each pay packet, so disposable income - what households have left to spend or save once deductions are made - falls. Because household spending tends to follow disposable income, shops and services tend to see fewer sales; where the rise is steep, some workers put in fewer hours or move income out of sight into untaxed work, weakening the very revenue the rise sought.

### Through prices

An indirect tax added at the point of sale raises the price buyers face, and the quantity demanded tends to fall. Work invented figures through: fuel duty of N$3 a litre adds N$150 to a 50-litre tank, since 3 x 50 = 150; a Swakopmund tour operator filling daily faces about N$3 300 across a 22-day month. As fares rise to cover the duty, some passengers travel less - the tax steers buying away from fuel while raising revenue for the treasury.

### The limits of taxation

Taxes change choices, and that is their limit. Set high, a duty tends to push buying into untaxed channels - cross-border trips where neighbours levy less, informal stalls, undeclared cash work - so the quantity falls by less than forecast and the revenue raised falls short of it. Drivers in Portugal near the Spanish border, for example, tend to cross to fill up where fuel duty is lower - one reason forecasts of duty revenue are written with care.

### Taxation and the economy as a whole

Beyond single households, firms and markets, taxation shapes the economy as a whole in four ways. Incentives to work and invest: where income tax rates are high, some workers may choose fewer hours or untaxed informal work, and where company tax is high compared with other countries, firms - including foreign firms deciding where to build - may invest less or invest elsewhere, which tends to slow growth. Prices: a rise in an indirect tax such as value-added tax lifts the prices of many goods at once, so it raises the general price level, while a rise in income tax that cuts household spending tends to ease the pressure on prices. The distribution of income: a progressive income tax narrows the gap between high and low incomes after tax, while a heavier reliance on value-added tax tends to widen it, because poorer households spend more of their income. Total spending, output and jobs: a general tax rise takes spending power out of the economy, so sales, output and employment tend to fall, while a tax cut tends to raise them where there is spare capacity. Which effect dominates depends on the tax chosen and the state of the economy: a value-added tax rise in a boom mainly lifts prices, while the same rise in a slump deepens the fall in sales.

## Fiscal policy, defined

### Fiscal policy, defined

Fiscal policy is the use of government spending, taxation and borrowing to influence the economy. It works through the budget: each year the government sets out what it will spend, what it will raise in taxes, and what it will borrow where the two do not match - and each of those three decisions moves the economy. The word itself comes from the state's purse: fiscal questions are budget questions, and a change to any of the three levers counts as a fiscal measure.

### The budget as instrument

The yearly budget is the instrument of the stance: in the Netherlands, budget day each September sets the year's tax and spending plans together, and it is through those plans - not through any single decree - that the fiscal stance changes. A Namibian illustration: a year in which the budget raises the gravel-road programme and lifts the tax-free allowance is a year of changed fiscal policy, whatever else stays the same, and a year in which the two levers are held is a year the stance is unchanged.

### Fiscal against monetary

Fiscal policy is budget policy: spending, taxation and borrowing, decided by the government in the budget. Its counterpart works through the price of borrowing money and the lending of banks - monetary policy, which belongs to topic 4.4 and is named here only to mark the boundary. A finance minister changing a tax rate is acting fiscally; a central bank changing the cost of credit is not, whatever the newspapers call it. Keeping the two apart is a real boundary, and the test is which authority sets the measure: the budget's levers are fiscal, the lenders' price is not.

## The measures of fiscal policy

### Three levers

The measures are changes in the level of government spending, changes in the rates and structure of taxation, and changes in the amount the government borrows. They are set together in the yearly budget: revenue is forecast, spending is planned, and the borrowing is whatever bridges the gap between them. No lever moves alone for long - a spending rise must be financed from revenue, from borrowing, or not at all.

### The expansionary stance

An expansionary stance raises spending or cuts taxes to lift total spending in the economy. Trace it through an invented project: public money committed to the Otavi water scheme lets contracts to builders who hire where workers are idle - and where there is spare capacity, the extra spending tends to raise output and employment rather than prices; near full capacity it tends instead to push prices up. The chain runs from the budget line, to the contractor, to the pay packet, to the town's shops.

### The contractionary stance

A contractionary stance cuts spending or raises taxes to slow total spending in the economy. Trace it: a frozen public wage bill and a higher vehicle duty leave households and firms with less to spend, so buying across the economy tends to cool, easing the pressure on prices - at the cost of weaker sales for firms and, in time, fewer hours of work on offer.

### How fiscal measures move the budget balance

Every fiscal measure moves the budget balance, because the balance is revenue minus spending. Other things being equal, a rise in government spending or a cut in tax rates widens a deficit or shrinks a surplus; a cut in spending or a rise in tax rates narrows a deficit or moves the budget towards surplus. Work an invented budget through. A government starts with a balanced budget: N$70 billion of revenue and N$70 billion of spending. It raises spending on school building by N$3 billion, so spending becomes 70 + 3 = 73, N$73 billion, and it cuts income tax rates, so revenue falls by N$2 billion to 70 - 2 = 68, N$68 billion. The budget now shows a deficit of 73 - 68 = 5, N$5 billion, which tends to be borrowed. Had it instead raised value-added tax to collect N$3 billion more while holding spending at N$70 billion, the result would have been a surplus of N$3 billion. The state of the economy moves the balance too, even with no change in policy. In a downturn, incomes, spending and profits fall, so tax revenue falls, while more people need support, so spending rises: the deficit widens on its own. In a boom the reverse tends to happen. During the world financial crisis of 2008 and 2009 the United Kingdom's budget deficit widened sharply, partly for this reason, as tax receipts fell and support payments rose. Measures also feed back on the balance: where a tax cut raises spending and incomes, part of the lost revenue may return through sales and income taxes, so the deficit may widen by less than the first calculation suggests.

### Reading a stance off a budget

A budget that raises spending on bridge repairs when work is scarce is expansionary; one that trims the public wage bill while prices climb is contractionary. The stance is read off what the budget does with its three levers, not off what the finance minister says about it - and a budget may be expansionary on spending while contractionary on tax, which is why the levers are read together.

## Fiscal policy and the aims

### Onto employment, growth and prices

Where there is spare capacity, an expansionary stance tends to raise output and employment: hired workers spend, and their spending sustains further jobs, so the growth and employment aims move together. As the economy nears full capacity, the same measures tend instead to push the general price level up, so a stance that lowers unemployment adds pressure to prices - the aims begin to move against one another, and the stance cannot serve both at once.

### For whom the effects land

The effects land unevenly. Workers gain as jobs return; firms gain sales where spending rises; savers and households on fixed incomes lose where prices climb, since the same money buys less; and the government's own accounts move with the stance, a tax cut lowering the revenue that funds the very services the aims are pursued through. Naming the party each effect lands on is what turns a stance into an analysis.

### Fiscal policy and the balance of payments

Fiscal policy reaches the balance of payments through spending on imports. A contractionary stance - higher income tax, or cuts in government spending - lowers households' disposable income, so they buy less of everything, imports included. For Namibia, which imports most of its vehicles, fuel and machinery and many of its foods, a large part of any change in spending falls on imports, so a contractionary budget tends to narrow a current account deficit. The cost lands at home: lower spending also means fewer sales for local firms, slower growth and fewer jobs - the conflict between aims set out in topic 4.2. An expansionary stance works the other way: where much of the extra income is spent on imported goods, the current account deficit tends to widen. Fiscal policy can also act on what the country produces over time: government spending on roads, ports and training may lower exporters' costs and raise the quality of what they sell, and a lower company tax may attract foreign firms that produce for export, which tends to strengthen the current account in the longer run, though such effects are slow and uncertain. Greece in the early 2010s is an example from outside Africa: deep cuts in government spending and higher taxes narrowed its large current account deficit, largely because imports fell, while unemployment rose steeply.

### Fiscal policy and the redistribution of income

Fiscal policy is a government's main tool for redistributing income, and it works on both sides of the budget. On the revenue side, a progressive income tax takes a larger share of high incomes than of low ones, so incomes after tax are less unequal than incomes before it. On the spending side, transfers such as the old-age pension and child grants add to the incomes of poorer households, and free or subsidised services such as schools and clinics are worth most, as a share of income, to households with the least. Raising regressive taxes works the other way: a higher value-added tax takes a larger share of a poor household's income, which is why Namibia charges no value-added tax on a list of basic foods such as maize meal and bread. Work invented monthly incomes through: N$3 000 and N$40 000, a gap of N$37 000. A new grant of N$1 400 raises the first to N$4 400; a tax rise of N$1 000 on the second lowers it to N$39 000; the gap narrows to N$34 600. There are limits. Taxes raised steeply on high earners may weaken their incentive to work, invest or start firms, and some may move income out of the country, so the revenue that funds the transfers may fall short of the plan. Whether fiscal policy narrows the gap therefore depends on how progressive the taxes are, how well transfers reach the poorest households, and how strongly high earners respond.

### Borrowing and the future

A deficit-financed programme shifts part of today's cost onto future taxpayers, who meet the interest and repayments from later budgets. Whether that is justified turns on what the borrowed funds buy: a water scheme that raises the district's productive capacity goes some way to repaying itself, a recurring wage bill does not - and on the size of the gap. An invented Vietnamese province that borrowed to build flood defences left later budgets with repayments but left its farms standing; the trade is the same one a Namibian finance minister weighs over an N$8 billion gap.
