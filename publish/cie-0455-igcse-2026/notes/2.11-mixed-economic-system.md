## What a mixed system is

### Sharing the decisions

A mixed economic system is an economic system in which the what, how and for whom questions are answered partly by market forces and partly by government. Private firms, owned by private owners, decide what to produce in response to prices and profits. Government decides a further share of allocation through its budget, its public services and the firms it owns. Neither side runs the whole economy: the market answers for much of daily buying and selling, and the state answers for the services and investments it judges the market handles badly.

### Between market and command

A market economic system leaves allocation to private owners responding to prices. A command system places allocation in the hands of a central authority that sets targets and owns the productive assets. A mixed system combines elements of the two: it keeps private property, prices and profit-seeking firms, while government directs a share of resources itself and steps in where it judges a market outcome to be failing. The balance differs from country to country; one mixed economy may lean towards the market, another towards the state.

### Two mixed economies in practice

Namibia allocates everyday goods through markets: a Katutura bakery decides each morning how many loaves to bake by reading yesterday's sales, minibus owners set fares that fill their seats, and shops in Oshakati stock what their customers ask for. Beside that market half stands the state half: government pays for state schools, state hospitals and the freight railway out of the budget, corrects prices it judges harmful, and owns a share of the country's firms. Germany mixes the same way from the other end: world-scale private firms trade internationally while the state runs the schools, much of the railway and wide rules for banks. What makes both economies mixed is the shared feature - prices answer the allocation questions the state leaves open, and government answers the rest.

## When government steps into a market

### Action to change a market outcome

Government intervention to address market failure is deliberate action by a government to change the outcome a market reaches on its own, taken because that outcome is judged to waste resources or leave needs unmet. Its tools are price limits, indirect taxes, subsidies, regulation, privatisation, nationalisation and direct provision.

### Price limits and their side-effects

A maximum price is a legal ceiling; it binds when set below equilibrium. A minimum price is a legal floor; it binds when set above equilibrium. Take bread held at N$8 against a N$10 market price: buyers want more, bakers offer less, and a shortage appears, with queues and empty shelves. A beef minimum of N$54 against a N$45 market price runs the other way: farmers offer more, buyers take less, and a surplus builds that someone must store or buy. Brazil has used minimum prices to hold up farm incomes, and buys the resulting surplus.

### Drawing a maximum price

A maximum price diagram is built in five steps. Label the vertical axis price (N$ per loaf) and the horizontal axis quantity (loaves per week). Draw demand sloping down and supply sloping up; in the Oshakati bread market they cross at N$10 and 7 500 loaves a week. Draw a horizontal line at N$8, below the equilibrium, and label it maximum price. Neither curve moves. At N$8 read the quantity demanded off the demand curve, 9 000 loaves, and the quantity supplied off the supply curve, 5 000 loaves. The gap between them, 4 000 loaves a week, is the shortage. Shortages tend to bring queues, first-come rationing and black markets in which loaves change hands above N$8. A maximum price set above the equilibrium has no effect, because the market price already sits below it. The same diagram, with different labels, shows a maximum wage in the labour market and a maximum exchange rate in the currency market.

### Drawing a minimum price

A minimum price is drawn the same way, with the line above the equilibrium. In the beef market, price in N$ per kilogram goes on the vertical axis and quantity in tonnes per month on the horizontal; demand and supply cross at N$45. Draw a horizontal line at N$54 and label it minimum price. At N$54 read off the two quantities: buyers want 700 tonnes and farmers offer 1 050 tonnes. The gap, 350 tonnes a month, is the surplus. Farmers who sell receive N$9 a kilogram more, while consumers pay more and buy less. The surplus has to go somewhere: the government may buy and store it, at 350 000 kg x N$54 = N$18 900 000 a month, sell it abroad, or watch it spoil. A minimum price set below the equilibrium has no effect.

### Price limits in the labour market

In the labour market the price is the wage rate, so the diagram has the wage rate (N$ per hour) on the vertical axis and the quantity of labour on the horizontal, with demand for labour sloping down and supply of labour sloping up. A minimum wage is a horizontal line set above the market wage. At that wage employers demand less labour, a contraction along the demand curve, while more people offer to work, an extension along the supply curve. The gap is an excess supply of labour, which is unemployment. Workers who keep their jobs earn more, while some lose work or cannot find it; the more elastic demand for labour is, the larger the job losses. A minimum wage set at or below the market wage changes nothing. A maximum wage is the reverse: a line below the market wage, at which employers want more workers than offer themselves, leaving a shortage of labour and unfilled posts.

### Price limits in the foreign exchange market

In the foreign exchange market the price is the exchange rate, the price of one currency in another, such as US cents per Namibian dollar, on the vertical axis, with the quantity of the currency on the horizontal. A minimum exchange rate is a line above the market rate: more of the currency is offered for sale than buyers want, and the central bank holds the floor by buying the excess with its reserves of foreign currency, which it can keep doing while those reserves last. A maximum exchange rate is a line below the market rate: buyers want more of the currency than is offered, and the central bank holds the ceiling by selling its own currency, adding foreign currency to its reserves. From 2011 to 2015 the Swiss central bank held such a ceiling, selling francs to stop the franc rising against the euro. Closer to home, the Namibian dollar is held at one-for-one with the South African rand, a fixed rate the Bank of Namibia backs with foreign currency reserves.

### A tax on the diagram, in words

Picture the sugary drink market: demand sloping down, supply sloping up, price in N$ per litre against quantity in litres per week, crossing at N$10 and 4 000 litres. An indirect tax of N$2 a litre raises supply costs, so supply shifts left and upward to a curve labelled S1; demand does not move. The new crossing sits at N$11 with 3 500 litres: consumers pay N$1 more a litre, sellers keep N$9 after the tax, and government collects N$2 x 3 500 = N$7 000 a week. Because supply slopes upward, the price rises by less than the N$2 tax. A subsidy works in reverse: paying millers N$5 a bag shifts supply right, tending to lower the price and raise the bags traded.

### A subsidy on the diagram, in words

A subsidy is a payment from the government to producers for each unit they supply, which lowers their costs. Take maize meal: price in N$ per 10 kg bag on the vertical axis and bags per week on the horizontal, with demand and supply crossing at N$100 and 20 000 bags. A subsidy of N$5 a bag shifts supply down and to the right to S1, N$5 below the original curve at every quantity; demand does not move. The new equilibrium, where demand meets S1, sits at N$97 and 21 500 bags. Buyers pay N$3 less, millers receive N$97 + N$5 = N$102 a bag, and the government pays N$5 x 21 500 = N$107 500 a week. Because demand slopes down and supply slopes up, the price tends to fall by less than the subsidy. The subsidy raises consumption of a good the government wants more of, at a cost that has to come from taxes or from other spending given up.

### Rules, ownership and direct supply

Beyond prices, taxes and subsidies, government can act on rules and ownership. Regulation sets binding rules firms must follow, such as taxi safety checks. Direct provision has the government supply the good itself, as with state clinics and schools. Privatisation moves a state firm into private hands, nationalisation the reverse - they change who decides, not just the price.

### Regulation and privatisation: what follows

Regulation sets rules rather than prices: safety standards for minibus taxis, limits on factory smoke, bans on selling alcohol to children, licences to fish. It can cut harm or raise quality without the government buying anything, but complying adds to firms' costs, which may pass into prices, and a rule tends to work as well as it is inspected and enforced, which costs the government money. Privatisation sells a state-owned firm to private owners. The profit motive, and competition where rivals exist, can push the firm to cut waste and improve service, and the sale raises revenue for the government. But a private owner may raise prices, close loss-making services such as rural bus routes, or shed workers, and a privatised firm with no rival may abuse its power unless it is regulated.

### Nationalisation and direct provision: what follows

Nationalisation moves a private firm into state ownership. The state can then run it for social aims - keeping an essential service such as water or electricity running, holding prices within reach of poorer households, protecting jobs - but buying and running it costs public money, and without the profit motive or competition a state firm may be run less efficiently, with its losses covered by taxpayers. Direct provision has the government supply the good itself, free or below cost: state schools, clinics, street lighting, flood defences. It raises consumption of merit goods, supplies public goods the market tends to under-provide, and reaches households who cannot pay. Its costs are the taxes that fund it and the other spending given up, and where demand outruns funding it may bring queues, crowded classrooms and lower quality. The National Health Service in the United Kingdom is a long-standing example of direct provision of health care.

### How well intervention works

Effectiveness is a matter of fit, information and cost. A measure that fits the failure tends to move the outcome towards what the community wants; where government misreads the market, or where policing a rule costs more than it gains, the measure may fall short - a cap below cost may thin the service instead of protecting the buyer.

### When intervention fails

Intervention can fall short of fixing a market's drawbacks. Government failure occurs when an intervention leaves the allocation of resources worse than before. Its usual sources are poor information, as when a maximum price is set without knowing suppliers' costs; side-effects, such as the shortages and black markets a price ceiling may bring or the stored surpluses a price floor leaves; the cost of policing a rule, which can exceed what it saves; and decisions shaped by political pressure rather than by the failure itself. Whether intervention works therefore depends on how well the measure fits the failure, how good the information behind it is, and whether its cost is smaller than the gain. A subsidy on vaccination fits a merit good closely; a price cap on a service whose costs are rising may simply thin the service.
