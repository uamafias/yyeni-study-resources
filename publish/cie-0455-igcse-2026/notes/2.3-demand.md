## What demand means

### Demand, defined

Demand is the quantity of a good that consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price over a period of time. At the Opuwo market, buyers may take 200 bottles of Kaoko Juice in a week at N$10 a bottle: that figure, at that price, over that week, is the quantity demanded. Willingness on its own is not enough. A student who wants a bottle but has N$4 to spend wants the juice yet cannot pay, so that want stays outside demand. Demand backed by the ability to pay is called effective demand.

### Willing and able in practice

Test the definition against three buyers at the juice stall when a bottle costs N$10. Buyer A has N$12 and wants the juice: willing and able, so A demands a bottle. Buyer B has N$12 but prefers the water next door: able, yet not willing, so B adds nothing to demand. Buyer C wants the juice but has N$6: willing, yet not able, so C adds nothing either. A stall in Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam sorts its buyers the same way: a tourist with the money but no wish adds nothing, and a student who cannot meet the day's price adds nothing either.

### Demand is not supply

The confusion learners make: demand and supply are mixed up, because both are quantities and both appear as curves in the same diagram. The boundary: demand is the buying side, the quantities buyers are willing and able to take at each price; supply, developed in topic 2.4, is the selling side, the quantities producers are willing and able to offer. The test: ask which side of the market the quantity belongs to. When demand rises, buyers take more at each price; when supply rises, sellers offer more at each price.

### Connections

Every act of demand is a choice with an opportunity cost, which topic 1.3 develops; the selling side of the market follows in topic 2.4.

## Price and quantity demanded

### The inverse relationship

When the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded tends to fall; when the price falls, the quantity demanded tends to rise. This inverse relationship holds where nothing else changes: a higher price leaves some buyers unable to pay and turns others towards different goods; a lower price draws new buyers in. A firm that writes down the quantity demanded at each price it might charge has drawn up a demand schedule.

### From schedule to curve

Kaoko Juice's Opuwo schedule: at N$15 a bottle the quantity demanded is 40 bottles a week, at N$12 it is 60 bottles, at N$8 it is 90 bottles. Drawn as a diagram, the price of a bottle goes on the vertical axis and the quantity of bottles on the horizontal, and the demand curve slopes downward through the three points. A kiosk in Sao Paulo in Brazil books the same relationship for bottled water. The curve as a whole is demand itself, not any single point on it.

### A movement, not a shift

When the good's own price changes, the market moves along the demand curve. Cut the price from N$12 to N$8 and the market slides down the same curve from 60 to 90 bottles: an extension of demand. A price rise moves it back up: a contraction of demand. Nothing outside the price has changed, so the curve stays put: quantity demanded has changed; demand, the whole relationship, has not.

### Which axis is which

The confusion learners make: price and quantity are mixed up on the two axes. The boundary: price goes on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal, so moving down the curve means a lower price and a larger quantity. The test: label the axes first; a price fall shown as a move up the curve means the axes are wrong.

### Connections

The demand curve is half of the market: topic 2.4 draws the other half, and topic 2.5 joins the two to set the market price.

## From one buyer to the whole market

### One buyer and all buyers

Individual demand is the quantity of a good that one consumer is willing and able to buy at each price. Market demand is the quantity that all the consumers in the market together are willing and able to buy at each price. A single household in Oshakati may demand 4 litres of milk a week at N$10 a litre; the market for milk in Oshakati is the sum of every such household at that price.

### Adding the buyers at each price

Work price by price. At N$10 a litre, household A demands 4 litres a week, household B demands 6 litres, and the rest of Oshakati's households together demand 90 litres. Market demand at N$10 is therefore 100 litres a week. Repeat at every other price and the market demand schedule is complete. In the Netherlands the same rule builds the demand for bicycles in Amsterdam from the demand of each commuter: one city, thousands of individual schedules, added at each price.

### Why the market curve is the one that matters

Because market demand pools every buyer, it is usually larger and steadier than any individual demand: one household may stop buying altogether when its income falls, yet the market total may hold up when other households keep buying. This is why a firm planning production looks at market demand, not at any single customer.

## The conditions of demand

### What shifts the curve

The conditions of demand are the influences other than the good's own price: consumers' incomes; the prices of related goods; tastes and preferences; expectations of future prices; and the number of consumers. A change in a condition shifts the whole demand curve: an increase in demand moves it to the right, meaning more is bought at every price, and a decrease in demand moves it to the left.

### A shift worked through

A travel magazine names the Etosha area the region's top destination, and tastes turn towards holidays there. At N$900 a night, Etosha Gate Lodge faced demand for 40 rooms a weekend; after the article, the same N$900 brings demand for 60 rooms. The room price has not changed, so this is an increase in demand: the curve has shifted right, and more rooms are wanted at every price.

### Substitutes and complements

Related goods work in two opposite ways. Substitutes replace one another: when the price of Oshakati Textiles' school shirts rises, parents tend to switch towards shirts imported from Vietnam, and demand for the imported shirts rises. Complements are used together: petrol and a trip to the coast are consumed as one outing, so a rise in the price of petrol tends to lower demand for weekends at Swakopmund. A bakery in Portugal sees the same with its custard tarts and the pastries next door.

### Expectations and the number of buyers

Two conditions of demand work through timing and headcount rather than through income or taste.

Expectations of future prices. Suppose builders in Oshakati hear that the price of cement is expected to rise by 15% next month. Buying now is cheaper than buying later, so many bring their purchases forward: at today's unchanged price of N$110 a bag, more bags are demanded this month. That is an increase in demand, a rightward shift of the curve, caused by what buyers expect rather than by today's price. The effect also works in reverse: if buyers expect a price to fall, as with a new phone model about to replace the old one, many tend to wait, and current demand decreases. Demand that is pulled forward is often followed by weaker demand once the expected change has happened.

The number of consumers. Market demand adds up the demand of every buyer in the market, so more buyers means more demanded at each price. A town grows when its population rises or when people move in for new work or study; when a new college brings 2 000 students to a town, demand for meals, rooms and transport there increases at every price. The age make-up of the population matters too: a country with more young children demands more school uniforms, while an ageing population, as in Japan, demands more health care. A fall in the number of buyers, as when people leave a town after a mine closes, decreases demand and shifts the curve left.

### Movement or shift?

The test is whether the good's own price changed. A cut in the price of juice slides buyers down an unchanged demand curve - a movement. A rise in consumers' incomes, with the price unchanged, moves the whole curve to the right - a shift. Mixing the two up reverses cause and effect: a shift of demand changes the market price; a price change moves the market along the curve.

### Connections

A shift of demand changes the market price once supply is drawn in, which is topic 2.5's work; how strongly quantity responds to price is topic 2.7.
