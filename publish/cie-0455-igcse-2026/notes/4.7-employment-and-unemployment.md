## What employment and unemployment mean

### Employment and unemployment

Employment means doing paid work: a person of working age who works for pay, or who holds a job and is away from it briefly, is employed. Unemployment means being without paid work while seeking it and able to start it, so a person counted unemployed passes three tests: of working age, without a paid job, and actively seeking one. A person who is not working and not seeking work - a full-time student, a parent at home, a retiree in Swakopmund - is outside the labour force rather than unemployed. The labour force is the employed together with the unemployed, and in Portugal a registered job seeker passes the same three tests, which is why the terms carry one meaning across countries.

### Full employment, not zero unemployment

Full employment does not mean an unemployment rate of zero. It means unemployment has fallen to the level that remains when the economy is working at capacity: people still move between jobs, and job seekers' skills still take weeks to match the posts waiting, so some unemployment remains at the top of a boom. Full employment is therefore read as a low, steady rate rather than an empty register, and a government is likely to claim success where the remaining joblessness is short-term and falling.

### Retirement is not unemployment

Retirement is often mistaken for unemployment. The two are separated by the seeking test. A 66-year-old Otavi worker who leaves the foundry and draws a pension has left the labour force, and no job is sought, so he is not unemployed; a 30-year-old Katutura worker who loses a shop job and visits firms daily is unemployed, because the seeking and the availability are present. Ask of anyone without work: is this person seeking and able to start? Where the answer is yes, the person is unemployed; where it is no, the person is outside the labour force.

## How the pattern and level of employment change

### The pattern and its drift

The pattern of employment is the split of the employed across sectors, occupations and regions, and between public and private, formal and informal work. As an economy develops, the share of workers in primary work - farming, fishing, mining - tends to fall and the share in tertiary work - shops, transport, tourism, offices - tends to rise, because richer households spend a smaller share of income on food and a larger share on services. Two further shifts run with development: changing social attitudes and wider schooling have raised the proportion of women in paid work, in Namibia as in Japan, and a turn towards a market economy tends to shrink the public-sector share as private firms take on more of the hiring, a movement Vietnam's state enterprises lived through.

### Formal work, women and the public sector

Three further shifts run alongside the move from primary to tertiary work. The first is towards the formal economy. Formal work is work in registered firms and public bodies, paid through payrolls, taxed, and covered by labour law and social security; informal work is unregistered - a street vendor in Oshakati, a mechanic fixing cars in a yard, a domestic worker paid in cash - with no written contract or protection. In Namibia a large share of workers are in informal work. As an economy develops, the formal share tends to rise: firms grow and register so that they can borrow from banks, win government contracts and sell to large buyers; more workers have the schooling formal employers ask for; and governments become better at enforcing registration and tax rules. South Korea's move from farms and small workshops into factory and office jobs shows the shift. The second is a greater proportion of women in the labour force. Changing social attitudes about whether women should work outside the home, more girls completing school and college, smaller families, laws against discrimination in hiring and pay, and the growth of service jobs have raised the share of women in paid work, in Namibia as in Japan. Where attitudes change slowly, or childcare is scarce, the share tends to rise more slowly. The third is a fall in the proportion employed in the public sector as a country moves towards a market economy. State-owned firms are sold, reorganised or closed, services once run by ministries are contracted out to private firms, and new private firms take on more of the hiring, so even where public jobs hold steady in number, they make up a smaller share of a growing total. Vietnam lived through this from the late 1980s, as private workshops and foreign-owned factories took on workers while state enterprises shed staff.

### Oshana's numbers

Read a worked example with invented figures. In 2016, of every 100 workers in Oshana region, 46 worked in primary occupations, 20 in secondary and 34 in tertiary; by 2026 the split is 28 primary, 20 secondary and 52 tertiary. The tertiary share rose by 18 workers in every 100 as tourist lodges outside Etosha and shops in Oshakati took people on. The level of employment moved as well: in a strong year of visitor spending the region's jobs grew by 4 000 workers, and in the drought-slow year that followed, 1 200 jobs went as spending weakened - the level riding the cycle while the pattern kept its long drift.

### What moves the level of employment

The level of employment is the number of people in work. It changes for two broad reasons. The first is the demand for workers: when total spending rises, firms need more workers and employment rises, and in a downturn it falls - the cycle seen in Oshana. Over the long run, growth in the economy's output tends to raise employment too, although new machines may let output grow with fewer extra workers. The second is the supply of workers: the labour force grows as the population of working age rises, as more women take paid work and as people migrate to find jobs. Because of this, employment and unemployment can rise together. Work invented figures through. In one year a region's labour force grows from 140 000 to 146 000 and the number employed rises from 120 000 to 124 000. Employment has risen by 124 000 - 120 000 = 4 000, but unemployment has risen too, from 140 000 - 120 000 = 20 000 to 146 000 - 124 000 = 22 000, because the labour force grew by 6 000, faster than the jobs. So a rise in the level of employment is good news for the people who found work, but on its own it does not show that unemployment has fallen.

## How unemployment is measured

### Two measures

Two measures are used. The claimant count records the people registered as seeking work and claiming government support for the unemployed; it comes from benefit records, so it is quick and cheap to gather, and it tends to miss seekers who cannot claim or do not register, among them many informal workers. A labour force survey asks a sample of households about a fixed week: a person is counted unemployed who was without paid work, could start and had sought work recently. Namibia, like Germany, runs a labour force survey, and the survey tends to record more unemployed than the register because it reaches people the benefit records miss.

### The unemployment rate

The unemployment rate expresses unemployment as a percentage of the labour force: unemployment rate = (number unemployed / labour force) x 100. Work it through with invented figures. Oshana's labour force is 200 000 workers, of whom 16 000 are without work and seeking it. Dividing, 16 000 / 200 000 = 0.08, and 0.08 x 100 = 8%, so Oshana's unemployment rate is 8%. The labour force itself is the employed plus the unemployed - those seeking work - so a region of 240 000 residents of working age with 40 000 outside the labour force has the same 200 000 workers in its labour force: 240 000 - 40 000 = 200 000.

### Why the measures differ

Because the claimant count follows the benefit rules and the survey follows the seeking tests, the two often disagree. The survey tends to sit above the register, since seekers without benefits still count there. However, the gap is not fixed: where the rules for claiming tighten, the register falls without a single job being found, so a fall in the claimant count may reflect policy rather than work. Reading either measure, keep its definition in view - the figure is as wide as the net it was gathered with.

## Causes and types of unemployment

### Three types

Frictional unemployment is the short-term joblessness of people moving between jobs: a student leaving college in Windhoek takes weeks to find a first post, and at any moment some people are in that gap, which is why frictional unemployment persists in a well-run economy. Structural unemployment arises when a lasting change removes the need for particular work: machines replace hand tasks, a product is displaced, or the skills workers hold no longer match the skills employers seek. Cyclical unemployment arises when total spending across the economy falls in a downturn: firms sell less, cut output and lay workers off, and the jobs tend to return as spending recovers.

### Structural or cyclical?

Structural job losses are often called cyclical, and the reverse. The boundary is the cause. Cyclical unemployment rides total spending down and up with the cycle; structural unemployment rides a lasting change in products, methods or trade, and survives the recovery. Germany's Ruhr shows the difference sharply: when its steel mills closed as world demand moved on, the job losses were structural - the posts did not return when spending next rose, and retraining and new industries, not waiting, answered them. To tell them apart, ask what caused the loss and whether the jobs return with recovery. A fall in spending points to cyclical; a lasting change points to structural.

### Two closures in Erongo

Two closures in Erongo region tell the types apart. In a year when visitor spending across the economy falls, Swakopmund's Sea Breeze Lodge lays off 30 of its 90 staff - cyclical unemployment, likely to ease when the visitors return. The same year, Walvis Cold Storage installs automated packing lines and lets 60 of its 200 handlers go while demand for its fish is rising - structural unemployment, brought by a lasting change in method, and waiting for a recovery is likely to return none of it. Same region, same season, two different types, and the policy that answers each differs: measures that lift spending for the first, retraining for the second.

## Consequences of unemployment

### Individual, firms, economy

Start with the individual: a jobless welder in Walvis Bay loses the wage that paid for food, school fees and rent, so household income falls and poverty risk rises, and skills fade while work is missed. The effect spreads to firms: households without wages spend less, so shopkeepers and suppliers sell less, and even firms untouched by the layoffs meet weaker demand. For the economy as a whole, output is lost - the goods and services the jobless could have produced go unmade - tax revenue falls while spending on support rises, and skills fading over long spells lower what the economy can produce. One job loss travels: worker to household to high street to treasury, which is why the chain is worth tracing in that order.

### A town that lost its mill

Work it through in one place. Katima Mulilo's sawmill closes and 300 workers lose their pay. Their households cut spending at once, so the town's shops and taxi drivers sell less that same month, and the haulier and fuel station that served the mill lose its orders too. The regional government collects less in tax while support claims rise. In Brazil, whole industrial districts have lived this sequence when plants closed, and the towns that recovered were the ones whose workers could retrain or move. The chain has a direction: the closure caused the unemployment, and the falling spending, lost output and strained budget are what the unemployment then produced.

### Causes given where consequences were asked

Causes are often mixed up with consequences: answers about unemployment's consequences sometimes list what produced the unemployment instead. The direction matters. A cause runs toward unemployment - a fall in spending, a closure, a new machine; a consequence runs away from it - lost income, weaker sales, lost output. To tell them apart, ask of each event named which way the arrow points. An answer about consequences names what follows the job losses; an answer about causes names what came before them, and the two must not be swapped.

## Policies to reduce unemployment

### The range of policies

Policies to reduce unemployment are government measures that raise the number in work, and they work through two doors. The demand-side door lifts total spending: public building projects put job seekers onto sites, and tax cuts leave households and firms with more to spend, so orders reach firms and hiring follows. The supply-side door makes job seekers easier to hire: vocational training teaches the skills employers seek, education raises the skills school leavers bring, and job-placement help speeds the match between vacancy and seeker. Namibia's vocational training centres stand at the supply-side door; Vietnam drew jobs by making hiring easier.

### How a training policy runs

Trace one policy. The government opens a logistics course in Otavi for 400 job seekers. The course teaches the warehouse and driving skills the new depot outside the town is advertising for, so graduates move from the register into posts that exist. Because each graduate earns a wage again, household spending in Otavi rises, and shops take on staff to serve it - a second round of jobs the policy did not pay for directly. Where the skills taught match the vacancies waiting, the chain runs to its end; where they do not, graduates return to the register.

### Which policy, which unemployment

The decisive question is what caused the unemployment. Where the cause is weak spending - a cyclical downturn - measures that lift spending tend to work, and where spare capacity exists, the extra income earned is spent again and further jobs follow; but the jobs last as long as the demand does. Where the cause is missing skills or vanished industries - structural unemployment - training and education tend to work, slowly and at cost, and the jobs they lead to tend to last; but training teaches nothing to a worker idled by a downturn, and spending lifts nothing where the old industry is gone. Speed, cost and the life of the jobs all weigh, so an effective programme mixes the doors.
