## What inflation and deflation mean

### Two directions of the general price level

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level of an economy over a period, usually a year: prices on average rise, and a unit of money buys fewer goods than before. Deflation is the mirror image: a sustained fall in the general price level over a period, where prices on average fall and a unit of money buys more. Both are statements about the general level - the cost of the whole basket of goods and services a typical household buys - and neither describes a single good. A steady 4% yearly rise in prices and the long period of gently falling prices Japan lived through are the two directions of the same measurement.

### A rise in one price is not inflation

The boundary: a single price can rise while the general level is steady, because other prices may hold or fall. Bread becoming dearer at a Katutura market stall after a poor wheat harvest is a rise in one price; it becomes inflation where the rise spreads until the whole basket costs more than before. The test: ask what happened to the general price level, not to one good. A second boundary learners cross: a slower rise in prices is not deflation. Where prices rise 4% a year and then 2% a year, the general level is still rising, more slowly; deflation needs the level itself to fall, as when a price index falls from 120.0 to 117.6 - the level has fallen, not merely its rate of rise.

## Measuring the general price level

### The basket and its weights

The Consumer Prices Index (CPI) measures the general price level by tracking the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services a typical household buys - mahangu meal, bus fares, rent, clothing - with each item weighted by the share of household spending it takes. The index number has no unit of its own: an index of 120.0 means the basket costs 120% of what it cost in the base year. Statistics offices reweight the basket from household spending surveys as habits shift, as the Dutch statistics office does each year for the Netherlands.

### Two rates worked out

The yearly inflation rate is the percentage rise in the CPI over a year. Worked: the CPI moves from 120.0 to 126.0. Step one: 126.0 - 120.0 = 6.0 index points. Step two: 6.0 / 120.0 = 0.05. Step three: 0.05 x 100 = 5, so prices rose 5% that year. A falling index works the same way. The CPI moves from 120.0 to 117.6. The change is 117.6 - 120.0 = -2.4 index points. Then -2.4 / 120.0 = -0.02. So -0.02 x 100 = -2: the general price level fell 2% that year, which is deflation.

### One index, different households

The CPI is one average, and the average conceals a spread. Where food prices rise 12% while clothing rises 2%, a household spending a large share of its income on food faces a faster rise in its cost of living than the index shows, and a household spending little on food faces a slower one. The measured rate depends on the basket: an Oshakati household and a Windhoek professional household share the same index but live different price rises.

## The causes of inflation and deflation

### Demand-pull inflation

Demand-pull inflation arises where total spending in the economy grows faster than the economy's capacity to produce, so buyers compete for scarce goods and sellers raise prices. A lodge region at full capacity shows the mechanism: where visitor numbers surge in an Etosha season and rooms cannot be added at once, room prices are pulled up by the bidding of buyers. Fast growth of wages and credit pulls the same way across an economy, as Brazil saw in years when spending ran far ahead of what producers could supply.

### Cost-push inflation

Cost-push inflation arises where the costs of production rise and firms pass the rise on in higher prices, with the economy's total outlay unchanged. A fuel price rise works this way: haulage becomes dearer, food travels dearer, and shop prices follow. A rise in imported input prices after a currency weakens does the same for an economy that imports much of what it processes. The direction matters: costs rose first and prices followed, where demand-pull has spending rise first.

### The causes of deflation

Deflation has a demand-side cause and a supply-side cause. Demand-side: the economy's whole outlay on goods falls - credit tightens, incomes drop, or households wait for lower prices - so firms cut prices to win the fewer buyers there are; a fall that begins in weak demand risks feeding itself, because buyers who expect lower prices tend to postpone purchases. Supply-side: inputs become cheaper or productivity rises, so firms can charge less without losing profit, and a good harvest that halves maize prices lowers the basket for all. The two causes share the falling index and differ in what happens to output: demand-side deflation tends to drag output and jobs down with it, where supply-side deflation can leave them intact.

## The consequences of inflation and deflation

### Who gains and who loses from inflation

Inflation moves buying power between people: savers holding money lose, because the same sum buys less as prices climb; lenders repaid in fixed money sums tend to lose where the interest does not make up the climb, while borrowers gain the mirror image - a debt fixed in money is repaid in money that buys less than the sum lent. Workers on fixed money wages tend to lose until pay catches up. Firms face dearer inputs, but many can raise their own prices too, so how they fare depends on which rises faster.

### Inflation through the wider economy

Where inflation is high or unexpected, its consequences run beyond redistribution. Firms cannot predict input costs or set prices with confidence, so investment waits, and wage bargaining turns into a chase: prices climb, wages follow, costs climb again. Exports tend to become dearer against rivals' goods where domestic prices climb faster than trading partners' prices - the current account of topic 6.4.

### Deflation's chains

A falling general price level chains into the real economy in two ways. First, buyers expect lower prices later, so they postpone purchases; firms see sales fall, cut output and lay off workers; the workers who lose income spend less, so prices fall again. Second, debts are fixed in money, and each unit of money now buys more, so the real burden of each loan grows as the price level falls - households and firms cut spending to service debts, deepening the fall.

### Which is harder to manage

A low, steady rise in the general price level tends to be easier to manage than falling prices: wages and debts adjust more smoothly where the price level edges up gently, and the chains above stay quiet. Deflation, where it takes hold, risks the self-feeding spiral of postponed spending - the reason the price stability aim of topic 4.2 is set at a low, steady rise rather than zero.

## Policies to control inflation and deflation

### The range of policies

Against inflation a government and its central bank hold three routes. Monetary policy: raising interest rates makes borrowing dearer and saving better rewarded, slowing the growth of total spending. Fiscal policy: cutting government spending or raising taxes removes spending from the economy directly. Supply-side measures that raise capacity or ease input costs - infrastructure, training, cheaper logistics - work on cost pressure where spending restraint cannot. Against deflation the routes run in reverse: interest rates are cut and government spending climbs, aimed at restarting total spending before the chains of the last section take hold.

### A rate rise against demand-pull pressure

Suppose the central bank lifts its policy rate from 5% to 9% against a 6% yearly climb in prices driven by fast credit growth. Loans become dearer, so households borrow less and firms postpone loan-financed expansion, and saving is better rewarded, so the economy's whole outlay grows more slowly. With sellers no longer able to count on buyers bidding against each other, the demand-pull pressure eases. Consumers face gentler price moves and savers gain income, while borrowers meet dearer credit and firms weaker sales while the policy works.

### How effective each route is

Effectiveness depends on the cause of the climb. Where outlay runs ahead of capacity, the rate rise is well aimed and works through the whole banking system at once. Where costs push prices up - a fuel rise, a poor harvest - dearer borrowing squeezes output and jobs without touching the cause, and supply-side measures or waiting out the input price do more. The effects also arrive with a delay, and deflation is harder still: households expecting lower prices may keep postponing even with cheap credit, where confidence is low. Each route trades against other aims - slower growth and fewer jobs while prices come down, or higher public borrowing where the government spends against deflation.

### Fighting deflation, and how well it works

Against deflation the routes run in reverse, and how well they work depends on the cause and on confidence. Where deflation comes from the demand side, the central bank cuts interest rates, so borrowing is cheaper and saving pays less, encouraging households and firms to spend now; and the government raises its own spending or cuts taxes, adding orders directly or leaving households more income to spend. Because a demand-side fall leaves factories idle and workers without jobs, there is spare capacity, so extra spending tends to raise output and employment, and rising incomes add further spending - the reverse of the chain in the last section. The limits are real. Where households expect prices to keep falling, they may keep postponing purchases even when credit is cheap. Once interest rates are close to zero there is little room to cut further. And extra government spending adds to public borrowing that later taxpayers repay. Japan shows the difficulty: prices drifted down for long stretches from the late 1990s even though its interest rates were held close to zero. Where deflation comes from the supply side - cheaper inputs or higher productivity, with output holding - there may be little to cure, because jobs and output are not falling, and a government may choose to leave it alone.
