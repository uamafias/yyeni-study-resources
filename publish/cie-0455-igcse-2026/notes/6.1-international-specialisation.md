## What national specialisation means

### The definition and its basis

Specialisation at a national level is where a country concentrates its resources on producing a narrow range of goods and services, and trades the surplus for the other goods its people want. The basis, in broad terms, is superior resource allocation and cheaper production methods: a country tends to specialise where its land, climate, workers' skills or equipment let it produce at a lower cost per unit than its trading partners. Namibia's fish processing at Walvis Bay grows from a resource the country holds in strength; Vietnam's highland coffee grows where the crop suits the land. Each country puts its resources where they produce most.

### How the basis works

Start from the country's resources. Where a country holds a resource in strength - a deep-water port, a dry climate, skilled workers - production built on that resource tends to need fewer inputs per unit, so the cost per unit tends to fall. Because the country can produce these goods cheaply, land, labour and capital move towards them and away from goods that other countries produce more cheaply. The surplus is then traded: what the country makes best is exchanged for what it has decided not to make, and the total available to its consumers tends to rise.

### The idea in figures

A processing line at Walvis Bay with 60 workers packs 9 600 cartons of fish a day, 160 cartons for each worker; a general food workshop with the same 60 workers packs 4 200 cartons, 70 cartons each. The port-side line's cost per carton sits lower because its workers produce more in a day. In Vietnam, highland farmers harvest 2.4 tonnes of coffee a hectare where lowland farms harvest 1.5 tonnes, so the highlands give more coffee from each hectare. Each country produces where its resources work hardest and trades for the rest.

## Advantages and disadvantages at a national level

### Who gains what

The advantages land on three parties. Consumers gain variety and price: goods the country does not make arrive through trade and tend to cost less than home production at a higher cost per unit. Firms gain scale: selling abroad lets a specialist firm produce larger quantities, which lowers the cost per unit further - flower growers in the Netherlands sell far beyond their home market. The economy gains export revenue, jobs and skills in the specialised sector.

### The risk of a narrow base

The disadvantages grow from how narrow the specialisation is. Where a large share of export revenue rests on a single product, a fall in its world price cuts earnings sharply. Take a country earning N$4 000 million from exports, with N$3 200 million of it from one crop: a fall of a fifth in the crop's price takes the revenue down to N$2 560 million, with the tonnage unchanged. Workers in the sector feel the change first, and the economy has less for imports.

### Disadvantages for consumers and firms

The risk of a narrow base falls on the economy as a whole, but specialisation also carries disadvantages for consumers and for firms.

Consumers: a country that specialises buys many of its goods from abroad, so its consumers depend on imports. When supply is disrupted - by a war, a drought in the exporting country, a port closure, a shipping delay or an export ban - goods may run short and prices rise. In 2022 the war in Ukraine, a large exporter of wheat and sunflower oil, pushed up the price of bread flour and cooking oil in many importing countries, southern Africa included. A fall in the exchange rate also makes imported goods dearer at once. And where home producers have closed, consumers lose the choice of goods made locally.

Firms: specialised exporters sell into world markets they do not control. When world demand falls or a buyer country raises a tariff, their sales and prices tend to fall together, as a Walvis Bay fish processor finds when European demand weakens. Firms that rely on imported inputs - machinery, fuel, packaging - face higher costs or delays when supplies are disrupted or the currency falls. Firms making goods the country no longer specialises in face foreign rivals producing at a lower cost per unit, and some may be forced to close, taking jobs with them.

### General or the context

The confusion learners make: a question set in a context is answered with the advantages of specialisation in general, without touching the information provided. The boundary: the general advantages name what tends to happen where a country specialises; the context asks what those tendencies mean for the economy in the extract, with its figures and products. The test: each point should use something the extract states - a figure, a firm, a product. Where a point could sit under any question about specialisation, it needs the extract's evidence first.

### How the balance is struck

How far the advantages outweigh the disadvantages tends to turn on the spread of the exports and the ease of shifting resources. Revenue from several products carries a smaller risk than revenue resting on a single crop, because a price fall in one product is then a smaller share of the whole. Where workers and equipment can move, a lasting fall in demand costs less.
