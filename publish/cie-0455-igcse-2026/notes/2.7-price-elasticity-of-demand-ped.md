## What price elasticity of demand means

### Responsiveness, measured

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how strongly the quantity demanded of a good responds to a change in its own price. Topic 2.3 established that a lower price tends to bring a larger quantity demanded; PED puts a number on that tendency. Two markets can obey the same law and behave very differently: when fuel prices rise, drivers in Windhoek may cut back little, while a rise in the price of marula juice may empty a stall in Oshakati. Three parts of the definition do the work. It is about the good's own price, so a substitute's price change sits outside the measure. It works with quantities demanded at prices, the relationship topic 2.3 drew as a demand curve. And it measures degree, not a yes or no: every demand curve responds somehow, and PED says how much.

### Not the same measure as supply's

The confusion learners make: price elasticity of demand and price elasticity of supply are mixed up, because both attach to a price change. The boundary: the demand version measures how buyers respond - the change in quantity demanded when the good's own price changes. The supply version, taught in topic 2.8, measures how sellers respond - the change in quantity supplied. The test: ask which side of the market the quantity belongs to. Petrol stations cutting opening hours after a price fall is a supply response; households buying fewer litres is a demand response.

### One question, two answers

Put the definition to work. A bus fare between Katutura and the city centre rises from N$10 to N$12: some commuters walk, and PED will measure how far quantity demanded fell, as a percentage. Household electricity in Swakopmund rises by a similar percentage: few households can light their homes another way, so quantity demanded falls by less. "Demand falls" is the beginning of an answer; how far it falls is the rest. Sellers setting prices and governments setting taxes both need that second number.

## Calculating PED and reading it off the curve

### The formula and its sign

PED equals the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. The raw answer comes out negative, because the law of demand makes the two changes run opposite ways; it is stated as an absolute value, the sign dropped by convention, and the size compared with 1. Worked case: Kaoko Juice sells 200 bottles a week at N$10 in Opuwo, raises the price to N$12, and sales fall to 150 bottles. The percentage change in quantity demanded is negative 25 per cent, since 50 / 200 x 100 = 25; the percentage change in price is positive 20 per cent, since 2 / 10 x 100 = 20. PED is 25 / 20 = 1.25, so demand is price-elastic: quantity demanded moved further, in percentage terms, than the price did.

### An inelastic case, worked

A minibus operator in Ruacana charges N$40 a trip and carries 500 passengers a month. The fare rises to N$60 and passengers fall to 460. Quantity demanded fell by 40, since 500 - 460 = 40, or 8 per cent of 500; price rose by N$20, or 50 per cent of N$40. PED is 8 / 50 = 0.16, and because 0.16 lies between 0 and 1 demand is price-inelastic: passengers cut back far less than the fare rose, in percentage terms.

### Reading elasticity off the diagram

On a demand diagram - price on the vertical axis, quantity demanded on the horizontal - elasticity is seen by comparing how far a point moves along each axis. A steeply drawn curve shows quantity demanded moving little for a given price move: inelastic demand, like the Ruacana trips. A flatter curve shows it moving far: elastic demand, like the juice. Steepness is judged in relation to the scales chosen, so the axis movement is the safer guide than the look of the line. And the whole range matters: a curve can hold an elastic stretch near high prices and an inelastic stretch near low ones.

### Three special values: 0, 1 and infinity

Three values of PED have names of their own, and each has its own curve shape.

Perfectly inelastic demand, PED = 0. Quantity demanded does not change at all when the price changes, so the percentage change in quantity is zero and so is PED. The demand curve is a vertical line at that quantity: whatever the price, the same amount is bought. Few real goods come close, but a hospital that needs a fixed number of doses of a life-saving drug each week, whatever they cost, is near this case. Every rise in price then raises spending on the drug by the full percentage of the price rise, because no dose is dropped.

Perfectly elastic demand, PED = infinity. At one price buyers will take any quantity offered, but at any higher price quantity demanded falls to zero, because buyers can get an identical product elsewhere at that price. The demand curve is a horizontal line at that price. A grower near Grootfontein selling maize to a national mill that pays every grower N$5 000 a tonne is close to this case: he can sell as many tonnes as he grows at N$5 000, but if he asks N$5 100 the mill takes none of his maize.

Unit elasticity, PED = 1. The percentage change in quantity demanded equals the percentage change in price, so the two offset each other. The demand curve is drawn as a smooth curve that bends towards the origin, along which price multiplied by quantity is the same at every point: N$20 x 100 units, N$25 x 80 units and N$40 x 50 units each give N$2 000. The percentage formula, which measures each change from its own starting value, gives exactly 1 only for very small moves along this curve. Section 2.7.4 shows what unit elasticity means for spending.

These three mark out the scale. Between 0 and 1 demand is inelastic; above 1 it is elastic; and the vertical and horizontal lines are the two extremes between which real demand curves lie.

## What makes demand elastic or inelastic

### Five determinants, one channel

The main determinants of PED are the availability and closeness of substitute goods, the proportion of income the purchase takes, whether the good is a necessity or a luxury, the width of the market defined, and the length of time since the price changed. Each works through the same channel: it decides how easily a buyer can leave the good behind.

### How each determinant works

Substitutes: where close alternatives are easy to switch to, a price rise tends to push buyers towards them, so demand is price-elastic - one brand of cooking oil in a Windhoek supermarket competes with others on the same shelf. Share of income: a good taking a small slice of a budget, like salt or matches, tends to carry inelastic demand, because even a large percentage rise changes the weekly bill little; an Etosha Gate lodge stay takes a large slice, so demand there tends to respond sharply to its price. Necessity against luxury: a necessity, like the medicine a clinic in Gobabis dispenses, is bought even at a higher price - inelastic; a luxury can be postponed - elastic. Width of market: demand for "fruit juice in Oshakati" is more elastic than demand for "fruit", because narrowing the market stacks substitutes beside it. Time: after a price change, habits and contracts hold buyers where they are, so demand looks inelastic at first and grows more responsive as people adjust.

### The number is not the explanation

The confusion learners make: what PED was and what determined it are treated as the same question. The boundary: the calculation of PED is arithmetic on two percentage changes; the determinants are the features of the product and its market that explain why the arithmetic came out at that size. The test: ask whether the sentence names a number or a market feature. "PED is 1.25" answers what it was; "demand is elastic because three substitute brands sit on the same shelf" answers what made it so.

## PED, total spending and revenue

### Spending, revenue, and the two rules

Total spending by consumers on a good equals the price multiplied by the quantity sold - the seller's revenue from the good. Elasticity decides which way the total moves when price moves. Where demand is price-inelastic, a price rise tends to raise total spending, because quantity demanded falls by a smaller percentage than the price rose, so the bigger price wins. Where demand is price-elastic, the same rise tends to lower it, because quantity demanded falls by a larger percentage, so the lost customers win. A price cut runs the rules in mirror.

### One price rise, two totals

The inelastic case from section 2.7.2: the Ruacana minibus at N$40 carrying 500 passengers earned spending of N$20 000 a month, since 40 x 500 = 20000. At N$60 with 460 passengers, spending is N$27 600, since 60 x 460 = 27600 - up N$7 600, because the 8 per cent fall in passengers was smaller than the 50 per cent rise. The elastic case: Kaoko Juice at N$10 sold 200 bottles, spending N$2 000, since 10 x 200 = 2000; at N$12 with 150 bottles it is N$1 800, since 12 x 150 = 1800, a fall of N$200. On the diagram, each total is the rectangle from the origin to that point on the curve, height the price and base the quantity: elasticity decides which rectangle is larger.

### Price cuts, and the unit-elastic case

A price cut runs the two rules in mirror. Where demand is price-elastic, a cut tends to raise total spending, because quantity demanded rises by a larger percentage than the price fell. Where demand is price-inelastic, a cut tends to lower total spending, because quantity demanded rises by a smaller percentage than the price fell, so the lower price on every unit outweighs the few extra units sold.

Worked case, inelastic: a bakery in Ondangwa cuts its loaf from N$14 to N$12 and sales rise from 1 000 to 1 050 loaves a week. The price fell by 2 / 14 x 100 = 14.3 per cent; quantity rose by 50 / 1 000 x 100 = 5 per cent; PED = 5 / 14.3 = 0.35, so demand is inelastic. Spending falls from N$14 x 1 000 = N$14 000 to N$12 x 1 050 = N$12 600, a loss of N$1 400 a week. Worked case, elastic: a guesthouse in Keetmanshoop cuts its room rate from N$600 to N$540 a night, 10 per cent, and bookings rise from 100 to 130 nights a month, 30 per cent; PED = 30 / 10 = 3. Spending rises from N$60 000 to N$540 x 130 = N$70 200, a gain of N$10 200.

Unit elasticity sits between the two. Where PED is exactly 1, the change in quantity offsets the change in price, so total spending is unchanged whichever way the price moves; on the diagram, the rectangle under the old point and the rectangle under the new point have the same area. A seller facing unit-elastic demand gains no revenue from changing its price, a seller facing inelastic demand tends to gain from a rise and lose from a cut, and a seller facing elastic demand tends to do the opposite.

### Revenue is not profit, and one market is not the whole economy

Two confusions travel here. Revenue against profit: revenue is the money coming in from sales, profit what remains after the costs of production are paid, so a revenue rise settles nothing about profit until costs are known. One product against total demand: this elasticity is the responsiveness of buyers of one good, one market at a time. A total is not an average: total spending is price times quantity, an average divides a total by a count. The test for each: name what the figure adds up, and over what.

## Why PED matters to sellers and government

### What each decision-maker does with the figure

A producer setting a price is likely to weigh PED, because the effect of a price change on its revenue depends on whether demand for its product is elastic or inelastic: with inelastic demand a rise tends to bring more revenue, with elastic demand the same rise tends to shrink it. That is why the Ruacana operator could raise its fare and collect more, while the juice stall in Opuwo lost N$200 by trying. A government choosing an indirect tax is likely to weigh PED for the same reason: a tax tends to raise more revenue where demand is price-inelastic, because quantity demanded falls little when the tax pushes the price up. Where a government wants a tax to change behaviour rather than raise money, it is likely to pick goods whose demand is price-elastic, because the quantity bought responds to the price rise the tax causes. Consumers read the figure from the other side, as the next section shows.

### What PED means for consumers

Consumers use PED too, even without calculating it. Where demand for a good is price-inelastic - water, electricity, bus fares to work, basic medicines - a price rise leaves households little room to escape: they keep buying close to the same quantity, so they spend more on the good and have less left for everything else. A household in Katutura spending N$600 a month on electricity pays about N$720 if the price rises by 20 per cent and its use hardly changes, and the extra N$120 has to come out of food, clothing or saving. The squeeze tends to be hardest on low-income households, for whom such necessities take a larger share of income.

Where demand is price-elastic, as for soft drinks or one brand of cereal, consumers protect their budgets by switching to substitutes or going without, which is also why sellers of such goods hesitate to raise prices. PED shapes who pays an indirect tax as well: the more inelastic the demand for a good, the more of the tax tends to be passed on to consumers through a higher price, because they keep buying, while consumers of goods with elastic demand avoid much of it by switching. A household planning its budget can therefore expect price rises on necessities to bite, and can look for substitutes where they exist. Shoppers in the United Kingdom showed both responses when energy and food prices rose sharply in 2022: they paid the higher energy bills they could not avoid, and switched to cheaper own-brand groceries where they could.

### How far the figure can be trusted

PED is usually estimated from past market data, so decisions that lean on it carry the risk that the elasticity measured yesterday drifts as substitutes, incomes and tastes change: the Ruacana operator's 0.16 says little about a year in which a new road brings a bus service to the falls. A seller that raises price on the strength of an inelastic figure may find the estimate was taken before buyers adjusted, and the time determinant means responsiveness tends to grow as buyers find their alternatives.

### The one sentence to carry into the exam

PED measures how strongly buyers respond to a price change; the determinants say why the response is that size; the spending rules say what the response does to revenue; and producers and governments act on the number, at the risk that it was measured in a market that has since moved.
