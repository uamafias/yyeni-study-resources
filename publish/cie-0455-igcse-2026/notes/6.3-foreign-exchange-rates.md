## The price of one money in terms of another

### The definition

The foreign exchange rate is the price of one currency expressed in terms of another: the number of units of one currency needed to buy one unit of the other. A bank screen in Windhoek quoting N$18.50 per US$1 states that one US dollar costs 18.50 Namibian dollars. A quotation names two currencies and a direction: the same rate read the other way round states how many US dollars one Namibian dollar buys, and the two readings are reciprocals of each other. A quotation also belongs to a moment, because the price moves as the market behind it moves.

### One rate, two price tags

Follow the rate into two price tags. A Katutura electronics shop imports a music system priced US$400: at N$18.50 per US$1 the shop pays 400 x 18.50 = N$7 400, so the home price of an import depends on the rate as well as on the foreign price. In the other direction, a German buyer of Namibian dried fruit converts the Namibian-dollar price into euros with the same rate, so one quoted price reaches two currencies through the exchange rate. When the rate moves, both price tags change although neither seller changed the quoted price.

### Not a characteristic, not a function

Answers on this objective sometimes offer the characteristics of money - durable, portable, acceptable - or its functions - a medium of exchange, a store of value. The boundary: a characteristic is a property money has, a function is a use money serves, and the exchange rate is a price between two currencies, how much of one the other buys. The test: ask whether the statement names a price between two currencies or a property or a use of money itself; the first is an exchange rate, the second and third belong to the topic on money.

## How the market sets the rate

### Demand, supply and equilibrium

Demand for the Namibian dollar comes from foreign buyers who need it to pay for Namibian exports, from visitors planning to spend in Namibia and from investors who wish to hold assets in the currency. Supply of the Namibian dollar comes from residents who sell it to obtain foreign currency for imports or assets abroad. The equilibrium foreign exchange rate is the rate at which the quantity of the currency demanded equals the quantity supplied; at that rate, every Namibian dollar offered finds a buyer.

### A week in the market for US dollars

Picture the Windhoek market for US dollars, with the price of one US dollar in Namibian dollars on the stall. At N$16 per US$1, importers want 4 million US dollars while sellers offer 6 million, so the excess of 2 million US dollars tends to push the price of the dollar down. At N$14 per US$1, importers want 7 million while sellers offer 3 million, so the shortage tends to push the price up. At N$15 per US$1 the two quantities match at 5 million US dollars, and the rate settles. The same balancing that sets the price of mangoes at the Oshakati market in topic 2.5 sets the price of a currency.

### Showing the currency market on a diagram

A currency market is drawn like any other market, with its own labels. Take the South African rand, which floats, priced in US dollars.

The axes: the vertical axis shows the exchange rate - the price of one rand in US dollars (US$ per rand); the horizontal axis shows the quantity of rand traded per period, such as billions of rand a week.

The curves: the demand curve for rand, D, slopes down from left to right. At a lower price of the rand, South African goods, holidays and assets are cheaper for foreign buyers, so they want more rand to pay for them. The supply curve of rand, S, slopes up: at a higher price of the rand, foreign goods are cheaper for South Africans, so they buy more imports and sell more rand to pay for them. Supply comes from South African importers, travellers, investors buying assets abroad and people sending money abroad.

The equilibrium: where D and S cross, the quantity of rand demanded equals the quantity supplied. Mark that rate on the vertical axis - say US$0.055 per rand - and the quantity on the horizontal axis.

A change in demand: if foreign demand for South African exports rises, D shifts right to D1. At the old rate there is now excess demand for rand, so the rate rises to a new equilibrium where D1 crosses S - say US$0.060 per rand, with more rand traded. The rand has appreciated.

A change in supply: if South Africans import more, they sell more rand, so S shifts right to S1. At the old rate there is excess supply, so the rate falls to a new equilibrium where D crosses S1, and the rand has depreciated.

Write the point beside the diagram: which curve shifted, why, which way the rate moved and what that means. Because the Namibian dollar is held at one rand, what the diagram shows for the rand against the US dollar holds for the Namibian dollar too.

### Rise, fall and the two names

A rise in the foreign exchange rate and a fall are sometimes confused, and the confusion costs marks. A rise in the Namibian dollar's rate against the euro means one Namibian dollar buys more euros: the currency has appreciated. A fall means one Namibian dollar buys less foreign currency: it has depreciated. The boundary is which currency the quotation prices: more Namibian dollars per euro means the Namibian dollar weakened, not strengthened. The test: ask what happens to the amount of foreign currency one unit of the home currency buys - more is appreciation, less is depreciation, whatever direction the quotation is stated in.

## Why the rate moves

### Four causes

Four causes drive most fluctuations. Changes in the demand for exports and imports: foreign buyers must obtain the currency to pay for exports, adding to demand for it, while home importers sell it to pay for imports, adding to its supply, so trade volumes move the currency market from both sides. Changes in the rate of interest: deposits pay better or worse than abroad, and funds tend to follow the better return. Speculation: traders act on expectations of where the rate is heading. Entry or departure of a multinational corporation: investment flows move demand for the host currency.

### Two causes traced

Trace a rise in overseas demand, using Zambia, whose currency, the kwacha, floats: a German tour operator books a lodge near Livingstone and must pay in kwacha; because the operator buys kwacha to pay, demand for the currency rises, tending to push its price in euros up - an appreciation. Trace an interest change: Zambia's central bank raises its rate of interest from 6% to 9% with rates abroad unchanged; because kwacha deposits now pay more, funds are likely to flow in and demand rises with them. A decrease in the rate of interest runs the chain in reverse. The Namibian dollar is held at one rand (section 6.3.5), so these forces reach it through the rand: it rises and falls against the euro as the rand does.

### Changes in demand for imports

Changes in demand for imports work through the supply side of the currency market. When South African firms and households buy more imported cars, machinery and fuel, the foreign sellers want paying in their own currencies - euros, US dollars, yuan. So importers sell rand to buy those currencies, and the supply of rand rises. With more rand offered at each rate, the rate tends to fall: the rand depreciates. A fall in demand for imports reduces the supply of rand and tends to push the rate up.

Figures invented for teaching: suppose South Africa's car dealers order an extra 10 000 vehicles from Germany at 30 000 euros each. They must buy 10 000 x 30 000 = 300 000 000 euros, selling rand to do so. Because the Namibian dollar is held at one rand, a weaker rand means a weaker Namibian dollar against the euro too, so Namibian importers of European goods pay more, even though the extra imports were South Africa's.

### Speculation and multinationals

Speculation acts on expectations: a trader expecting the kwacha to strengthen buys it now, adding present demand and tending to push the rate the way guessed; an expected fall adds present supply instead. A multinational's entry works like an investment flow: a Germany-based retailer opening outlets in Lusaka converts foreign funds into kwacha, and its departure removes that demand.

### Levels and changes

Two slips appear often. One treats an increase and a decrease in the rate of interest as if both moved the exchange rate the same way; the direction must be carried through the chain. The other writes a low inflation rate where a fall in the rate of inflation is meant: a low inflation rate is a level, prices rising slowly, whereas a fall in the rate of inflation is a change, prices rising more slowly than before. The test: does the sentence name a level or a change, and does the cause push currency demand the way the answer claims?

## What a move in the rate does

### Prices first

The first consequence of a rate movement lands on prices. A depreciation of the Namibian dollar means each unit of foreign currency costs more Namibian dollars, so imported goods and inputs tend to cost more at home even though the foreign seller has not changed the quoted price, and a given Namibian-dollar export price converts into fewer foreign-currency units, so exports tend to become cheaper abroad. An appreciation works in the opposite direction on both sides: imports cheaper at home, exports dearer abroad. Exporters, importers and consumers feel the change before anything else in the economy does.

### The figures for one depreciation

Let the Namibian dollar, moving with the rand, depreciate against the euro from N$19 per euro to N$21 per euro. A drum of imported dye priced 20 euros costs Oshakati Textiles 20 x 19 = N$380 before the move and 20 x 21 = N$420 after it. In the other direction, a crate of dried fruit priced N$399 costs a Portuguese buyer 21.00 euros before and 19.00 euros after. Neither seller changed a quoted price; the rate alone moved both price tags. Households buying imported goods face the same shift the textile firm faces on its inputs.

### From prices to spending

Whether spending moves with prices depends on the price elasticity of demand, met in topic 2.7. Where demand for the exports is price-elastic, the cheaper foreign price brings a large enough rise in quantities to raise export earnings. Where demand for imports is price-inelastic, as with engines that have no close home substitute, the dearer imports raise import spending rather than reduce it. The trade balance therefore tends to improve after a depreciation where the demand for exports and imports is price-elastic enough to respond and where supply in the economy can respond to the larger orders. Where these conditions fail, dearer imports can outweigh the export gain.

## Floating and fixed systems

### Two ways to set a rate

A floating exchange rate is determined by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market: the authority holds it at no level, and it moves whenever either side of the market shifts. A fixed exchange rate is held by the monetary authority at a chosen level: the authority stands ready to buy and sell the currency from its reserves to keep the rate there. The boundary is who sets the rate - the market or the authority. The test: watch what happens when export demand shifts; a rate that moves with market conditions floats, and a rate that stays at its announced level is being held fixed.

### Namibia's parity and the euro

The Namibian dollar is pegged one-for-one with the South African rand: it has stood at one rand per Namibian dollar through years in which demand and supply for Namibian exports have swung, which is the behaviour of a fixed arrangement. A Windhoek importer paying a South African supplier knows in advance what each Namibian dollar will fetch, whatever the market behind it does. Pegged to the rand does not mean fixed against every currency: the rand floats, so the Namibian dollar rises and falls against the euro and the US dollar as the rand does. By contrast, Germany, Portugal and the Netherlands share the euro, whose rate against the US dollar moves with the market: the euro area floats against outside currencies, and its traders live with those movements.

### What each system offers

A floating rate adjusts by itself: when export demand falls, the currency tends to ease, which lends exporters a hand without the authority spending anything, but the rate can swing sharply, and a trader pricing next quarter's order faces that uncertainty. A fixed rate gives traders certainty about conversion prices, which helps firms commit to contracts across borders, but holding the level costs reserves, and a rate held out of line with market conditions may keep imports cheap or exports dear for longer than the market would allow. Which system serves an economy better tends to depend on how much the economy trades, how large its reserves are, and how steady its trade flows are.
