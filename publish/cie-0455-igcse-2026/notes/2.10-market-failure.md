# 2.10 Market failure

**What this topic asks of you**

- Define market failure, and settle the key terms around it: public good, merit good, demerit good, private, external and social costs, and private, external and social benefits.
- Identify the causes of market failure - public goods, merit and demerit goods, external costs, external benefits, the abuse of monopoly power, and factor immobility - and analyse how each leaves the market at the wrong level of output.
- Analyse the consequences of market failure: the over-consumption of demerit goods and goods with external costs, and the under-consumption of merit goods and goods with external benefits, each traced through to the parties it lands on.

## What market failure means

### Market failure, defined

Market failure is the failure of the price mechanism, left to itself, to allocate resources in the way that best serves society: some goods are produced at levels above what their full effects on others justify, and others at levels below what those effects warrant. A lighthouse on the coast of Portugal shows the idea: ships within sight of the beam use it whatever they paid, so the light's benefits never reach the price, and private builders tend to put up too few.

### Private, external and social costs

A private cost is a cost the decision-maker pays directly: the diesel a Windhoek bus operator burns is a private cost. An external cost is imposed on people outside the transaction and goes unpaid by them: the exhaust pedestrians breathe is a cost the operator never settles. A social cost is the two together, the full cost of the activity to society. The benefits mirror this: a private benefit lands on the buyer, an external benefit on people outside the transaction, and a social benefit on both combined.

### Putting costs and benefits into figures

Social cost and social benefit are sums, so they can be put into figures. Kalahari Coaches of Windhoek pays N$9 000 a day for diesel and drivers' wages: that is its private cost. Its fumes and noise cost roadside traders and pedestrians an estimated N$1 500 a day in cleaning, lost trade and clinic visits: that is the external cost. The social cost is the two added together: N$9 000 + N$1 500 = N$10 500 a day. Benefits add up the same way. A Keetmanshoop household that connects its yard to the town sewer gains a private benefit it values at N$3 000 a year: no pit to empty and a cleaner yard. Its neighbours gain an external benefit of about N$1 200 a year from fewer flies, less smell and a lower risk of disease. The social benefit is N$3 000 + N$1 200 = N$4 200 a year. Keep the words apart: an external cost is the part that falls on others, while a social cost is the whole, private plus external; the same holds for benefits.

### Public goods, merit goods, demerit goods

A public good has two features. Its benefits cannot be confined to those who pay - street lighting over Katutura's taxi ranks lights the non-payer as surely as the payer - and one person's use of it leaves as much for the next. A merit good, such as schooling or vaccination, is one whose benefits consumers tend to undervalue, so too little is bought. A demerit good, such as tobacco or strong drink, is one whose harm consumers tend to undercount, so too much is bought.

### Market failure is not business failure

The confusion learners make: market failure sounds like a firm going under. The boundary: business failure is one firm closing because it cannot cover its costs; market failure is a whole market settling at the wrong level of output, even while its firms trade on. The test: ask whether the statement is about a single firm's survival or a whole market's level of output. When Etosha Gate Lodge shuts its gates after two quiet seasons, that is business failure; when too little is spent on Walvis Bay's flood defences, that is market failure.

## Why markets fail

### Six causes, one thread

The causes share one thread: some cost or benefit never reaches the price. Public goods fail because non-payers cannot be excluded. Merit goods are under-consumed because consumers undervalue them; demerit goods over-consumed because consumers undercount their harm. Goods with external costs are over-produced because the damage to others stays outside the price; goods with external benefits are under-produced because the gains to others never raise demand. A dominant seller may hold output back to keep prices up. Immobile workers and capital cannot reach the uses that need them.

### A cost imposed on others

Otavi Charcoal Works earns N$40 000 a season burning wood waste. Its smoke drifts over 60 homesteads, and each spends N$250 a season on cleaning and clinic visits, so the season carries 60 x N$250 = N$15 000 of costs the firm never pays. Because the price of charcoal holds none of this, the market reads the activity as more profitable than it is for society, and output tends to sit above the level society would choose. A paper mill on a river in Vietnam works the same way: the fishers downstream pay, the price does not.

### The free-rider chain

Start with flood defences at Walvis Bay. Because each protected street stays dry whether or not its households contributed, each household is likely to wait for others to pay. This waiting is the free-rider problem, and it causes private providers to withdraw. The consequence is under-provision relative to what the defences are worth to the town as a whole - the classic shortfall of a public good, landing on the households and firms the waters would reach.

### Trapped factors and dominant sellers

Factor immobility fails markets in a second way. When net-mending work at Walvis Bay declines, the workers cannot at once become plumbers in Windhoek: their skills are fixed to the old trade, and homes and family keep them where they are. Labour then sits unused in one place while needs go unmet in another. And where a single dominant seller stands behind barriers to entry, it may hold output below the level a market of many small sellers would produce, to keep its price up.

### Abuse of monopoly power

Monopoly power is the power of a single seller, or of one seller far larger than any rival, to set its price because buyers have few other places to go. It rests on barriers to entry that keep rivals out, such as high start-up costs, distance from other suppliers, or control of a key resource. Holding that power is not in itself a market failure; abusing it is. A seller abuses its power when it raises its price above the level rivals would allow, holds back output, lets quality or service slip, or blocks newcomers. Take Karas Cement Works, the single cement producer within reach of builders in southern Namibia. Suppose it raises its price from N$95 to N$120 a bag and monthly sales fall from 10 000 to 8 000 bags. Its revenue rises by just N$10 000, from N$950 000 to N$960 000, and with less cement to make its costs are likely to fall, so its profit rises. Builders pay N$25 more a bag, and 2 000 fewer bags a month go into houses, shops and clinics. Fewer resources go into building than society wants: that is the misallocation.

## What market failure costs

### Misallocation

Over-consumption means buyers take more of a good than the level that matches its full social costs; under-consumption means they take less than the level that matches its full social benefits. In each case resources are misallocated: too much of the economy's land, labour and capital sits behind goods that do more harm than their buyers reckon, and too little behind goods that do more good than their buyers count.

### The over-consumption chain

Start with cheap strong brew sold at N$10 a carton in Swakopmund, where 2 200 cartons a month are bought against the 1 500 the town clinic judges manageable. Because the price holds none of the clinic hours and lost work the brew causes, buyers face a cheaper choice than the one the town bears, and consumption settles 700 cartons a month above the manageable level. This causes the town's nursing hours to flow into repairing harm rather than treating other patients. Drinkers gain cheap brew, while families lose rest and wages and the health service loses capacity.

### The under-consumption side

An Oshakati clinic sells flu vaccinations at N$60 each, and 300 are bought in a season, though the protection each vaccinated person gives to classmates and bus queues means the level serving the whole town is nearer 900 - a shortfall of 600 vaccinations. Buyers count the protection they themselves receive and skip the protection they give others, so too few are sold, and the missed protection lands on the unvaccinated neighbours. Night-school enrolment in a Brazilian city shows the same shortfall for a merit good.

### Measuring the shortfall

Put the vaccination market into figures. Buyers pay N$60 and weigh only the protection they receive themselves, so the market settles at 300 vaccinations a season. Counting the protection each vaccination gives to others, the clinic puts the level serving the town nearer 900. The 600 between the two is the under-provision: the part of the good's value the price never registered. The 900 is an estimate, so the size of the shortfall is a judgement, but its direction follows from benefits no buyer pays for.
