## Absolute and relative poverty

### The two definitions and the boundary between them

Absolute poverty is a lack of income so severe that a household cannot meet its basic needs - enough food, clean water, shelter and clothing. It is judged against a fixed poverty line: an income per person below that line means absolute poverty. The line is fixed in real terms, so a household either falls below it or clears it, whatever the rest of society earns.

Relative poverty is different in kind: a household whose income is well below the average for its own society, so it is shut out of the living standards most people around it take for granted. It is judged against a moving line, usually a set share of average income, so the line rises as the society grows richer. The test that tells the two apart is the line each is measured against: fixed basic needs for absolute poverty, the incomes of others for relative poverty. A household can therefore clear the absolute line and still sit below the relative one. Germany shows the point: a rich country with little absolute poverty still records relative poverty, since some incomes sit far below its high average.

### One household against both lines

The Amunyela household in Katutura has four members and an income of N$2 400 a month. Income per person is N$2 400 / 4 = N$600 a month. Suppose the absolute poverty line is N$700 per person: at N$600 the household is in absolute poverty. Suppose average income per person is N$2 000 and the relative line is 60% of it: 0.60 x N$2 000 = N$1 200 per person, so the household sits far below that line too.

Now the earner's pay rises to N$4 000 a month. Income per person becomes N$4 000 / 4 = N$1 000, which clears the absolute line of N$700. It remains below the relative line of N$1 200, so the household has escaped absolute poverty yet stays in relative poverty - the two lines tell different stories about the same household, so a question names the line it means.

## The causes of poverty

### The main causes and their shape

The causes work on income or on need. On the income side sit unemployment and low pay: a household whose earners cannot find work has no wage to live on, and low pay holds a household near the poverty line even in work, often because weak education and skills limit what a worker can command. On the need side sit large households with many dependants, where one wage must feed many, so income per person is low even where the wage is not; illness and disability, which cut earnings and raise spending at once; and old age without a pension. Two causes reach whole regions: drought that destroys harvests where families farm to eat, and distance from jobs, as in Namibia's sparsely settled north, where the nearest employer may be far away. The causes also tend to compound across generations: low income limits the schooling a household can afford, and weak schooling limits the next generation's pay.

### One closure traced through a town

Trace a cause through a town. A garment workshop in Oshakati closes, and its 120 workers lose their monthly wage - that is the starting point. Because each wage of about N$3 600 was the main income of a household, spending on food, rent and transport is cut back at once; the town's shops lose about 120 x N$3 600 = N$432 000 of sales a month. This causes the shops to take on fewer workers and some to close, so poverty spreads to households whose earners never worked for the garment firm. Therefore one closure can create more of its own kind: joblessness lowers spending, and lower spending takes away more jobs. In Vietnam, garment towns that lost orders saw the same spread, with poverty rising furthest among the least skilled workers.

### Illness and age as causes of poverty

Two causes deserve a closer look, because each works on income and on need at the same time.

Illness: when an earner falls seriously ill, the household loses wages if the earner cannot work, or earns less where hours are cut, and in a job without sick pay the wage stops at once. At the same time spending rises: medicine, transport to a clinic, and a relative's time spent caring rather than working. Suppose a taxi driver in Rundu earning N$4 000 a month is too ill to work for three months: the household loses 3 x N$4 000 = N$12 000 of income and pays for treatment besides. Where healthcare is not free, savings may be used up or livestock sold, so the household can stay poorer even after recovery.

Age: older people who can no longer work lose their wage, and without a pension or savings they may fall into poverty; Namibia pays an old-age pension to older residents for this reason. At the other end of life, young people may struggle to find a first job because they lack experience, and youth unemployment tends to be higher than adult unemployment. A household with many children or elderly relatives also has more people to support from each wage, so income per person falls.

## Policies to alleviate poverty and redistribute income

### Policies that redistribute income directly

To redistribute income is to narrow the gap between the highest and lowest incomes, moving spending power from the better-off to the worse-off. The main direct policies: progressive taxation, which takes a larger share of income from high earners than from low earners; more generous transfer payments, such as the old-age pension, child grants and unemployment benefits, which put money straight into low-income households; a minimum wage, which sets a legal floor under pay; and direct provision, such as free basic schooling and state healthcare, which gives poor households services they could not buy. Brazil ties family grants to school attendance, and the Netherlands holds pensioner incomes up through transfers - the same levers outside southern Africa.

### A grant traced to the line

Trace a transfer. A household of four in Otavi earns N$2 400 a month, which is N$600 per person, below the absolute line of N$700. The government pays a child grant of N$250 per child a month, and the household has two children, so the grant adds 2 x N$250 = N$500. Household income becomes N$2 400 + N$500 = N$2 900, which is N$725 per person: the grant carries the household across the absolute poverty line. Because the grant is paid for each child in school, attendance tends to rise as well, which works on the weak skills that caused the low income in the first place.

### Strength, and the drawbacks that temper it

Grants work fast and reach households no employer can help - that is their strength. The drawbacks temper them. A poverty trap: as a household earns more, its grants are withdrawn, so the gain from extra work is small and some earners may decide work does not pay. Cost: transfers and free services must be funded from taxation, so a wide programme may strain the budget or force other spending down. The minimum wage has its own limit: where the floor is set above the market wage, firms take on fewer workers than want jobs at that pay - more so where demand for that labour is elastic - so the policy aimed at the poor may add to joblessness among them.

### Longer-run policies: promoting growth and improving education

Grants and minimum wages act on today's incomes. Two further policies work on the causes, over years rather than months.

Promoting economic growth: when output grows, firms take on more workers and may pay more, so unemployed people find work and the low-paid gain hours. Growth also brings in more tax revenue at the same tax rates, which can fund pensions, grants, clinics and schools. Growth may therefore lift households out of poverty, provided the gains are shared with low-income households: where growth comes from a mine or an oil field that employs few people, or the new jobs need skills the poor do not have, poverty may fall little while average income rises. In China, rapid growth over several decades lifted hundreds of millions of people out of absolute poverty, while income gaps widened at the same time.

Improving education: schooling and training raise workers' skills and productivity, so they can get better-paid jobs and are less likely to be unemployed. Education also breaks the cycle in which poor parents cannot afford schooling for their children. A young person in Rundu who trains as an electrician may earn several times what casual labour pays. The drawbacks are time and cost: the benefit appears years later, the government must fund teachers and schools, and skills raise incomes only where there are jobs that use them.
