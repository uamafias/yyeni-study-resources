## What the government does in an economy

### What the government does

The government is the authority that runs a country. In an economy its role is to provide public goods and services that firms tend not to find profitable to sell - street lighting in Oshakati lights everyone who passes, and buyers tend to wait for a neighbour to pay rather than paying themselves - to raise revenue through taxes on income and spending, to transfer some of that revenue to households through payments such as the old-age pension, to regulate the markets that firms and consumers trade in, and to manage the economy as a whole toward aims such as jobs and steady prices. The German city of Hamburg runs its water supply the same way: one public provider, one set of pipes, funded from public revenue.

### Local and national government

Government works at more than one level. Locally, Namibia has town and municipal councils, such as those of Oshakati and Walvis Bay, and regional councils. A local council provides the services residents use every day: water and sewerage, refuse collection, street lighting, local roads, parks and markets. It also plans how the land in its area is used - which plots are for houses, which for shops and which for workshops - and approves building plans, so that, for example, a fuel depot is not put up beside a school. A council pays for this mainly through rates, a local tax charged on the value of property in the town, through charges for water and electricity, and through grants from the national government. Because a council is close to its residents, it can match services to what its own town needs: a port town such as Walvis Bay needs heavy-vehicle roads to its harbour, while Oshakati's large open market needs stalls, water points and refuse removal. Nationally, the central government in Windhoek does the jobs that serve the whole country: defence and the police, national roads, schools and hospitals, the old-age pension, the national taxes on income and spending, the laws that regulate markets, and the management of the whole economy toward aims such as jobs and stable prices.

### The government's international role

Internationally, the government acts for the whole country, because a firm or household cannot sign an agreement with another state. It does four main things. First, it makes trade agreements. Namibia belongs to the Southern African Customs Union with Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho and South Africa: goods move between the members without import taxes, and the members charge one common set of import taxes on goods from outside the union. A Namibian meat processor can therefore sell beef in South Africa without paying an import tax there. Second, it joins international organisations: the United Nations; the World Trade Organization, which sets rules for trade between its members; and the International Monetary Fund, which can lend to a member government that is struggling to pay for its imports. Third, it gives or receives aid - grants and low-interest loans from one country to another that fund projects such as roads, clinics and water schemes. Fourth, it works with neighbouring governments on matters that cross borders: Namibia, Angola and Botswana share the water of the Okavango River, so plans to take water from it are discussed between the three governments rather than left to any one of them. Countries outside Africa do the same: the members of the European Union let goods move between them without import taxes and negotiate trade deals with other countries as one group. The international role carries a cost as well as a benefit: a country inside a customs union gives up the right to set its own import taxes, so its firms gain a wider market while its government loses one of its own choices.

### Why the government steps in

Why does the government do these jobs rather than leave them to markets? Start where a market works: two traders exchange because each gains. Then notice what the market cannot do. Because buyers who do not pay still enjoy street lighting, a firm selling it collects from a few and serves many, so providing it tends to be unprofitable. Where one seller grows so dominant that buyers lose choice, it may raise prices above what a competitive market would charge, so the government regulates it. And where income divides very unequally, a government that judges this unfair can redirect resources through taxes and pensions. Each government function traces to something the market alone does not deliver, which is why the role of government differs from country to country and from year to year.

### How far the role reaches

The role is wide, not unlimited. In Namibia the government runs schools, pays the old-age pension out of tax revenue, licenses banks and inspects taxis; in the Netherlands the government spends close to half the country's yearly output, while in a poorer country the same list is shorter. The boundary is not fixed. A very poor country may lack the revenue to pay pensions on a wide scale - revenue limits it. Taxes raised too high may push a business toward shutting or moving - the activity shrinks, and with it later tax revenue. Running an industry, as with a national airline, requires skill and information a government may lack, so it is likely to run some industries less well than a firm with market experience. So a government widens its role where it can raise revenue and run the task, and narrows it where it cannot - the role moves with capacity.

### The boundary in a real situation

In 2026 the small farms of the Kavango valley have no all-weather road to the market in Rundu. No single trader gains enough from a road to build it, so none is built. The government may build it because every farm gains and so do shoppers in Rundu, so it uses public revenue to build what no single buyer would. People react, too: if the money comes from a new levy on the same farms, some farmers would rather keep their earnings and wait; if a trucking firm lobbies for the levy to fall on its rival's goods, the rule may help that firm while leaving shoppers worse off. There is also a limit: where the valley's next project costs more than the region can raise, the government cannot build it, and the want goes unmet. The example shows every side of the role: provider, taxer, regulator, and the boundary of what is possible.
