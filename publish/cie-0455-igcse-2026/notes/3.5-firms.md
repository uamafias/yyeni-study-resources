## Sorting firms: sector, ownership and size

### Three ways to sort a firm

A firm is a business that makes or sells goods or services, and economics sorts firms in three ways. By sector of production: primary firms extract or grow raw materials, such as a fishing firm at Walvis Bay; secondary firms process those materials into goods, such as a canning plant; tertiary firms supply services, such as the transport that moves the cans to shops. By ownership: a private-sector firm belongs to private individuals or groups who take the risk in the hope of profit, whereas a public-sector firm, also called a state-owned firm, belongs to the government, which appoints who runs it. By size: few workers and a small output make a firm small; many workers and a large output make it large.

### One firm, three sectors

Otjiwa Dairy in Gobabis works in all three sectors at once. Its own herd of 300 cows gives N$1.2 million of raw milk a year - farming is primary activity. Its pasteurising plant turns the milk into 400 000 tubs of yoghurt - processing is secondary activity. Its 6 vans deliver the tubs to shops across the region - a delivery service is tertiary activity. Classifying such a firm means sorting its activities one by one. A Japanese firm that catches its own fish, cans them and sells them through its own stalls makes the same point: a firm is not stuck in one sector by its name.

### Wanting a price rise is not what causes one

Learners mix up two different statements: the reason any firm prefers to charge more, and the reason a merger can leave buyers paying more. The boundary: wanting a higher price is a motive firms generally share - a higher price usually means more revenue from each sale - and it exists whether or not any merger happens. A merger resulting in higher prices is an outcome, which may follow where the joining removes one of the market's competing sellers, so the pressure of rivalry on price weakens. The test: ask whether a statement names what a firm aims at, or what changed in the market - the same want existed before the merger, when prices did not rise, so it cannot explain what changed.

## Small firms and why they survive

### What makes a firm small

A small firm employs few workers, produces a small output and usually serves a local market, with the owner often running it directly. Marula Foods in Oshakati is a working example: 6 workers press marula fruit into 9 000 bottles of juice a year and sell them to 15 shops within 60 km of the plant. Where the line sits between small and large differs between countries and industries - one uses the worker headcount, another yearly sales or the capital the firm uses - so smallness is a judgement against a measure, not one fixed headcount.

### Why small firms survive, and what they add

Small firms often survive beside large ones because they sell what large firms find hard to copy. Where a shopper values being known by name, the corner shop's owner remembers the account; where a narrow group of customers wants something particular, a small firm serves a niche too thin for a large firm to bother with. Flexibility counts too: a small firm changes its stock within days. What they add follows from that survival: jobs in places large firms do not reach, training for workers who may later start firms of their own, and spending kept circulating locally - though the wages and job security they offer tend to lag behind those of large firms.

### When a supermarket opens at the edge of town

In 2025 a large supermarket opened at the edge of Oshakati, near the streets where the town's small shops trade. The chain runs from its buying power: bulk purchases put its prices below the small shops', and the range under one roof is wider. Customers respond, so the small shops nearby are likely to lose revenue. Some change what they do - opening longer, delivering, stocking what the supermarket does not carry - and may hold enough trade to keep going; those that meet it head-on on price are the most exposed. In Vietnam, family-run food stalls keep trading beside convenience chains for the same reason: speed and familiarity are things a chain cannot easily supply.

### The challenges small firms face

Small firms also face challenges that large firms feel less. Finance comes first: a small firm has few assets to offer as security and a short trading record, so lenders see more risk and tend to lend less, at higher interest, or not at all, and growth is often limited to what the owner can save. Costs come second: buying in small quantities earns no bulk discount, and fixed costs such as rent are spread over a small output, so average costs tend to be higher than a large rival's. A small bakery in Rundu paying N$9 a kilogram for flour, against N$7 for a national chain buying by the truckload, starts each loaf at a disadvantage. Competition comes third: a supermarket or national chain can undercut on price and outspend on advertising. Finally, much rests on the owner: illness or one poor decision can stop the firm, and a fall in demand in its one town hits it harder than a firm selling across the country.

## How firms grow

### Two ways to grow

A firm grows internally when it expands through its own resources - opening a second branch, installing another production line, taking on more workers - and the firm remains itself throughout. It grows externally when it combines with another firm: a merger joins two firms into one by agreement, and a takeover sees one firm buy control of another. The two paths differ in what they need: internal growth needs time and finance; external growth needs a willing partner or the money to buy one.

### Why firms grow, and how it is paid for

The push to grow comes from the firm's aims: higher profit, a larger share of the market, and the lower average costs that growing output tends to bring. Growth needs paying for before it pays back - a second line must be built before it fills a single bottle - so the firm draws on retained profit, borrows, or asks its owners to invest more. The effects then spread through the firm: output and revenue rise together, and the workforce grows, giving workers more openings and clearer promotion paths. The strain spreads too: managers who ran one line now run two, and the personal contact a small workforce allowed is harder to keep as the payroll lengthens.

### Kaoko Juice builds a second line

Kaoko Juice in Opuwo sold 240 000 litres of juice in 2024. In 2025 it opened a second bottling line, funded from retained profit and a bank loan, and by 2026 sales had reached 500 000 litres a year. No other firm joined it, so the growth was internal: the same firm, with more of its own equipment and 30 more workers. The 30 extra jobs are the gain workers feel directly; the loan repayments are the cost the firm carries until the extra output earns them back. A family brewery in Bavaria tells the same story on another continent - new vats bought from its own profits, more beer, more brewers - because internal growth keeps every step in the owner's hands.

## Mergers and takeovers

### Merger and takeover

A merger joins two firms into one combined firm by agreement: the owners of both decide together that a single firm serves them better, and two names become one. A takeover is different in form: one firm buys control of another, which usually keeps trading under new owners. A reader of business news can tell them apart by asking who wanted the deal - agreement between both points to a merger; a buyer and a target point to a takeover.

### Why firms combine, and who feels it

Firms combine for scale. A merged firm runs one booking office where the two firms ran two, buys its inputs in bigger quantities, and reaches further on a shared delivery fleet. Where the two firms were each other's closest rivals, combining also removes a seller from the market, and the pressure of rivalry on prices may weaken with it: where customers have few alternatives, the combined firm is likely to face less resistance to a higher price. The consequences land unevenly. Workers in duplicated jobs may face cutbacks: one booking office needs one manager, not two. Customers may pay more where the rival that held prices down is gone. Rival firms now compete against a bigger firm with lower average costs.

### Two bakeries become one in Otavi

Otavi had three bakeries. In 2025 the two larger ones merged: before, one supplied 3 100 loaves a week and the other 1 900; the merged bakery now supplies 5 200 loaves a week from a single building. Within a year the town's bread price had risen from N$10 to N$12 a loaf. The merged firm wanted higher prices - firms generally do - but the want is not the cause: what changed is that the town's closest pair of rivals became one seller, and the remaining bakery now carries whatever pressure exists to hold prices down. Two bicycle makers in Osaka that merged show the other side of the same coin: shared factory costs on one side, one fewer competing brand in the shops on the other.

### Horizontal, vertical and conglomerate mergers

Mergers are named by how the two firms' activities relate. A horizontal merger joins two firms in the same industry at the same stage of production: two bakeries, two banks, two bus companies - the Otavi bakeries above are an example. A vertical merger joins two firms at different stages of the same production chain. It is backward when a firm joins with its supplier, nearer the raw material - a bakery merging with the flour mill that supplies it - and forward when a firm joins with its customer, nearer the final buyer - the flour mill merging with a chain of bakeries, or a brewery with a chain of bars. A conglomerate merger joins firms in unrelated industries: a Walvis Bay fishing company merging with a Windhoek furniture maker. Large business groups in Japan and South Korea grew partly in this way, holding firms in shipbuilding, electronics and insurance under one owner.

### What each type of merger offers, and what it risks

A horizontal merger offers the clearest economies of scale - one office, one delivery fleet, bigger orders for inputs - and a larger market share. Its risks fall largely on others: customers may pay more where the merger removes a close rival, workers in duplicated jobs may lose them, and the larger firm may meet diseconomies of scale. A backward vertical merger secures the firm's inputs: the bakery that owns its mill can count on flour when flour is short, controls its quality, and no longer pays the miller's profit on top of the cost of milling. A forward vertical merger secures outlets: the mill that owns bakeries knows its flour has buyers, and it gains the profit the bakeries made. The vertical risks are that the firm loses the freedom to buy from a cheaper outside supplier, its managers must learn to run a different kind of business, and rival bakeries may find flour harder to obtain. A conglomerate merger spreads risk: a firm in two unrelated markets may keep earning when demand in one of them falls, as furniture sales may hold up in a poor fishing season. Its weakness is that the managers may know little about the new industry, and few production costs can be shared, so economies of scale tend to be small.

## Economies and diseconomies of scale

### Average cost and scale

A firm's average cost is the cost of producing one unit: the whole cost of production divided by the number of units produced. Economies of scale are the falls in average cost that come as a firm grows larger. Diseconomies of scale are the rises in average cost that may return when a firm grows beyond the size its management can coordinate. Both are named for scale because both are about size: what happens to the cost of one unit as the firm's size changes.

### Oshakati Textiles reads its own figures

Oshakati Textiles grew by opening a second production line: output rose from 1 000 to 2 000 shirts a month and average cost fell from N$40 to N$34 a shirt. The savings come from being a bigger firm. Ordering twice the cotton earns a bulk-buying discount. A firm this size can justify a specialised cutting machine that a small output could not keep busy. Banks tend to lend to a larger firm more cheaply, and it can afford a specialist production manager. This differs from spreading the same rent over more units without growing, which lowers average fixed cost and belongs to topic 3.7. A Brazilian steel works gains the same way, buying iron ore by the shipload at a lower price per tonne.

### Too large to coordinate

The fall has limits. Past some size, average cost starts rising again, and the mechanisms are the reverse of the ones that brought it down. Coordinating departments takes meetings and reports, and in a firm of many layers a decision that once took a morning takes a week. Messages travel through more people on the way from the top to the line, so instructions arrive late or changed. Workers in a very large firm may feel like a small part of a big process, and effort and care tend to fall with that feeling. Where growth came by merger the strain is sharper still: two sets of managers and habits must be made into one.

### Internal and external economies of scale

The savings at Oshakati Textiles are internal economies of scale: they come from the firm's own growth. External economies of scale are falls in a firm's average cost that come from the growth of its whole industry in an area, whatever the firm's own size. As fish processing has grown around Walvis Bay, firms there can hire workers already trained in filleting and cold storage; specialist suppliers of nets, ice and packaging have set up nearby, as have engineering firms that repair boats and refrigeration units; the port and the roads serving it have been improved; and training courses for the industry are run locally. A new processor that opens there with 20 workers gains these savings at once, although it is small. In Switzerland's Jura region, watchmakers have long gained in the same way from a pool of skilled craftworkers and specialist parts makers.

### External diseconomies, and what scale does to an industry

The growth of an industry in one area can also raise costs. External diseconomies of scale are rises in a firm's average cost caused by the growth of its industry in an area: roads and port berths become congested, so deliveries take longer; firms compete for land, so rents rise; firms compete for the same skilled workers, so wages are bid up; and water and electricity supplies may be stretched. These raise the costs of the firms across that area, including firms that have not grown at all. Internal and external effects therefore act on different scales. Internal economies and diseconomies change one firm's average cost as that firm grows. External economies and diseconomies change the average costs of the firms across an industry as the industry grows in an area. While an industry is growing from a small base, external economies tend to win, and the industry's costs fall, which may let its firms charge lower prices and compete abroad; as it crowds into one place, external diseconomies tend to take over, and some firms may move to a new area.
