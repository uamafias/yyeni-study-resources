## Why market prices change

### Equilibrium price and what moves it

The equilibrium price is the price at which the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy equals the quantity sellers are willing and able to offer, so the market clears. At the Otavi milk depot, N$10 a litre clears the market when the 900 litres offered each week match the 900 litres taken. The causes of price changes are changes in market conditions: a change in a condition of demand, such as incomes or tastes, shifts the demand curve; a change in a condition of supply, such as input costs or the weather, shifts the supply curve. Once a curve shifts, the old price no longer clears.

### A flood at the Oshakati market

Floods destroy the vegetable gardens around Oshakati, so growers bring fewer bags to the weekend market at each price: the supply curve shifts left. At the old price of N$20 a bag, buyers want more bags than growers offer, so the price is bid up until, at N$26, the quantity demanded matches the smaller quantity supplied. The weather caused the rise; the rise is its consequence. A bakery in Munich lives the same chain when a poor wheat harvest makes flour dearer.

### Cause or consequence?

The confusion learners make: naming the price change as its own cause. The boundary: a change in the good's own price moves the market along unchanged curves, and is what a shift has already caused; a change in a market condition shifts a whole curve, and is what causes the price to change. The test: ask which side moved first. The flood cut the harvest, supply shifted left, and the price followed - not the other way round.

## What a price change does

### Four shifts, four consequences

The consequences of a price change are read as a pair: the new equilibrium price, and the new equilibrium quantity - the quantity bought and sold at that price over a period, the market's sales. Where demand shifts right and supply holds, both tend to rise: consumers pay more and sellers sell more. Where demand shifts left, both tend to fall. Where supply shifts right, the price tends to fall while sales tend to rise; where supply shifts left, the price tends to rise while sales tend to fall.

### A bumper harvest at Gobabis

Good rains swell the cabbage harvest around Gobabis: the supply curve shifts right, and at the old price of N$15 a head there are more cabbages than buyers take. The price falls to N$10 a head, and sales rise from 400 to 520 heads a week, since 520 - 400 = 120 heads. Households pay less for each head, and traders usually sell more heads at the lower price. Rice traders near Can Tho in Vietnam see the same after a strong Mekong harvest.

### When demand falls

A decrease in demand works the other way. Suppose a mine near Rosh Pinah lays off a third of its workers. Incomes in the town fall, and incomes are a condition of demand, so the demand curve for takeaway meals shifts left from D1 to D2 while supply stays where it was. At the old price of N$50 a meal, sellers now offer more meals than buyers take: a surplus. Sellers cut the price to clear unsold food, and the market moves down the unchanged supply curve to a new equilibrium at N$42, with sales down from 900 to 700 meals a week, since 900 - 700 = 200. Both the equilibrium price and the quantity sold have fallen. Households that still buy pay less per meal, while the takeaway owners sell fewer meals at a lower price, so their takings fall from 900 x N$50 = N$45 000 to 700 x N$42 = N$29 400 a week.

### Reading the consequence off the diagram

A demand-and-supply diagram shows the story in words. Put the price of the good on the vertical axis and the quantity on the horizontal axis. Draw and label the original demand curve D1 and supply curve S1, crossing at the original equilibrium. When a market condition changes, shift the curve whose condition moved, in the correct direction, and hold the other still. The new crossing, marked and labelled, is the new equilibrium: read off the new price and the quantity sold, and state in words what the diagram shows - which way the price and the sales moved, and on whom it landed.
