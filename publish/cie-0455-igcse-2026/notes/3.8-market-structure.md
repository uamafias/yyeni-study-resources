## Markets with many sellers

### What a competitive market is

A competitive market is a market with a high number of buyers and sellers in which each seller is too small to influence the market price on its own. The price is set by demand and supply across the whole market, and a seller who charges noticeably above the going price tends to lose customers to rivals. The curio stalls of Katutura show the shape of it: many small sellers, similar goods, one going price. Flower stalls in a town in the Netherlands trade the same way - one price among many small sellers, with no single stall able to lift it alone.

### Two new salons at the Katutura rank

Twelve salons trade at Katutura's hairdressing rank at about N$60 a haircut, each carrying costs of about N$40 a cut. Two new salons open. Because clients walk freely between salons, the newcomers win trade by charging close to N$50, and the going price drifts down from N$60. Each salon's profit narrows from about N$20 to about N$10 a cut, since N$60 - N$40 = N$20 and N$50 - N$40 = N$10. The entry of new sellers raises the quantity of haircuts offered at each price, and that is what pulls the price down.

### Price, quality, choice, profit

The effect of a high number of firms runs to four places. Price: with many sellers and free switching, the going price tends to sit near the level that covers costs plus a modest margin, because a dearer seller loses clients. Quality: a seller whose quality slips tends to lose customers to nearby rivals, so rivalry holds standards up. Choice: many sellers usually bring many varieties to choose between. Profit: each seller's profit tends to be squeezed by the same rivalry, and unusually high profit tends to attract new sellers, whose entry narrows it over time. A juice seller at the Opuwo market works under the same four pressures as a noodle stall in Vietnam.

## Markets with a single dominant seller

### What a monopoly is

A monopoly is a market with a single dominant seller - in the extreme, the only supplier - protected by barriers to entry that keep new sellers out. The barriers take several forms: a government licence, control of a key input, the high cost of setting up, or an established position buyers already trust. Because buyers who want the good have few alternatives within reach, the seller sets its own price within what buyers will pay, rather than taking a price set by many rivals. The ferry at Katima Mulilo under its single licence shows the shape, as does the one rail line into a Brazilian mining town.

### The fuel station at Ruacana

The fuel station at Ruacana is the only station within 90 km, and the distance is its barrier. When it lifts its price from N$22 to N$25 a litre, its monthly sales fall from 9 000 litres to 7 200 litres, a loss of 1 800 litres, since 9 000 - 7 200 = 1 800: the demand curve it faces is the whole area's demand curve, sloping downward, so a higher price tends to cost it sales. Because no rival can open within reach, the price may sit above the level a second station would tend to charge, and buyers pay it for the litres they still buy.

### Advantages and disadvantages

A single dominant seller cuts both ways. Where its large scale lowers its cost per unit, part of the saving may reach buyers through a lower price, and a single supplier may build a network - pipes, lines, tracks - that many small sellers could not fund separately, keeping supply steadier. Its profit, protected by the barriers, may also fund research into new products and methods that a small firm could not afford, and the security of its position makes long, costly projects less risky - a water utility may spend on new treatment methods knowing no rival will take its customers before the spending pays back. Against that, the barriers may let the price sit above the many-seller level and hold output and choice back; the pressure to keep quality up, and to innovate at all, is weaker where customers cannot easily switch; and profit may stay high precisely because newcomers are kept out. The gains tend to land on the seller; the higher prices and thinner choice land on buyers.
