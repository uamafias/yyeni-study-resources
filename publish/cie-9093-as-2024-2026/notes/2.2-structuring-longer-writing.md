# 2.2 Structuring longer writing

**What this topic asks of you**

- Choose a whole-text structure that fits the category and the task; order material for effect; plan and write one continuous piece of 600–900 words.
- Build paragraphs with topic sentences, connectives and internal coherence; develop each point or moment as fully as the form, purpose and audience need.

## Whole-text structure

### Whole-text structure

Whole-text structure is the shape of a piece from its opening to its ending: what it promises, how it develops, and how it closes. The shape is a decision, made before the writing starts, and the extended writing task — one continuous piece of 600–900 words in the category chosen from the paper’s selection, carrying twenty-five marks — is the task where the decision earns its keep.

### The shape each category tends to take

The shape each Section B category tends to take is different, and the plan starts from the category’s own shape. A story follows an arc: an opening situation, a conflict that complicates it, a turning point, and a resolution that shows what changed — the arc named in topic 2.3. A discursive piece explores: it opens the question, gives the sides their room, and reaches a reasoned view — the dialectic taught in topic 2.4. A review judges: it introduces what is reviewed, sets the criteria, weighs the evidence and lands the verdict — the shape taught in topic 2.5. One shape is not better than another; each is what its category’s reader arrives expecting.

### Planning the 600–900

Planning a 600–900-word piece runs in four steps. First fix the purpose and the audience, because the shape serves them. Then choose the shape: the arc, the exploration, the judgement — or a shape the task itself suggests. Then mark the sections: what each one does, in order, and where the turning point or verdict sits. Then give each section its share of the words — roughly 100 for an opening, 400 to 600 for the developing middle across two or three sections, and 100 for an ending that keeps its room.

### Chronological and thematic order

Chronological organisation follows time’s order: first this happened, then that. Thematic organisation follows the argument’s or the picture’s own logic: the cheapest objection first, the widest view last, the senses in the order a visitor would meet them. The choice is made for effect: a memoir of one day wants chronology, because the day’s shape is the story; a review of a course wants themes, because the reader deciding whether to enrol wants the teaching, the value and the verdict, not the Tuesday. Chronology is the habit; effect is the reason to keep it or break it.

### Worked example: a plan made in four steps

A task sets a description of a crowded place in the minutes before an event begins, with a focus on anticipation and the effect of tension built from calm rather than noise. The plan: purpose and audience — a general reader to be placed in the crowd; shape — a single scene built in waves, not a story; sections — the emptying last hour (100 words), the arrivals (200), the murmur building (200), the last minute (150), the moment itself withheld (50); shares — given, and the ending kept its room. Four steps, four minutes, and the writing that follows has somewhere to go.

### Worked example: withholding in a review

The review of a first-aid course holds its verdict to the last paragraph, and the holding is structural: the account, the criteria and the evaluation each end with the reader’s question still open — is it worth it? — so the verdict lands into a reader who has weighed the evidence themselves and is ready to be told. The same review with the verdict first would inform just as efficiently and persuade half as well, because a verdict before the evidence asks for trust and a verdict after it has earned the trust — which is why reviews are built back to front from their own conclusions.

### The ending, chosen

Ending on one image or line makes a piece memorable because memory keeps the last thing given: the closing choice is the piece’s parting gift, and it is chosen, not fallen into. The 600–900 range has a lower limit because development needs room: 600 words is where developed sections become possible, and writing short of it thins every section alike. Both facts bear on the same paragraph — the last one — which is why the plan reserves its hundred words before anything else spends them.

### The plan is the exam skill

Section B is worth twenty-five of Paper 2’s fifty marks, and it is the task most improved by planning and most damaged by its absence: a piece planned in four minutes has sections, shares and a kept ending, and a piece unplanned discovers its shape in the last hundred words, which is where the rushed ending comes from. Practise the plan until four minutes buys it — purpose, shape, sections, shares — and the writing time that follows is spent writing rather than deciding.

### Counterarguments placed, not appended

The discursive article about social media gives the friendships-have-thinned case its own full section, and the strongest version of it, before the reply — because a counterargument answered in a clause is a counterargument being hidden, and the reader knows it. Placing the objection beside the point — and answering it in the same room — lets the reader watch the argument survive its strongest test. Appended objections, dealt with in a sentence each at the end of a paragraph, read as decoration and convince no one who held them.

### Two failures of the whole

The first failure is the abrupt or rushed ending: the piece stops because the words ran out or the time did, and the resolution is compressed into a line or missing entirely — the reader is dropped where the piece should have landed them. The second is writing well short of 600 words, often because Section A took too long: the sections shrink, the development is the first casualty, and the ending is the second. Both failures share a cure, and it is the plan: a piece with its sections marked and its ending’s room kept does not run out of shape, because the shape was spent on purpose.

## Paragraphs and development

### Topic sentences and coherence

A topic sentence states what a paragraph is about — usually its first sentence — and the reader uses it to know what they are reading and why. Internal coherence means every sentence in the paragraph serves that topic: nothing belongs there that the topic sentence did not promise, and nothing the topic promised is missing. A paragraph with both is a room with the door marked; a paragraph without them is a corridor.

### Connectives and discourse markers

Connectives and discourse markers carry the reader between paragraphs and sentences: they show the relation — addition, contrast, sequence, consequence — so the reader does not have to guess it. "However" turns a paragraph; "for this reason" hands the consequence over; "by contrast" sets two things side by side without the reader doing the lifting. In extended writing they are the joints of the whole: the shape planned in the last section becomes visible to the reader through them.

### Paragraphing at random

Writing without paragraphs, or breaking them at random, hides the piece’s structure: the reader cannot see the sections, and the writing carries all its development in one undifferentiated stretch. Paragraphs are the shape made visible — one paragraph per section of the plan, or two where the section is long — and the break is chosen where the plan turns. The test is mechanical: a new paragraph begins where a new job begins, and a paragraph that does two jobs is two paragraphs wearing one indent.

### Development

Development is the detail, example or explanation that carries a point or a moment further: the claim plus its reason, the image plus its texture, the moment plus what it meant. It is what the extended-writing table rewards as ideas developed, and it is the difference between a piece that names six things and a piece that carries three of them somewhere.

### Developing a point

Developing a point runs in four moves: claim, reason, example, consequence. The claim is stated. The reason says why it is so. The example shows it being so — a real one, small enough to see. The consequence says what follows from it being so — what the reader should now think, feel or do. Four moves, and the point has been carried rather than named; four moves again for the next point, and the section is developed throughout.

### Development versus repetition

Repeating a point in new words is not developing it: development adds reason, example or consequence, and repetition adds only phrasing. "The market is busy. The market is full of people. The place is crowded" says one thing three times; "The market is busy — by nine you cannot cross the fruit row in a straight line, and the sellers have given up apologising for it" says it once and then shows, and the showing is the development. The test is the addition: what did the second sentence add? If the answer is only phrasing, it is repetition.

### Worked example: a paragraph built properly

A topic sentence: "The last hour before the festival is the quietest one, and the town knows it." The reason: everyone who can be ready is ready, and the waiting is the only work left. The example: the stallholders who were shouting prices at seven are drinking tea at their own tables by nine, and the church has rung the hour twice to nobody. The consequence: the reader is placed in a crowd-piece’s strangest pocket of calm, and the anticipation the task asked for is built from silence rather than noise. Claim, reason, example, consequence — and a paragraph that carries its point.
