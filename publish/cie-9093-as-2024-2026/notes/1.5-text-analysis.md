# 1.5 Text analysis

*cie-9093-as-2024-2026. Offline study notes, rendered from the approved content units on 2026-09-29. Everything here is also what the flashcards are built from.*

**What this topic asks of you**

- Identify a text’s characteristic features and relate each to its meaning, context and audience; characterise its overall style and prove it through specific language choices.
- Analyse form, structure and language and how they work together; show how several choices combine to create one meaning or effect; write about effects precisely.
- Organise the analysis around the text’s purpose and effect, not the order of its paragraphs; write it in a formal, precise analytical register.

---

## Reading the text as a whole

### Characteristic features

The characteristic features of a text are the features that make it recognisably the kind of text it is: its conventions of form, its structural habits, its recurring language choices. A feature is characteristic when it belongs to the kind rather than the instance — the headline belongs to the article; a particular headline belongs to this one — and the analysis uses the instance to show the kind.

### The opening move

The opening move of an analysis fixes the text’s form, audience, purpose and context in a sentence or two, because every feature discussed later is judged by what it does for them. "This is a charity leaflet for the general giving public, written to persuade during a drought appeal" is one sentence that arms every later point; it ends with the overall effect the text has on its reader. The text analysis task is set with the instruction Analyse, is worth twenty-five marks — five for reading, twenty for analysis — and the reading marks credit a subtle, assured grasp of meaning, context and audience, shown by use rather than retelling.

### Style

A text’s overall style is the manner its choices produce together, and it is characterised in two or three precise words — wry and conversational; urgent and plain; intimate and exact — which are then proved through specific choices. The adjectives are the claim; the choices are the evidence; and a claim about style counts only when specific choices prove it, so no adjective is written down without a quotation waiting to follow it.

### Worked example: the opening done properly

Take this opening, written for the purpose: "Nobody tells you that the hardest part of moving a school is the furniture." The form is a first-person column, the audience is general readers with an interest in schools, the purpose is to entertain as much as inform, and the style is wry and confiding: "Nobody tells you" draws the reader into a shared secret, and the joke puts the difficulty in the furniture rather than the children. Two or three precise words, proved at once — the analysis has begun, and the retelling has not started.

### What sits on the reading marks

The reading column of the task’s table credits a subtle, assured grasp of the text’s meaning, context and audience, and it is worth five of the twenty-five marks — a fifth of the task earned or lost in the first two minutes. The grasp is shown by use: an analysis that fixes the frame correctly and then quotes aptly has demonstrated its reading without a single retold event, and an analysis that retells has demonstrated the opposite. Learn the opening move until it is reflex, and the reading marks follow the analysis around like a shadow that has been paid for. The remaining twenty are for what this unit’s companions teach: the style characterisation, the three grounds, the four moves, the organisation and the register.

### Answering the wrong question

The error is responding to the topic of the text: agreeing with its argument, explaining its subject further, or giving an opinion on the matter. Analysis is about how the text is written, and the test is the transplant — if a sentence of the answer would still make sense in an essay about the subject, it is about the topic and not the text. "Fog harvesting is a brilliant idea" is an opinion; "the writer personifies the fog as a visitor to make the reader welcome the scheme" is analysis.

## Analysing form, structure and language

### The three grounds

Form covers the kind of text and its conventions; structure covers the organisation of the whole text — its opening, sequence, shifts of focus, paragraphing and ending; language covers the choices inside sentences: words, images, grammar, sound. The three are grounds, not headings: an analysis need not take them in separate sections, and a point that joins a structural choice to a language choice is usually the stronger for it.

### Structure is a system of decisions

The structural features worth analysing in a non-fiction text are its opening and what it promises, its shifts of focus, its paragraph lengths, its sequence, its repetition across the text, and its ending. Each is a place a writer made a decision — what to tell first, where to turn, how long to stay, what to echo, how to leave — and the analysis names the decision and its effect, never just the location.

### Effect

An effect is what a writer’s choice leads the reader to think, feel or do at that point in the text, and it is not the same as what the words mean: "the water arrives sideways" means fog, and its effect is to surprise the reader into reading on. The analytical sequence runs feature, evidence, effect on this reader, link to the text’s purpose — four moves, in that order, until they are habit.

### Choices that work together

Several choices can work together towards one effect, so the strongest analysis shows how a structural choice and a language choice support each other: a text that opens and closes in the same place, and uses the same image at both, makes its ending an arrival rather than a stop. Analysing features as a system means tracing that joint effect rather than listing devices one by one — the text’s meaning is made by combinations, and the paragraph that shows a combination says something no list can.

### Worked example: two choices, one effect

From a feature on a night rescue: the structural choice is a single one-sentence paragraph — "By midnight, the boy was out." — and the language choice inside it is the passive, which names no rescuer. Together they make the rescue feel like the mountain’s decision rather than any person’s achievement, and the analysis that shows the pairing has argued something about the text: that it credits the place, not the team. One effect, two choices, one point — which is the shape of the strongest paragraphs.

### Worked example: one choice, all four moves

Take this sentence from an appeal leaflet, written for the purpose: "Your N$150 is a bookcase’s twelfth shelf." The feature is the concrete arithmetic — a donation translated into a piece of furniture. The evidence is the exact words, quoted. The effect: the reader is made to picture their own money standing inside a classroom, which turns a transfer into a placement, and the reader who can see the twelfth shelf has already agreed to its existence. The link to purpose: the leaflet exists to convert generosity into specifics, and this sentence converts N$150 into wood — the four moves, in one sentence of analysis, and the sequence done is the sequence learned.

### Form and structure held together

A charity appeal opens with the problem, moves to the mechanism, prices the gift, and closes on the children. That is structure. The same appeal is a leaflet — headings, direct address, a call to action — and that is form. A strong point names both and shows them working together: the leaflet’s heading convention ("Give a classroom a bookcase") promises the structure’s first move, so the form opens the door the structure walks through. An analysis that treats form as a list at the start and structure as a route in the middle has described a filing system; an analysis that shows them shaking hands has analysed a text. And the language sits inside both, which is why the strongest points are often the hardest to file: a leaflet’s imperative verbs are language, but their placing in the closing paragraph is structure, and the analysis that says the imperative arrives last, when the reader has been made ready to act, has joined all three grounds in one sentence. The task names form, structure and language as the ground it rewards; it does not name them as three separate rooms. The analysis that treats them as one working machine is describing what the writer actually built.

### The four moves on a hard case

Some choices resist the sequence, and the resistance is instructive. A single word’s connotation is easy to run: feature (the noun), evidence ("rumour"), effect (rain made half-believed), link (the piece’s purpose of making the reader feel the dryness). A structural choice is harder, because its evidence is a place rather than a phrase: the feature is the held-back rescue, the evidence is "the account of the rescue is held back until the final paragraph" with the reader directed to the arrangement, the effect is the reader spending the piece asking whether it succeeded, and the link is the suspense the piece exists to build. Run the sequence on the hard cases too, and let the evidence bend to the choice — the sequence is the same, and only the quotation changes shape.

### Summary and spot-naming

Two errors hollow an analysis out. Retelling or summarising the text, often at length, replaces analysis with content, and the cure is the anchor rule: content is mentioned only to anchor a point about a choice. Naming devices and sentence types without saying what any of them does is feature-spotting, and it earns little; the deletion test catches it — remove the device’s name from the sentence, and if nothing about meaning or effect is left, nothing was analysed.

## Organising the analysis

### Organising by effect

An analysis organised by effect or purpose builds an argument about the whole text; one that follows the order of the text’s paragraphs tends to become a commentary on each in turn. The plan is made before writing: decide the two or three things the text does to its reader, give each a section that gathers evidence from wherever it occurs, and let the introduction state the view and the conclusion gather it.

### The analytical register

The analytical register is formal and precise: third person, present tense, exact terms, embedded quotations, no chatty asides. "The writer personifies the fog as a visitor" is in the register; "I think this bit is really clever" is a conversational commentary, and it weakens the analysis because it argues nothing — the register is the argument’s clothing, and chatty dress makes the reader expect a chat.

### Worked example: a plan made before writing

From a charity leaflet, planned by effect rather than paragraph order: first, the leaflet makes the reader feel the drought ("walks into every village at once"); second, it makes the solution feel easy ("that is not charity; that is infrastructure"); third, it makes giving feel immediate ("Do it now, while the dry season is doing its worst"). Three effects, one section each, evidence gathered from across the text — and the walkthrough trap is avoided because the plan never once asked "what happens in paragraph two?"

### In the exam

The text analysis is Section B of Paper 1: twenty-five marks, five for reading and twenty for analysis, opened by the instruction Analyse and set on an unseen text of about 550–750 words. Two hours and fifteen minutes cover both sections, so the analysis gets its share of the time — and the strongest answers spend two minutes of that on the plan, because a plan by effect saves the ten minutes a walkthrough costs when its paragraphs need rebuilding. Write the opening, make the plan, then write to it: form, audience, purpose and effect in a sentence; two or three sections argued by evidence from across the text; a conclusion that gathers.

### One toolkit, three tasks

Everything taught here works on the other analysis questions too: the comparison of Paper 1 Question 1(b) is this analysis done to two texts, with the connectives added; the reflective commentary of Paper 2 is this analysis done to your own writing, with the brief in place of the context. Learn feature, evidence, effect, link once, and the whole AO3 share of the qualification — forty of the hundred marks — is one skill wearing three different briefs. The organisation by effect and the analytical register travel with it, and the misconceptions filed here — summary, spot-naming, the walkthrough — are the same errors the other tasks record, which is why this topic’s habits are worth more than its marks: they are the whole qualification’s habits, learned once, in the place with the most room to practise them.

### The walkthrough

Working through the text paragraph by paragraph, or jumping around it with no line of argument, scatters the analysis: points about the same effect end up far apart, and the answer never states a view of the text. The test is to read the first sentence of each paragraph in turn — together they should state an argument about the whole text, not a route through it. The fix is the plan by effect, made before writing, and the paragraphs follow the plan instead of the source’s order.
