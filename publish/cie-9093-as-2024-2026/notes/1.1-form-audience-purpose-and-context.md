# 1.1 Form, audience, purpose and context

**What this topic asks of you**

- Know the conventions of the forms the papers use and what each convention does for its reader; recognise an unseen text’s form from its features and say what those features are there to do.
- Work out who a text is for and how that audience shapes it; explain how genre, purpose and context contribute to what a text means; read an unseen text accurately before analysing it.

## Forms and their conventions

### Form and conventions

Form is the kind of text a piece is, such as an article, a speech, an email or a review, together with the conventions its readers expect of that kind. Conventions are those expected features: a headline in an article, a salutation in a letter, a call to action in an advertisement. They are not decoration and they are not rules; they are the signals a reader uses to recognise what kind of thing is being read, so a writer can follow them or break them deliberately, and either choice means something.

### The forms the papers use

The forms the papers set are known by their conventions, and the list below names what is expected of them. An article carries a headline, a standfirst, a byline, quoted voices and short paragraphs. A speech addresses its audience directly, signposts its route, repeats key points so listeners can hold them and closes memorably. A formal letter and an email open with a salutation, state their purpose early and sign off. A blog uses a personal voice, direct address, headings and an invitation to respond. A review states what is reviewed, judges it against criteria, gives evidence and a verdict. A diary or a memoir writes in the first person and sets reflection beside events. A leaflet, brochure or advertisement uses headings, direct address, imperatives, benefits and a call to action. A report has a title, a stated purpose, findings in sections, recommendations and an impersonal register. A podcast or scripted talk keeps a host’s voice, spoken markers and signposting for listeners.

### Worked example: recognising a form

Take this opening, written for the purpose: "Dear Ms Shipanga, I am writing on behalf of the Ondangwa residents’ committee about the noise from the depot after dark." The layout is a message to one named reader, so it is a letter. The address — "Dear Ms Shipanga" — is direct and personal. The voice is a group speaking as one person, "on behalf of". The register is formal and public. The purpose is stated early: "about the noise". That evidence, from layout, address, voice, register and purpose together, gives one answer: a formal letter of complaint from a committee.

### Non-fiction that tells a story

Non-fiction is writing that presents itself as true: articles, memoirs, reviews, speeches, letters, travel writing. The boundary to watch is that non-fiction can use narrative techniques, such as scenes, characters and suspense, without becoming fiction, so a memoir extract that reads like a story is still a memoir, and the analysis says what its choices do for its reader.

### A form that announces itself

A leaflet opens: "Three ways to cut your water bill this dry season — inside." The heading promises a numbered list, so a reader who has thirty seconds at a kitchen counter expects to find it; the imperative "cut" and the possessive "your" put the reader in the sentence; the dash and the word "inside" withhold the answer, which is what makes the reader open the fold. Follow the conventions and the reader knows how to read it; the analysis then says what this leaflet does with them.

### When a convention is broken

A writer can break a convention on purpose, and the break carries meaning precisely because the reader expected the convention: a review with no verdict makes the reader supply it; a report that suddenly says "I" admits a person behind the findings, and the register shift is the point. In analysis, a broken convention is named as a choice like any other — what was expected, what came instead, and what the difference makes the reader notice.

### Naming is not analysing

The error to avoid is listing a form’s conventions as if the list were the analysis. A sentence that says an article "has a headline, subheadings and short paragraphs" has named three conventions and analysed nothing. The test is the job: every convention does a job for the reader, and a convention counts in an analysis only when the writer says what job it does for this reader. The headline of a feature article, for instance, teases rather than summarises, which suits a reader choosing what to read for pleasure.

## Audience, purpose, genre and context

### Audience and the implied reader

Audience is the readers or listeners a text is designed for, whether the text states them or the reader has to work them out from the choices. The implied reader is the reader the choices assume: a text that explains what a river crossing is assumes a reader who has never made one. The two can differ from the people who actually read it, and a writer can exploit that gap, as when an adult writes about teenagers for other adults.

### Working out an audience

Work out an audience from three questions, in this order. First, who is directly addressed: "Dear Ms Shipanga" points one way, "Hey everyone" another. Second, what they are assumed to know: a text that glosses "the rainy season" assumes a reader from far away. Third, what they are asked to do: vote, buy, forgive, attend, agree. The answers, not a guess, name the audience.

### Purpose, genre and context

Purpose is what a text sets out to do, such as inform, persuade, entertain, advise or argue; many texts do more than one at once, so ask which is primary and which secondary, and let the one most of the choices serve be the primary. Genre is a family of texts that share a purpose and conventions, and it is wider than form: travel writing is a genre that can appear as a blog, an article or a book. Context is the situation a text is produced and read in: when, where, by whom, for whom and through what medium. In analysis the word can also mean the words around a quotation, so say which sense you mean.

### Two contexts, one text

The context of production is the situation the writer wrote in; the context of reception is the situation the reader reads in. The same words mean different things in each. A speech written for a hall of supporters, read years later by strangers who know how the story ended, carries a meaning the writer could not control. The same holds for medium and date: readers bring different knowledge to a printed leaflet and a website post, or to a text from one decade read in another.

### Why the same fact reads differently

The same fact reads differently in a news report, a blog and an advertisement because the three answer different purposes for different audiences, and those purposes shape the choices around the fact. A dam saving forty million litres is a headline figure in a report, a triumph in the council’s leaflet, and a memory of a dry year in a resident’s blog. The fact is one thing; the choices — voice, register, sentence, image — are what the analysis is about.

### Worked example: fixing the frame

Take this sentence, written for the purpose: "You have two minutes to gather what matters and reach higher ground." The direct address "You" and the imperative "gather" suit an instruction read in a hurry, and "what matters" assumes a reader who owns things and stands to lose them: the implied reader is a resident in a flood-prone valley, not an official reading a report. Purpose: to move people now. One sentence, and form, audience and purpose are already fixed, which is why they come before any feature.

### A first reading of an unseen text

A first reading of an unseen text settles five things before any feature is chosen: the form, the audience, the purpose, the viewpoint, and the overall effect on the reader. Write them in the margin. Then check what the text says against what it does: what it says is its content; what it does is the effect of its choices. Gist and viewpoint come first because a feature can only be analysed as a means to an effect the whole text is working towards.

### Two errors that sink answers

The first error is naming the purpose and audience in a first sentence and never linking a feature to them again. The opening sentence is useful only if each analytical point ends by saying how the feature serves this text’s audience or purpose; otherwise it is a formality. The second error is misreading a detail of the source, such as who did what or what a phrase refers to, and building the answer on the mistake. Check each detail against the sentence it comes from: if you cannot point to the words, do not use the detail.

### Audience design at work

The same water-saving scheme, written twice. For the town council: "The scheme delivered a 31 per cent reduction in household consumption between June and August, at a capital cost of N$1.2 million." For a WhatsApp group of residents: "We used a third less water this winter — here is what actually worked in our yard." The designed-for reader of the first is an official who needs figures to allocate money; the second assumes a neighbour who will try things at home. Same content, different audience, different choices — which is the whole reason audience is named before any feature is analysed.

### In the exam

Both papers open from the same four words: form, audience, purpose, context. On Paper 1, the reading marks credit grasping the source’s meaning, context and audience, so the first reading fixes them before any feature is chosen; on Paper 2, the writing marks credit fitting the brief, so the brief is decoded the same way before the first sentence is written. Name them once, then keep using them: a feature is analysed as a means to an effect on this audience for this purpose.

### Writing in the wrong role

When a task sets a role and a purpose, both fix the writing. The role decides the voice and point of view; the purpose decides what each sentence is there to achieve. A learner asked to write as a park guide for visitors who writes instead as a reporter, describing rather than guiding, has missed the task whatever the quality of the sentences. The test is to name the speaker, the reader and the purpose before the first sentence, and to check every paragraph against all three.
