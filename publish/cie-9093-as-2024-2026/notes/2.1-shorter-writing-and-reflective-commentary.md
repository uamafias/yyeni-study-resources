# 2.1 Shorter writing and reflective commentary

*cie-9093-as-2024-2026. Offline study notes, rendered from the approved content units on 2026-09-29. Everything here is also what the flashcards are built from.*

**What this topic asks of you**

- Decode a brief — form, audience, purpose, role and focus — and write to fulfil it, whatever form it names.
- Write up to 400 words that use the space well, in the vocabulary, style and structure the brief demands; adapt style across forms and audiences.
- Reflect on your own writing and judge it against the brief: its purpose, form and audience; explain how your choices fulfil it, with evidence quoted from your text.
- Cover form, structure and language in the commentary, each tied to the audience it serves.

---

## Reading the brief

### The brief

A brief is the specification a piece of writing must fulfil: its form, audience, purpose, role and any focus the prompt adds. The brief is not a suggestion — every word of it is part of the task, and the fifteen marks of Paper 2 Question 1(a) are for a piece of no more than 400 words whose vocabulary, style and structure fit it. Read the brief twice before writing a word, and write its five parts down where they can be checked.

### Decoding the brief

Decoding runs in order. The form: what kind of text — speech, email, report, blog, story opening? The audience: who reads it — the hall, the cousin, the officials? The purpose: what must it do — persuade, describe, inform, entertain? The role: who is speaking — the head learner, the visitor, the club secretary? The focus: what extra thing does the prompt demand — "introduce both sides", "create a sense of unease"? Five answers, written down, and the piece is planned before it is written.

### Form is not purpose

A task’s form and its purpose are separate: a speech that informs and a speech that persuades are different speeches, and the difference shows in every choice — the informing speech signposts and explains; the persuading speech presses and asks. Writing the form without its purpose produces the right container with nothing inside: a report laid out faultlessly that recommends nothing, a speech addressed to the hall that asks the hall for nothing.

### Worked example: decoding done properly

A prompt sets: "Write a speech for a school debate, as the first speaker for the motion, introducing both sides of the question of school uniform." The decode: form, a speech; audience, the hall of learners and teachers; purpose, to open the debate persuasively; role, first speaker for the motion; focus, introduce both sides — the one requirement a learner most often drops. The piece that argues one side only has missed the focus in the prompt’s last four words, and its fifteen marks are marked as a poorer fit to the task even though every sentence might be well written.

### Three ways to miss the brief

The first error is writing in a different form from the one set — a story where a description was asked for — which misses the task whatever the quality of the writing, because the form is part of the specification. The second is ignoring the role or the occasion: a debate speech that never opens the debate, a report written by nobody in particular, leaves the brief half-decoded. The third is missing the focus sentence: the piece persuades without reasons when the prompt asked for reasons, or describes without the sense of calm the prompt named. Each error is checked the same way: reread the brief, name its five parts, and test the piece against all five.

## Writing within 400 words

### Planning the 400

Planning up to 400 words means sharing them: an opening of about fifty words that establishes the form and the voice; two or three developed sections of about a hundred each, carrying the piece’s content with its ideas developed; and a close of about fifty that lands the purpose. The words are a budget, not a ceiling to bump against — and the budget exists because the table rewards development, which needs room.

### The opening’s job

The opening of a 400-word piece must establish the form and the voice at once, so the reader knows what they are reading and who is speaking before the first section arrives: a speech greets its hall, an email finds its reader, a report states its subject and its purpose. In a piece this short the opening is a fifth of the whole, and a slow opening is a fifth wasted — the strongest openings are already doing the piece’s work.

### Why length is development

A very short answer loses the development marks because development is part of achieving the task: the table rewards ideas developed — claimed, reasoned, exemplified, carried — and writing far less than the space allows leaves them named. A piece of 180 words for a 400-word task has usually produced a list where the table wanted a carrying, and its mark reads as the task only partly achieved, whatever the sentences are like.

### Worked example: audience changing the words

The same content for two audiences. For classmates: "Look, the trip is happening — we just need two more people to make the bus pay for itself, so this is me asking you before I have to ask a teacher." For the school board: "The Geography field trip requires two additional participants to reach the minimum number for coach hire; I write to request approval to open places to the neighbouring class." The facts are one set; the vocabulary, the contractions, the pronouns and the structures are two — because the audience changed, and the change is visible in every choice.

### Register is decided by the pair

Formal and informal register are decided by the audience and purpose, not in general: a formal register suits an official reader and an official purpose, and an informal one suits a friend and a friendly purpose, and each mismatches the other’s task as badly as the reverse. The test of register is not "which is better?" but "which reader, and for what?" — a formal blog misplaces itself as completely as a casual report to the board, and the analysis names the pair that decides it. Learn to hear the question in the brief itself: ‘to the parents’ says one register, ‘to the board’ says another, and the brief has opinions about it from its first word.

### In the exam

The shorter writing task is Paper 2 Question 1(a): a response of no more than 400 words to a prompt, choosing vocabulary, style and structure to fit a specific form, purpose and audience, and it carries fifteen marks — expression and range, accuracy, organisation and development, task achievement, and the audience’s engagement, all five strands in one table. The part (b) commentary that follows is written about this piece, so its choices matter twice: write the piece as a text worth analysing, with choices you can name. The next section teaches the commentary that will analyse it; this one teaches the piece that gives the commentary something to say.

## What a reflective commentary does

### What a commentary is

A reflective commentary is an analysis of your own writing’s choices of form, structure and language, judged against the brief, and it is not a summary of what the piece says. It is analysis because it explains what the choices do: the commentary and the analysis of a stranger’s text are the same craft, with one difference — the text under the lens is yours, and you know what you intended, which is the thing to explain.

### Says versus does

The difference between what your text says and what your choices do is the whole space a commentary works in: the first is content, the second is craft, and the marks are for the second. "My piece tells the story of the festival" retells; "my piece opens in the middle of the dance so the reader arrives as a guest, which fulfils the brief’s focus on atmosphere" analyses — the choice, the quotation, the effect, the brief.

### Retelling and feature-listing

Two errors hollow a commentary. Retelling the content of your own piece tells the reader what they already know and analyses nothing — the marker has your text beside them, and the commentary’s job is to explain it, not repeat it. And a commentary with no quotation from the text it is about makes claims no reader can check: it quotes your own piece in short embedded stretches, exactly as an analysis of someone else’s text would. And a third error shadows them: listing the features you used — "I used adjectives and metaphors" — and stopping, which is feature-spotting in your own writing; the point is what each choice does for the audience and the brief. The fix for all three is the same: every sentence of the commentary names a choice, quotes the words, and says what they do.

### A commentary sentence, taken apart

Take the model sentence apart to see the moves separately. The choice: the direct address — a form-and-language decision about how the speech meets its hall. The quotation: ‘some of you have never known’, embedded in the commentary’s own grammar so the evidence arrives inside the claim. The effect: the closure is made to sound like a shared history being taken away, which positions the hall as defenders — the reader of the commentary is told what the audience of the speech was made to feel. The link: the brief’s demand for persuasion is served by that positioning. Four moves, one sentence, and the sentence is a template: any choice, any quotation, any effect, any brief, and the shape holds.

### The same craft as Paper 1

The commentary is the Paper 1 analysis turned inward: the feature-evidence-effect-link sequence is the same, the embedded quotation is the same, the analytical register is the same, and the errors that lose marks are the same — summary, spot-naming, stock effects, no evidence. What changes is the object (your own text) and the judge (the brief, standing where the source’s context stood). A learner who can analyse a stranger’s text can analyse their own; the only new skill is the honesty — explaining what you did, not what you wish you had done, which is why the commentary is written straight after the piece, while the decisions are still warm.

### Worked example: one commentary sentence

From a model speech: "The tricolon ‘we came, we saw, we were asked to leave’ undercuts the piece’s formality at the close, which fulfils the brief’s demand for a speech that entertains its hall while making its point." One sentence, all four moves: the choice (the tricolon), the quotation, the effect (undercutting formality, entertaining), the link to the brief. Twelve of these sentences, and the commentary is written — which is why the sentence shape is worth learning as a reflex.

## Writing the commentary

### The three grounds, in your own text

A commentary must cover form, structure and language, each tied to the audience it serves, because the brief was fulfilled by all three working together: the form found the reader, the structure carried them, the language held them. A commentary that covers one ground has described a third of the achievement — and the table rewards the whole.

### One ground is not enough

A commentary on structure alone, or on language alone, abandons two of the three grounds the table rewards: the piece was written with all three, and the commentary must account for all three. The error is common because language is the easiest ground to quote from; the cure is a plan with three headings before the writing starts — form, structure, language — and no heading left empty.

### Sharing the commentary out

A commentary is shared across form, structure and language by fixing them to choices: one paragraph on the form and its conventions (the greeting, the salutation, the report’s sections), one on the structure’s route (the opening’s promise, the order of the sections, the close), and one or two on the language’s work (the register, the imagery, the sentence shapes). The share is decided by where the choices sit: a piece with one striking image and a clever frame gives the image its sentence and the frame its own.

### Worked example: the three grounds found in one piece

Take the library report as the object. Its form choices: the title naming audience and subject, the numbered recommendations, the offer of a presentation — each one a decision about how the board is met. Its structure choices: findings before interpretation before recommendations, so the board receives evidence first and is led to action; the interpretation paragraph’s pivot (“not short of learners; short of room”) converts measurement into diagnosis. Its language choices: the exact figures, the quoted learner comment standing in the board’s papers, the costed recommendations. Three grounds, one piece, and a commentary that names a choice from each has covered the table’s whole ground.

### In the exam

The reflective commentary is Paper 2 Question 1(b), and it is worth ten marks for the analysis of your own Question 1(a) text: how its choices of form, structure and language fulfil the brief. The table rewards the same craft as the Paper 1 analysis questions — feature, evidence, effect, audience — with the brief in place of the context, and the same errors lose it: retelling, feature-listing, no quotation, one ground only. Write it in the analytical register, quote your own words, and keep every sentence pointed at the brief. The ten marks sit beside the fifteen of the piece, which makes the commentary a third of Section A’s total: twenty-five marks ride on one piece of writing and one account of its making, and the account is the cheaper half to learn, because it is the same analysis craft the rest of the qualification rewards.
