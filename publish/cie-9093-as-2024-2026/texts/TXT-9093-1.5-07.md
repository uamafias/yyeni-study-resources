WHAT THE WATER TAUGHT ME

Good morning, graduates. And good morning to the parents, who have been practising their faces all morning, and to the teachers, who are the reason half of you are sitting upright.

They asked me to speak about my work, and I will, but I want to warn you about the format: every speech like this one is a list of three things, and mine are no better than anyone else’s. Here they come anyway. Three things the water taught me. I have spent eleven years fixing rural water systems, and everything useful I know arrived in the order I am about to give it to you.

The first thing the water taught me is that the problem is never the pipe. When I started, I thought I was a plumber with a degree. I arrived in villages with engineering and left them with broken pumps, and it took me two years to understand why: a pump that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, and a committee with two women on it belongs to everyone, and I could give you the studies that prove this, but you have sat through enough statistics this morning. The pipe is the easy part. The people are the engineering.

The second thing — and graduates, this is the one I want you to keep — is that maintenance is the actual invention. We love the new thing. The new borehole, the new app, the new plan. And I have stood, in my career, beside twelve beautiful new things that died in a year, and beside one old pump, installed before I was born, that has never stopped, and the difference between the dead and the living is a man named Shipanga who is paid a small salary, on time, by the people who drink the water. Whatever you build in your lives — a company, a marriage, a garden — build the Shipanga into it. The boring thing done regularly is worth ten brilliant things done once.

And the third thing the water taught me is the one I did not want to learn. In 2019, in the north, I stood in a queue of women at a broken tap at five in the morning, and I was there to fix the tap, and the tap was fixed by noon, and it broke again within a year, because I had fixed the tap and not the thing around the tap. The women in that queue could have told me. Some of them tried. I was twenty-six and I had a degree, and I am telling you this at a podium because someone told me this once, at a podium, and I did not listen either, and you will not listen, and it is still my job to say it: the people standing in the queue know things. They are not waiting for your help. They are waiting for you to ask.

So that is the list. The pipe is easy and the people are hard. The boring thing, done regularly, is the invention. And ask the queue.

You are going out into a country that needs you, and it does not need your certainty — it has plenty of its own. It needs your questions.

Congratulations, graduates. Go home and call your mothers, and ask them something you think you know the answer to. Report back to me. I will be here.
