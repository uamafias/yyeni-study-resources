GIVE THE STREETS BACK TO THE BICYCLE

The loudest objection to the city’s new cycling plan arrived, predictably, from a car. "You will kill the town centre," a shopkeeper told the council, from behind a counter of goods that 71 per cent of his customers — according to the council’s own counts — arrive on foot, by bus or by bike.

I want to take that objection seriously, because it is the honest one, and because the dishonest ones are being handled by the usual organisations in the usual way. So: yes, the plan removes 240 parking bays. Yes, some drivers will find their Saturday harder. The question is what those 240 bays are currently achieving, and the answer is: they are achieving the thing that is killing the town centre, which is a street nobody wants to walk down.

Walk down Independence Avenue on any afternoon and count the people who linger. There are none, because lingering on a four-lane road is an act of faith. Now stand at the corner where the pilot project ran for one month last year — one month, two blocked lanes, some planters and borrowed benches — and count again. The pilot’s data, which the council published and almost nobody read, showed footfall up 40 per cent, and shopkeepers in the pilot zone — including two who had written to oppose it — asked for it to be extended.

The bicycle is not the point of the plan. The bicycle is the instrument. The point is the street: who it belongs to, who it is safe for, and whether a town centre is a place you pass through at forty kilometres an hour or a place you are in. Every city that has run this experiment in the last fifty years — and by now, most of them have — has discovered the same sequence: the protected lane, then the families, then the pavement cafes, then the complaints, then the quiet discovery that the town centre is doing better than it has in decades.

I use a car. I will lose two of my parking bays to this plan, and I have decided to be pleased about it, because I have also stood on Independence Avenue at five o’clock and watched what the four lanes do to the air, the noise and the faces. A street that is pleasant to walk down is a street people walk down. This should not need research to establish, but it has research, and the research says: planters, benches, bicycles.

The plan’s opponents deserve better answers than they have been given, and the honest ones are these: deliveries will be harder for the first year, and some streets will lose more than two bays, and the council has budgeted poorly for the disabled parking that must be kept. Every one of those objections deserves a line in the final design, and the final design has a public comment period, and the shopkeeper should write to it.
What the objectors are really defending, I think, is not parking but a memory — the memory of a town centre that worked when the car was the future. The car was the future. The future has moved on, and it is doing eleven kilometres an hour in the protected lane, in the rain, wearing a helmet, and stopping to buy bread.

Give the plan its two years. Count the feet. Then let the numbers argue with the shopkeeper, who will be busy serving the people they brought.
