I WAS SIXTEEN AND MY GUITAR WAS OLDER THAN MY MOTHER, AND I WAS going to play three songs at the cultural evening at the Otjiwarongo town hall, and I have never since been as frightened as I was in the hour before.

The fear was specific. I was not afraid of the audience in general — I had sung in church since I was nine, and a church teaches you that the audience is on your side. I was afraid of the moment between the introduction and the first note, which is the only silence in the world that is actually listening to you. In church the piano starts and you start with it. At the cultural evening there would be a man saying my name, and then a walk to a chair, and then a guitar being tuned by a girl who had never tuned anything in front of anyone, and then the silence.

My mother made me a dress the week before, from material she had been saving for a reason she never gave, and she ironed it twice, and she said the thing she says: "Sing to the back wall." She had said it since church. Sing to the back wall, because the back wall has paid like everybody else and it is the furthest away, and if the back wall can hear you the rest is a kindness.

The hall held two hundred, and the cultural evening was full, because the schools were on holiday and the town had nothing else on. I remember the programme in absurd detail: a marimba band from the secondary school, a poet who read for eleven minutes about the drought and got the biggest applause of the night before me, a dance troupe whose tape jammed and who danced to silence for eight seconds and did not stop — I learned more from watching them handle the silence than from anything anyone said to me that year.

My name was said. I walked to the chair. I tuned the guitar, which took a year, and the tuning was the performance’s secret friend: it gave me something to do with my hands and my eyes, and the audience forgives tuning the way it forgives a host lighting a candle — it is a thing that has to happen and everyone knows it.

Then the silence came, the listening one, and I sang to the back wall.

The first song was one I had written, which I have since stopped playing and will not pretend was good. The second was a hymn everyone in the hall knew, and in the second verse they sang the chorus with me, without being asked, the way a hall does when a hymn reaches the chorus and two hundred people decide at once not to be an audience. My mother says the dress was ruined by the tears by the end of the third song; I sang the third to the back wall with my eyes shut.

I did not win the evening’s small prize — the poet did, and rightly; eleven minutes about the drought and the biggest applause were no accident. The organiser paid me N$50 and a plate of food, and I still have the fifty somewhere, unfolded, because that was the first money anyone ever paid me to sing, and I did not know how to spend the first of a thing.

What I know now, that I did not know at the chair: the silence between the name and the first note is not the audience examining you. It is the audience arranging itself — getting ready to be with you. Two hundred people deciding at once to listen is the most generous thing a room can do, and a frightened girl in an ironed dress owes that room only one thing, which is to start.

I started. That is the whole story of my working life, told at its true size.
