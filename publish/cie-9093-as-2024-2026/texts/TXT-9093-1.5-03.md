THE HOUSE WITH THE YELLOW DOOR

The taxi could not find the street, and then it could not find the number, and then it could not find me, because I had not said the name of the house out loud in eleven years, and I said it wrong.

The yellow door had been repainted. That was the first thing, and it undid me more than the gate, which was the same, or the jacaranda, which had grown into a green country of its own, older now than my father had been when he planted it. Someone had repainted the door the same yellow — the exact yellow, I checked, standing in the road like a fool, my hand on the taxi’s window frame — and I understood that this was the difference between a house and a home. A house does not repaint its own door.

I had left at eighteen with a suitcase and a silence, and the silence had lasted exactly as long as the flight, because my mother met me at the gate with both arms up, shouting my childhood nickname to the whole street, a woman my age now — the age she was when I left, which is an arithmetic that waits inside every homecoming.

Inside, everything had moved and nothing had changed. The sofa faced the window. The photographs had grown — there were new children on the wall, my brother’s, and I studied their faces the way you study a language you once spoke. The kitchen smelled the way kitchens in memory are supposed to smell and never do: bread, and the particular soap, and under it, faintly, my father’s pipe, eleven years after the pipe.

We did not talk about the door for two days, and then we did.

"You repainted it," I said, meaning: why, meaning: he chose that colour, meaning: I know what it costs to hold a thing like that in place, meaning nothing I could say.

"The rain," my mother said, which was true, and was also the gentlest lie I had ever been told, because we both knew the paint had not been for the rain, and we both let the truth sit between us on the sofa like a guest.

I walked the house that first evening, alone, room by room, cataloguing the changes for no one: the new stove, the same curtain, my father’s chair, angled now towards the window he no longer sits at, which is what we do with the chairs of the missing in this family — we keep them, and we point them at the door, and we do not discuss it.
On the last evening we sat on the step with the tea she still makes too strong, and the street did what the street had always done — children, dogs, a radio somewhere, the neighbour’s gate — and my mother said the thing I had flown eleven years to hear, which was nothing, which was, "Your father would say the tree is getting out of hand."

The tree is getting out of hand. I looked at it for a long time, the way you look at anything that has kept growing while you were away, and I said, "Then we’ll get the shears."

Which is how we came to be in the garden at six the next morning, my mother and I, both of us in his gloves, cutting the jacaranda back from the roof, and laughing, and not one word of it about the eleven years, and every word of it about them.
