NINETY YEARS OF THE TAMARISK CLUB

The clubhouse is the same three rooms it was in 1936, and on Sunday mornings it still smells the same: liniment, cut grass and the particular tea that only this kitchen has ever managed to make bitter and indispensable at once.

The Tamarisk Football Club is ninety years old this month, and the club’s historians — an unpaid, argumentative committee of four — have prepared an exhibition for the hall, which is fitting, because the club’s entire history has been an argument conducted in good faith by people with mops.

The club was founded in 1936 by railway workers who had, the minute book records, "one ball, one field with ambitions, and no agreement about the offside rule". The minute book is the club’s real archive: it records a 1948 debate about whether players could be paid "in kind, specifically in bread", and the 1951 crisis, known to every member as the Great Teapot Affair, in which the club’s treasurer and its goalkeeper stopped speaking for eleven years over the purchase of a teapot that survives, polished, behind the bar.

The club has nearly died three times, and the reasons are instructive. In 1962, the field’s owner wanted it for cattle, and the club survived because the chairman of the rival club — the rival club — organised the petition that saved the field. In 1974 the north stand burned down, and the rebuild took eight years, conducted mostly by two brothers who disagreed about everything, including the fire’s cause, and who built the stand that stands today. And in 1999, with membership at eleven and the tea no longer enough, the club took the decision that old clubs find hardest: it opened the youth section, and let the children change the club’s manners.

The woman who led that change is now the club’s longest-serving committee member. Marta Shikongo, who joined at nineteen as a youth coach and is now sixty-one, has opinions about the club’s traditions that can be summarised as: all of them, including the tea. "A club is not a museum," she says, in the kitchen, gesturing at the teapot with an affection that contradicts her entirely. "It is a group of people who agree to keep showing up. The traditions we keep are the ones the children have made us keep."

Her daughter plays in the first team. Her granddaughter is in the under-nines, and on Sunday mornings the three of them can be found in the same kitchen where the tea is made bitter, arguing, Shikongo says, "in the family style".

The exhibition’s centrepiece, on loan from a bank vault in the capital, is the club’s founding ball — the actual ball, or so the committee of four maintains, though two of the four maintain it is the second ball, the first having, in the words of the minority report, ‘gone to the veld in 1953 and been replaced by a ball of no historical interest whatsoever’. The club has voted to display it beside the minority report, which is the most Tamarisk thing the club has ever done.
The anniversary exhibition runs for the month, and the committee of four has agreed on almost nothing about it, except the final display: ninety years of photographs, ending with the under-nines on the new field, which is the same field, with better grass, and the same ambitions.

Ninety years is not an achievement, one of the historians told me, and then corrected himself. "It is not a trophy. It is a decision, made every year by everyone who pays the subs and mops the floor and turns up. We have made it ninety times. That’s the whole history of this club, and the teapot knows it."
