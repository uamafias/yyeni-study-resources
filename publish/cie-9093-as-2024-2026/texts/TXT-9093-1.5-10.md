THE WHALE THAT WENT HOME

The young humpback stranded at Pelican Point on Tuesday morning was refloated last night at high tide by a team of volunteers, three boats and, in the assessment of the marine biologist who led the operation, "an unreasonable amount of luck".

The whale, estimated at eighteen months old and roughly eight metres, was found at 06:40 by a tour operator launching for the morning seal colony run. "It was on its side, breathing hard, and the first thing we did was the thing you are not supposed to do — we all took photographs," said Daan Pretorius, who called the stranding line at 06:52. By 07:30 the site held forty volunteers, a vet, two researchers from the fisheries institute, and a growing bucket brigade with wet towels.

Stranded cetaceans die of their own weight, and time is the enemy: out of water, the whale’s body begins to crush its own organs within hours. The team worked in shifts through the morning heat, keeping the animal wet, digging a channel beneath it, and rigging a sling from the tour operator’s boat trailer. Two attempts at refloating at the midday tide failed when the whale slid back into the shallows.

The turning point was not equipment but organisation. Dr Loide Amupolo, the marine biologist who coordinated the afternoon, assigned the crowd by task — water, shade, silence, and a cordon to keep the growing public at two hundred metres — and, in the moment the local coverage will remember, asked everyone in earshot to be quiet. "The animal is exhausted," she told the line of volunteers, "and sound travels through its body like through a drum. Help it by saying nothing."

At the 18:20 high tide, with three boats and the sling, the whale came off the sandbar on the second pull and held in the channel, blowing twice — "which is not gratitude," Dr Amupolo was careful to say, "it is breathing" — and at dusk it was tracked swimming west past the point.

The tag’s first three fixes came in overnight, all west of the point, and the institute’s overnight watch — two students and a kettle, in the tradition of every great scientific vigil — has released them with a map. The town has taken the whale to itself in the way coastal towns do: the bakery sold out of whale-iced buns by noon, the school has already asked Dr Amupolo for a visit, and the stranding line reports more volunteer sign-ups in one day than in the previous year.
Whether it survives the week is unknown. Young humpbacks strand most often from illness or separation, and Dr Amupolo’s team has fitted a temporary tag that will report for ten days. "We have given it a tide and a chance," she said. "The sea decides the rest."

The operation cost the volunteers a day and the fisheries institute an unbudgeted afternoon, and no one involved pretended otherwise. Asked at the end of it why forty people had spent a Tuesday holding wet towels on a whale, Pretorius answered with the sentence of the day: "Nobody who was there is asking that question."

The stranding line’s numbers, for the record, are public, and Tuesday’s volunteers have been invited to an open evening at the institute next month, where Dr Amupolo has promised to explain, over tea, what the drum of a whale’s body actually means, and how the next town can be ready with its buckets, its silence and its unreasonable luck.
