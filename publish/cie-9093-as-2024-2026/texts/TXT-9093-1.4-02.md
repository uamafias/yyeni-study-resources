LET ME SAY THE THING NOBODY AT THE TOURISM CONFERENCE SAID: WE ARE drowning, and the water is money.

I have lived in this town for twenty-two years. I ran the bookshop that used to be on Post Street, back when Post Street had a bookshop, a hardware store, a fishmonger and a place that fixed radios. The fishmonger is a coffee bar now. The radio man sells timeshares. The hardware store is an "artisanal marketplace", which is a word for a shop that sells the same wooden whale to people who arrived by cruise ship.

Nobody doubts the numbers, and this is the part that makes the argument hard to have. Before the harbour expansion, the town’s winter was three quiet months of wind and bond repayments. This year the winter occupancy was eighty-four per cent. The municipality’s rates revenue has doubled. There are jobs — real ones, and young people are doing them — and the bus that connects us to the city now runs twice on Sundays, which it never did when we asked for it politely for a decade.

So yes: the surge brought things. I am not the resident who wants the old town back, because the old town had a winter you could starve in, and I remember it.

But here is what the conference’s slideshow did not include. A two-bedroom house on Post Street rented, in 2019, for N$4,500 a month. The same house is on a short-let platform tonight for N$1,900 a weekend, and its long-term tenants — the nurse, the teacher at the primary school, the man who fixes the boats — have all moved out of town, because no landlord keeps a tenant at N$4,500 when tourists pay N$1,900 a night for the same rooms. The primary school has lost a teaching post this year: enrolment is down, because families with children are the families leaving.

The fishmonger did not close because nobody bought fish. He closed because his rent tripled, and the fish he was licensed to sell could not be sold at coffee-bar prices. The coffee bar, I should say, is excellent, and the young couple who run it are lovely, and they have also moved out of town, because they cannot live here either.

The town has not decided anything. That is my complaint, and it is not a small one. The harbour expanded, the cruise schedule tripled, and the short-let platforms arrived — three waves, no plan — and what we have now is the result of nobody deciding anything. The municipality has a tourism strategy, which is a document about growing tourism. It has no housing strategy, no rates plan for long-term tenants, and no answer to the question that the teacher asked at the meeting in the hall: who is this town for?

I do not think we are the first town in the world to face this, which is why I have stopped being interested in whether the surge is "good" or "bad". It is a tide. The question — the only one worth a column — is what a town does while the tide is in.

Other towns have done things. Some have capped short-lets to a fixed number of licences. Some have required that a share of new builds be long-term. Some have taxed the weekend and subsidised the winter, so that the surge pays for the season it disrupts. Every one of those decisions was unpopular with somebody, which is how you know something was actually decided.

I watched the conference end with applause for the occupancy figure, and I walked home past three short-lets and one lit window, and I thought: the town we are selling to the visitors is the town that cannot house the people who would make it worth visiting.

The tide is in. Build the sea wall, or move the village — but choose, because the water is rising either way.
