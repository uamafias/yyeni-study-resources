A BOOK IN EVERY CLASSROOM

Give a classroom a bookcase, and you change it for thirty years.

In the north of this country, 62 per cent of rural primary classrooms have no reading book that a child can take home. Not a library — a book. The regional figures for reading level at Grade 5 are the figures you would expect from that number, and we are not going to hide them behind the word "challenge": they are the lowest in the country, and the gap between them and the capital is the widest it has been since measurement began.

The Books-in-Classrooms programme puts a case of sixty books into a rural classroom — not a library, because libraries need rooms, staff and towns, and the classrooms we serve have a teacher, a blackboard and a wall. The case lives in the corner. The children take the books home. Sixty books, forty-two children, one year, and at the end of the year the case is counted, refilled and, where it worked, doubled.

We know it works, and we will show you the working. Twelve classrooms received cases in the pilot year, and the reading scores in those classrooms rose by an average of 19 per cent; the twelve comparison classrooms, matched by the university that evaluated us, rose by 4. In the teachers’ own words: "The children argue about the books." That sentence is our favourite data point in three years of running this programme, and we have checked, and it is the arguing that matters: a book argued about is a book read.

N$7,500 fills one case: sixty books, chosen with the regional curriculum office, in the three languages the classrooms actually teach, delivered by our own drivers, because the post does not reach these villages and the book must.

N$150,000 fills the twenty classrooms on our current waiting list. Your name — or the name of someone you are remembering — goes on the case’s brass plate, and every year you receive the reading scores of the classroom you filled, and one letter, chosen by the teacher, from a child who has the book you put in their hands.

The case is built for a corner, not a museum, and everything about it was decided by asking teachers what fails: the books are paperback, because hardbacks do not survive the walk home; they are in the three languages the classrooms actually teach, because a book in a language a child does not read is furniture; and they come with a simple repair kit, because a torn page mended in the classroom by a nine-year-old is a book kept, while a torn page sent away is a book lost.
We know the limits of what we do, and we will say them before you find them: sixty books is not a library, a year is not a childhood, and 19 per cent is not a rescue. But the corner changes the room. The teacher of pilot classroom four wrote to us in March to say the children had reorganised the chairs to sit closer to the case, and that she had not asked them to, and that is the whole programme, described once, by someone with no budget to describe it.
We are not asking for a library. We are asking for a corner, a case and an argument.

Give a bookcase. Change a room. The children are already waiting to argue about what you send.
