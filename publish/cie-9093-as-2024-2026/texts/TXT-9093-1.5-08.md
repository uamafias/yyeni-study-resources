A NIGHT AT THE OMURAMBA MUSEUM — REVIEWED FROM THE DARK

The new science museum on the Omuramba road promised "an evening where the exhibits come to life", and I want to open this review with fairness, because the next three paragraphs are going to sound like a complaint: they lit the building beautifully, they hired actors with real training, and my ten-year-old had a wonderful time. Hold all of that. Here is the rest.

The evening costs N$350 for adults and N$180 for children over six, and it runs from seven to ten on the first Friday of the month. The concept is sound: actors stationed through the galleries as historical scientists, the lights low, the exhibits — skeletons, early radios, a very fine Foucault pendulum — operating at full effect. My son spent forty minutes with a young woman playing a 1920s radio engineer, who let him solder a (safe, simulated, cleverly designed) joint, and he talked about it for the rest of the weekend. That engineer was worth the ticket price, and the museum should find out her name and thank her properly.

Now the problems, in the order we met them. The first is the sound design, which is a phrase I use loosely: the soundtrack of the evening is a loop of what I think was meant to be dramatic orchestral music, played at a volume that turns conversation into shouting and galleries into queues of people mouthing "WHAT". By eight o’clock my son and the radio engineer were discussing solder through mime, which is not a sentence I expected to write. The pendulum room — the museum’s best space, and the reason we came — had music in it, and a pendulum swinging in silence is worth twenty pendulums swinging over strings.

A word about the ticketing, because it explains the queues: the evening is sold as one price for everything inside, which means everyone wants everything, and the planetarium — one room, one show, ninety seats — becomes the bottleneck the whole evening pours through. Two shows would fix it; three would end the queue; the museum runs one, and the reason, a steward told me, is the actor’s union rules about evening work, which is the first time I have sympathised with a union in a review.
The second problem is the queue for the planetarium, which was forty minutes at 19:15 and had become a rumour by the time we reached the front of it. The planetarium show itself, when we got in, was excellent, and the presenter — a working astronomer doing a shift for reasons of love — answered my son’s question about Venus with more patience than Venus deserved.

The third problem is the food, which I mention only because the museum mentions it: "dining under the dinosaurs" is a phrase on the brochure that in practice means a food truck outside, which is fine, but the queue was fifty minutes and the dinosaurs were inside, and the physics of that arrangement does not work.

So: the exhibits, the actors and the astronomer were worth the money, the sound was worth complaining about, and the queue was worth leaving early for. My son gives it eleven stars out of five, because he is ten. I give it three and a half out of five, and a note for the museum: you built a beautiful thing, and then you scored it. The next first Friday, turn the music off in the pendulum room for one hour. You will not lose the drama. The drama is a pendulum, proving the earth turns, to a child who did not know it did.
