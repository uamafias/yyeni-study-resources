## Binary shifts and bit manipulation

### Binary shifts: logical, arithmetic and cyclic

A shift moves a register's bits sideways, and what happens at the ends decides which kind it is. A logical shift moves every bit the stated number of places: the bits that fall off the end are lost, and zeros enter at the other end. Left and right do arithmetic: a logical left shift by one place doubles an unsigned value, and a logical right shift by one place halves it, discarding the remainder - which is why shifts are the cheap way to scale by powers of two. A cyclic shift bends the register into a ring: the bits that fall off one end re-enter at the other, so no bits are lost and nothing new enters. An arithmetic shift is a logical shift that keeps the sign bit: on a right shift, the sign bit is copied into the vacated positions, so a negative value stays negative and halving a negative gives a negative. Three names to keep straight, and the ends are what tell them apart: bits lost with zeros entering (logical), bits travelling round (cyclic), the sign copied in (arithmetic, right).

### Worked example: one byte, four shifts

Take the register 1011 0110, unsigned value 182, through the shifts. Logical left by one: every bit moves left, the leading 1 falls off and is lost, and a 0 enters at the right: 0110 1100, value 108 - the doubling went past the register's width (182 doubled would be 364), so the lost leading 1 is the overflow and the register keeps the low eight bits of the doubled value. Logical right by one: every bit moves right, the trailing 0 falls off, a 0 enters at the left: 0101 1011, value 91, which is 182 halved. Arithmetic right by one, read as two's complement: the value is -128 + 64 + 16 + 4 + 2 = -42, and the shift copies the sign bit 1 into the vacated left place: 1110 1011, which is -128 + 64 + 32 + 8 + 2 + 1 = -21, so the negative value has halved and stayed negative. Cyclic left by one: the leading 1 falls off the left end and re-enters at the right: 0110 1101 - the same eight bits, rearranged, nothing lost.

### Masks for monitoring and control

A device's port packs several lines into one byte, and bit manipulation is how a program reads and drives one line at a time. A bit mask is a pattern of bits with 1s in the positions of interest and 0s elsewhere. To test a line, AND the port byte with a mask holding a single 1: AND gives 1 only where both operands have 1, so the chosen bit passes through and every other bit becomes 0 - a result of zero says the line was 0, a nonzero result says it was 1. To set a line, OR the command byte with a mask holding a 1 in that line's position: OR forces the masked bit to 1 whatever it held and leaves every other bit unchanged, so the device's line switches on with the rest of the port untouched. Masks are written as values: the mask 0000 1000 that tests bit 3 is denary 8, and a mask of eight 1s, 1111 1111, is denary 255 - a mask written from its binary pattern must be converted before it goes into an immediate operand.

### Common errors

Three slips recur. Giving a cyclic shift for a logical one: a logical shift discards the bits that fall off and fills with zeros, and the test is that bits reappear at the other end only in a cyclic shift. Forgetting the sign on an arithmetic right shift: the sign bit is copied into the vacated positions, and the test is that an arithmetic result for a negative value must stay negative. And writing a mask of eight 1s as #1: eight 1s make denary 255, so the immediate operand is #255 - the test is to convert the mask before writing it with #.
