## Ethics, professional bodies and copyright

### Why ethics matters to a computing professional

Ethics is the set of moral principles that guides how a person acts, and a computing professional works where those principles are load-bearing. The law sets a floor - theft, fraud, misuse are crimes - but most of the professional's day sits above that floor: whether to disclose a weakness a client would rather hide, how much care the users' data is owed, whether to ship a system the team doubts. A code of ethics names those choices and sets a standard above the law's floor, which is the first need it answers. The second need is standing. The public meets computing through its professionals, and one misused trust is remembered against all of them, so the profession polices itself before anyone else has to. That is what a professional body is for: the BCS (British Computer Society) and the IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronic Engineers) publish codes of conduct their members agree to follow, so joining one commits the professional to a written standard and signals to employers and clients that here is someone held to it - with the body's guidance to lean on when a decision is hard, which is the third need: hard decisions are lighter with a rule to lean on.

### Acting ethically or unethically: what follows

Every ethical choice in computing lands on three circles: the people the work affects, the client or employer, and the profession. Take the unethical side first, because its chain runs furthest, and its first link is ordinary: most unethical acts in computing begin not with a villain but with a shortcut - the corner cut quietly, the doubt unspoken, the data borrowed ‘just to check’ - which is why a code speaks to ordinary days and not only dramatic ones. A developer copies user data from a client's system: the users' privacy is breached and their trust is broken; the client's confidence collapses and the client warns others; the developer's name and the profession's standing fall together, because the public keeps one memory for all professionals. A house finds a safety flaw in a hospital system and stays silent: the patients carry the risk, the hospital believes in a system that fails, and the profession's word is worth less the next time. The ethical side runs its own chain, and it runs further. Disclosure of the flaw mends it before harm, so the patients are safe, the hospital keeps faith with the house, and the profession's standing is upheld - which is why ethical behaviour is not a cost but an investment: trust is what lets users supply honest data and rely on systems built with care, and trust is spent by the one and earned by the many.

### Why copyright legislation is needed

Software is a work made by someone, and copyright legislation gives that someone the rights of a maker: the legal right to control how the work is copied, adapted and distributed, so a program belongs to its maker unless the right is assigned away, exactly as a book belongs to its author. The need is sharpened by what software is: a work that can be copied exactly, at almost no cost, with no loss to the copy. A novelist's manuscript is safe in a drawer; a finished program can be taken in a moment and shared with the world, so without the right to control copying, a creator's work could be taken and sold by anyone and creating would stop paying - which is the need the law answers. The right holds at every size of maker: a lone developer's program is as much their work as a firm's, and the lone developer has less behind them to absorb the loss, so the law a firm can lean on is the law a lone developer lives by. Two words to keep straight: copyright is the right; plagiarism and copying without permission are acts against it. The legislation earns its keep at both ends of the market it protects: the maker of a popular teaching program and the maker of a niche tool for one clinic both live by the same right to refuse a copy, because the right is what turns making into a living - and the user’s side of the same law is the licence, the maker’s statement of what may be done, which is the next unit’s whole subject.

### Common errors

The recurring slip is defining copyright by its breach. Copyright is the legal right of the creator to control copying and use of their work; copying without permission and plagiarism are acts against that right, not what the right is. The test: is the answer about the right, or about breaking it? The right is copyright; the breach has other names.

## Software licences and Artificial Intelligence

### The licence types

Copyright belongs to the maker by default, and a software licence is the maker's statement of what a user may do. Four types cover the field. Free software, in the Free Software Foundation's sense, gives freedom rather than a price: the licence grants the freedom to run the software for any purpose, to study how it works, to change it and to share it and share changes, so the source code comes with it. Open-source software, in the Open Source Initiative's sense, makes the source available under terms that allow use, change and redistribution - the practical terms overlap with free software's, while the stated philosophy differs: free software speaks of users' rights as a principle, open-source speaks of a way of building good software. Shareware is free to try: the user installs and uses it for a trial period, and payment is expected if it is kept, with the source typically closed, so the trying is of the finished tool. Commercial software is sold for a fee with the source kept closed: the vendor's business is the software itself, and the licence permits use, not inspection or redistribution. Freeware stands outside the list as a warning: software free of charge is not the same as free software, because freeware carries no right to see or change the source - 'free' in free software is freedom, not a price.

### Justifying a licence for a situation

The licence is justified by what the situation needs, and two questions do the matching. Who can maintain the system? A school with keen programmers and no budget takes free or open-source software for its website: the source is available to change and share, the programmers restyle and extend it themselves, and no licence fee weighs on the club-rate budget. A firm with a payroll system and no software department takes commercial software: the vendor holds the system under a support contract, so a fault at month end is the vendor's paid problem, and someone is answerable for the mending. What must the software answer to? Work that must be inspected - a public body's counting system, say - leans towards the open licence, because anyone can check the code. Each licence's weakness is the other's strength: open upkeep rests on the user's skills or the community's goodwill, so a small business without those skills inherits the desk the licence leaves the work on; commercial mending comes under a contract and is charged for, so the firm pays in fees what the school pays in skills. Shareware fills the small-tool gap: try the tool on the real job, pay if it stays.

### Artificial Intelligence

Artificial Intelligence is the making of systems that perform tasks that normally need human intelligence: recognising speech and images, translating between languages, learning from data, making judgements. The applications are ordinary now - a phone that turns speech into text, a clinic system that flags suspicious images for a clinician to examine, a route planner that reads traffic - and each application leans on the human around it, which is where the ethics enters. An AI system's judgement affects people: who gets flagged, what a diagnosis suggests, whose face a camera names, and a judgement made by a system is still a professional's act somewhere, so the responsibility does not disappear into the software. The impact runs on three sides. Social: decisions that affect people are made faster and at scale, so the system's fairness matters to many people at once. Economic: work shifts - fewer hours reading every image, more hours on the flagged ones - so some work displaces while new work appears, and the professional's own judgement is part of what is redistributed. Environmental: the systems that learn from large amounts of data are built and run with power and resources, so the asking price of the judgements includes a resource bill. The professional's question is the whole of it: not only whether the system works, but whom it works on and what it costs.

### Common errors

The recurring slip is flattening free software into freeware. Freeware is free of charge, with the source closed and no right to change it; free software (Free Software Foundation) carries the freedom to run, study, change and share the source, whatever it costs. The test: can you see and change the source code? Then it is free software, not just freeware.
