## Effective communication, methods and choosing between them

### Communication and effective communication

Communication is the transfer of a message from a sender to a receiver. In business the sender and receiver may be two people, a manager and a workforce, or a business and its customers. Communication is effective when the receiver receives the message, understands it as the sender intended, and can act on it; a message that is sent but never understood has not communicated anything.

Because understanding is the test, effective communication is a two-way process. Feedback - the receiver's reply, question or action that shows what was understood - is what tells the sender whether the message arrived as intended.

### Internal and external communication

Internal communication is communication inside the business, between managers and employees or between one department and another: briefings, team meetings, memos and an intranet. External communication is communication between the business and the outside world: customers, suppliers, lenders, government and the local community, reached through letters, calls, advertising and the business's public pages.

The boundary is membership: a message that crosses the business's walls is external. The two need different treatment because the stakes differ. Internal messages shape coordination, safety and motivation among people who share the business's goals; external messages shape the business's image and sales among people who owe it nothing, and a poorly judged external message can travel to readers the sender never imagined.

### Why effective communication matters

Every business activity depends on instructions, requests and information reaching the right people in a usable form. Where communication works, employees know what to do and why, so errors are fewer and motivation tends to be higher because people feel informed rather than ignored; coordination between departments runs smoother, saving time and materials.

Where it fails, the costs are real: workers build the wrong thing, buyers order the wrong quantity, customers hear about offers too late. Ineffective communication is a standing cause of waste, accidents and lost sales, and the larger the business, the more layers a message must cross and the more places it can break.

### The methods a business can use

The methods divide into spoken, written-paper, visual and IT-based groups. Spoken (verbal) communication includes face-to-face conversation, meetings, telephone calls and video calls: one or more people talking, with immediate response possible. Written communication on paper includes notices, letters, memos and reports. Visual communication carries meaning through pictures rather than words alone - a diagram, a floor plan, a safety sign. IT-based methods transmit the message through digital technology: email, instant messaging, staff intranets, a business's website and its social media pages.

The groups differ along features that decide which to reach for: how fast the message travels, what it costs, whether it leaves a record, and whether the receiver can respond at once.

### What the methods do well

Spoken methods suit complex or sensitive messages, because tone and body language carry meaning and questions are answered at once; a walk to a machine teaches a safety rule faster than a paragraph can. Written methods leave a record: a memo can be reread, checked and filed, which is why agreements and instructions that matter tend to be written down. IT-based methods add speed and reach: an email crosses the world in seconds at almost no extra cost per message, a group message reaches a whole workforce at once, and both leave a timestamped record that can be checked later if a dispute arises.

Because IT-based methods are fast and cheap per message, most businesses now run routine internal and external traffic through them, reserving the spoken channel for what needs a human voice.

### What the methods do badly

No method is free of cost or risk. Spoken methods leave no record unless someone writes them down, and a meeting of many people is slow and expensive in wages; a message passed along a chain of people can also distort at each step. Paper-written methods take time to produce and deliver, and the receiver cannot ask the page a question. IT-based methods depend on equipment, electricity and connectivity, so a power cut or a dead network ends communication; they also assume the receiver reads them - an unread email has communicated nothing - and they carry security risks, because a message can be intercepted, forwarded or faked.

The honest lesson is fit: a fast, cheap method that the audience cannot or will not use is worse than a slower one that reaches them.

### Choosing a method: a worked example

Ongwediva Meat Processors must tell its 26 delivery drivers a change to the morning pickup roster, effective tomorrow. Dispatch knows that 20 drivers carry personal phones and six share a family phone a text reaches, at best, by chance; the six have always taken roster changes by call. Which method should dispatch use?

Work through the features. Speed: the change bites tomorrow, so a posted notice at the depot is too slow as the single channel. Record: a roster change needs a record both sides can check, so a purely spoken call is weak unless confirmed. Feedback: dispatch needs to know each driver has the new roster. Cost: 26 calls cost little; 26 text messages cost less still.

The recommendation: text the new roster to the 20 drivers who use personal phones and ask each to reply, phone the six who do not, and post a printed copy at the depot as the shared record. The condition that decides it is the audience: because six of the 26 drivers cannot be reached reliably by text, a single channel would leave them with yesterday's roster and the business with missed pickups. A Norwegian courier firm facing the same choice with drivers who all use an app could sensibly choose the single text channel - the circumstances, not the method, decide.

### The criteria that decide a recommendation

A justified recommendation names the circumstance and matches the method to it. The criteria worth weighing: who the audience is and what channels it can use; how urgent the message is; whether a record is needed; whether the message is sensitive, where tone and the chance to ask questions matter; what the method costs per receiver; and whether the sender needs proof the message was understood.

The recommendation then does three things: it states the choice, it weighs the decisive criterion against the strongest alternative - the case against the choice - and it names the condition under which the choice holds, such as the audience actually using the channel.

### Two errors to keep apart

Two confusions recur. First: "the message was sent, so the communication was done." In fact sending is one step; the message has communicated only when the receiver has understood it, which is why feedback is the test. Second: "the fastest method is the best method." Speed is one criterion among several; a same-day text to customers without smartphones communicates nothing, and a sensitive dismissal delivered by text strips out the dignity a face-to-face meeting allows. The test in both cases: ask what the receiver gets, not what the sender did or how quickly.

## Communication barriers and how to remove them

### Communication barriers

A communication barrier is anything that stops a message reaching its receiver, or distorts it so that it is not understood as the sender intended. The barrier may sit with the sender, the channel, the receiver or the surroundings.

Keep barriers apart from the objectives of a communication: the objectives - informing, instructing, persuading - are the results the message is meant to achieve; a barrier is an obstacle in the way of those results. The test: is the thing named the intended result, or what prevents it?

### How barriers arise and what they cost

Barriers arise in recognisable ways: jargon the receiver has not learned, more information than anyone can absorb, a channel the audience does not use, background noise, a language the receiver does not share, and messages passed through many hands that distort at each step.

Follow one chain. Where a supervisor hands new night-shift staff a work order full of abbreviations, the staff guess at the meaning - the barrier acting. Because they guess, wrong quantities are picked and a batch must be reworked - the consequence. The rework consumes wages and materials, and a delayed order costs reputation and sales too.

### Reducing and removing barriers

The cures follow the causes. Choose the channel for the audience, not the sender's convenience. Write plain language and spell out the jargon a message cannot avoid. Keep instructions short and number their steps. Ask for feedback - a receiver repeating the instruction back, a reply confirming receipt - because feedback is the only proof of understanding. Train new staff in the channels the business uses, and arrange translation where the workforce needs it.

A German engineering firm that runs a five-minute spoken handover between shifts, with a written log both supervisors sign, removes the barrier of the unread board: two channels, one message, each covering the other's weakness.

### Barrier or objective?

The pair to keep apart: the objectives of a communication are the results it is meant to achieve - to inform, to instruct, to persuade. The barriers are the obstacles in the way of those results. The test: does the statement name the intended result, or what prevents it? "Poor network coverage on the delivery route" is a barrier; "telling customers about the recall" is an objective it obstructs.
