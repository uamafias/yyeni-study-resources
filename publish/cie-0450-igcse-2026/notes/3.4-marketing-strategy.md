## Marketing strategy for a given situation

### The mix and the consumer

The marketing mix is the blend of four controllable elements - product, price, place and promotion - with which a business influences the customers, actual and potential, for what it sells. Topic 3.3 teaches each element in detail; this topic asks a sharper question: in a given situation, which element does the most work on the consumer’s decision?

Each element reaches the buyer through its own mechanism. The product works through fitness for the buyer’s need - a backpack that tears on the first trail fails its buyer whatever it costs. Price works through what the buyer is willing and able to pay. Place works through how easily the buyer can get the product. Promotion works through whether the buyer notices the product and knows what it offers.

### Which element matters most here

Which element most influences consumer decisions differs with circumstances, and the test is to ask which weakness would break the purchase. Take a new energy drink launched to Windhoek shoppers. Condition: nobody knows the drink, so the breaking weakness is attention - a drink no shopper notices stays on the shelf whatever it costs. Mechanism: promotion carries the launch, because tasting stands and social-media posts build the awareness the first purchase depends on. Consequence for the business: the promotion budget earns the early sales, and a price cut alone would discount a product nobody has heard of. Reverse the condition - the same drink, now well known, sitting beside three rival brands - and the breaking weakness shifts to place and price, because a shopper who knows the brand tends to take whichever tube is nearest and cheapest. The element that matters most is set by the circumstances, not by the product alone.

### What a marketing strategy is

A marketing strategy is a business’s plan for using the elements of the marketing mix together to reach a chosen target market and achieve its marketing objectives. Recommending one in a given situation means more than naming it: the recommendation has to state the choice, the circumstances that make it fit - who the target market is, what the competition is doing, what the business’s objectives and resources allow - and the criterion that decides between it and the alternatives.

### Recommending a strategy, with the case against

Kalahari Kerb runs one food truck selling spicy kapana bowls at a Windhoek taxi rank, and the lunch queue has stopped growing. The owner sees two paths: keep one truck and raise prices, or open a second truck at a petrol station on the Western Bypass and hold prices down. The facts point to a place problem: the queue is full at noon and thin after two o’clock, and social-media followers keep asking for a branch nearer their workplaces, so customers who want the bowls cannot reach them. Recommendation: open the second truck at the Bypass with the same product and the same price, because the criterion that decides is reach - the demand already exists where the truck is not. The case against: two trucks raise the running costs while the Bypass trade is untested, so the recommendation would change if a month’s trial showed the Bypass queue too thin to cover the extra costs. A New Zealand pie chain that faced the same choice rolled out one cart at a time for that reason - each site proved itself before the next opened.

### Two ideas often confused

A question about the roles of marketing is not a question about the four Ps. The roles are what marketing does for the business: identifying customer needs, satisfying them, and raising awareness of the product. The elements - product, price, place, promotion - are the controllable decisions the business blends to do those things. The test that tells them apart: ask whether the statement names a job marketing performs or a decision the business controls. A learner who answers a question about the roles of marketing with the four elements has confused the tool with the purpose, and the same confusion runs the other way when a mix question is answered with jobs instead of elements.

## Legal controls on marketing

### What legal controls are

Legal controls on marketing are laws that restrict what businesses may claim in their promotion and require the goods they sell to be safe, of acceptable quality and as described. Two areas concern marketing most. Misleading promotion: an advert, label or price claim may not deceive consumers about what the product does, what it costs or how its price compares. Faulty and dangerous goods: products may not injure the user, and a product that breaks or burns is in breach of the law whatever its advertising said.

### From rule to strategy

The rule changes the plan, and the chain runs from compliance to cost to customer trust. Condition: a Namibian importer of electric kettles learns that every model must pass a safety test before it may be stocked. Mechanism: testing adds cost and delay to each launch, so the importer’s marketing strategy budgets for it - promotions are scheduled after the certificate arrives, and the safer model’s higher price is explained on the label rather than hidden. Consequence for the business: launches run slower and cost more, but the importer can point to tested products, and shoppers who trust the label tend to keep buying. The chain also runs against the business that gambles: a European toy maker forced to recall a batch with detachable small parts paid for the recall, the lost shelf time and the publicity at once, and the fines were the smallest part of the bill. One boundary to keep: following the law is what is demanded; marketing ethically - such as choosing not to aim sugary-drink adverts at children - is a choice the law may not force.

## New markets in other countries

### What a foreign market is

A foreign market is the group of customers, actual and potential, for a product in a country other than the business’s own; entering it means beginning to sell to customers in that country. A Walvis Bay fish processor selling dried snack bites into Zambian supermarkets has entered the Zambian market - Zambian snack buyers are now its customers, actual and potential. New markets abroad offer growth potential because they add customers beyond the home market’s existing demand. Sales that have stopped growing at home may still grow in a country where the product is new, and selling in several countries can reduce a business’s dependence on any single economy: a drought year that hurts Namibian spending may leave another country’s untouched.

### How much growth is on offer

How much growth a foreign market offers depends on its facts: how many potential customers it holds, what they can afford and are willing to pay, how strong the local competitors are, and how well the product fits what consumers there prefer. Work it through. Condition: a Namibian firm makes sturdy school satchels priced at N$220 and studies the neighbouring markets. Mechanism: in a country with a young school-age population, rising incomes and few local bag makers, the satchels answer a real need that local supply does not; in a country whose schools supply satchels to parents directly, the same product answers no felt need at all. Consequence for the business: the first market offers growth - orders could build on the 1 800 satchels the firm already sells at home each year - while the second offers the costs of entry with little return. The growth potential lives in the market’s facts, and they have to be checked, not assumed.

### Problems at the door

Entering a foreign market runs into problems that selling at home does not. Cultural differences - in language, tastes, religious customs and buying habits - can make a sound product or its promotion misunderstood or unwelcome: a beef snack halts at a vegetarian majority, and a slogan that plays well in one language may insult in translation. Lack of knowledge raises the cost and the risk: a business that does not know the foreign market’s laws, competitors, distribution channels or consumers’ preferences cannot price, place or promote confidently, and works from assumptions instead. Keep the two apart with a test: a cultural difference is a fact about the market that the business must adapt to; a lack of knowledge is a gap in the business that research and time can close. Distance adds more: transport and communication costs tend to run higher on foreign sales, and a change in currency values between agreeing a sale and receiving payment can shrink the N$ the business finally banks.

### Two methods: joint ventures and licensing

Two methods stand out. A joint venture is a shared enterprise in which two or more businesses set up and jointly own a new business, each contributing investment and sharing the risks and the profits. Licensing is an agreement in which the product’s owner grants a producer in the other country the right to make and sell it there, in return for payments - usually a royalty on sales. The boundary between them is ownership: in a joint venture the entrant owns part of the new enterprise; under licensing it grants rights for payment and owns nothing in the foreign market.

### Botswana through either door

Suppose the satchel firm wants Botswana. Through a joint venture with a Gaborone stationery business, the two open a jointly owned shop and workshop: the Namibian firm brings the design and the brand, the partner brings premises, staff and knowledge of local schools, and each shares the investment, the risk and the profit. Through licensing, the firm instead grants a Botswanan manufacturer the right to make and sell its satchels there for a royalty of, say, N$12 on each one: little investment, quick entry, but quality, pricing and the brand’s reputation sit in the licensee’s hands. A Swedish flat-pack furniture business that licensed its name to unfamiliar factories learned the limitation the hard way: shoddy copies carrying its name damaged the brand’s standing abroad while the royalties trickled in.

### Choosing between the methods

Which method fits depends on the circumstances, and a recommendation should name what decides it. How much control matters: if the brand’s quality is the product - hand-finished satchels, a trusted name - the loss of control under licensing weighs heavily, and the joint venture keeps the business’s hands on the work. How much risk and investment the business can carry: licensing needs almost none of the entry cost, while a joint venture shares it with a partner who also shares local knowledge. How quickly entry must happen: licensing tends to reach shelves faster, because the licensee already makes and distributes there. And how much local knowledge the sale needs: the more the product depends on knowing local buyers, the stronger the case for a partner on the ground. The choice depends on which of these weighs most in the given situation, and a recommendation that names no criterion has justified nothing.
