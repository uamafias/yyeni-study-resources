## Why people work, Maslow's hierarchy, Taylor and Herzberg

### Motivation and why people work

Motivation is the willingness of an employee to work hard towards the business's goals because the work, the rewards or the surroundings give the worker a reason to. A motivated worker puts effort and care into the job; an unmotivated one does the least needed to keep it.

Why do people work? The first answer is money: wages and salaries pay for food, rent, school fees and everything else a household needs. Work is how most people finance their needs and wants. But money is rarely the whole reason. People also work for the satisfaction of doing something well, for the company of workmates, for the status a job carries, and for the sense of achievement that comes from meeting a target. A nurse in Keetmanshoop may value the respect of her community as much as her pay slip; a Windhoek apprentice mechanic may prize learning a skill that could lead to a workshop of his own.

The balance shifts with circumstances. Where pay is too low to live on, money dominates. Once pay covers the basics, the non-money reasons grow in weight - which is why two businesses paying the same wage can end up with very different effort from their people.

### What a motivated workforce gives a business

Three measures show whether a workforce is motivated. Labour productivity is the output each worker produces in a given period, such as loaves per baker per day. Absenteeism is the rate at which workers are absent from work when they are expected in, usually counted in days lost. Labour turnover is the rate at which workers leave and have to be replaced, usually written as a percentage of the workforce a year.

A well-motivated workforce tends to do better on all three, because motivated workers care about the outcome of their work. They produce more per shift, they turn up because they want to be there, and they stay because leaving means giving up something they value. The benefits compound: higher output per worker means the cost of producing each unit tends to fall; fewer absences mean fewer shifts run short-handed; fewer leavers mean less spending on advertising vacancies, interviewing and training replacements, and less skill walking out of the door. An unmotivated workforce pushes all three measures the wrong way, and the costs - idle time, mistakes, repeated hiring - arrive long before anyone writes them down.

### Reading the three measures at a real firm

Katima Coffee Roasters employs 40 workers. Last year each worker roasted an average of 50 kg of beans a day; this year the figure is 58 kg, so the same 40 people now produce about 320 kg more a day. Absenteeism has fallen from 8 to 5 days per worker a year, and labour turnover from 15 to 9 per cent, so about four workers had to be replaced this year instead of six.

That pattern is the classic signature of rising motivation. Each measure feeds the others: more presence keeps the roasters running, more staying means the firm keeps its trained hands, and the extra daily output spreads the same wages over more kilograms, so the cost per kilogram tends to fall. The manager who tracks the three measures can see whether a change in pay, job design or supervision actually moved anything - which is what makes them worth recording every period, not once.

### Maslow's hierarchy of human needs

In the 1940s the psychologist Abraham Maslow argued that human needs arrange themselves in a hierarchy: a lower level generally has to be reasonably satisfied before the next level up starts to motivate behaviour. Managers use the idea as a diagnostic tool rather than a law.

The five levels, from the bottom up, are physiological needs such as food, water, warmth and rest; safety needs such as security of income and of person; social needs such as friendship and belonging; esteem needs such as recognition, respect and status; and self-actualisation, the drive to reach one's full potential through challenging work. At work, pay and safe conditions serve the first two levels, workmates and team life serve the third, praise and promotion serve the fourth, and genuinely challenging work serves the fifth.

A Namibian mineworker whose wages barely cover food will be moved by a pay increase; the staff social club may mean little to him yet. A well-paid head-office analyst may be moved less by another N$500 a month than by the chance to lead a project that shows what she can do.

### Using Maslow as a manager

The practical lesson is that a satisfied need no longer motivates. A manager who diagnoses the lowest level that is still unsatisfied can aim rewards there and avoid spending money where it will not move anyone. A worker struggling to feed a family is unlikely to be stirred by a new job title; a worker whose pay is comfortable is unlikely to be stirred by another small allowance. Misdiagnosis wastes money in both directions, and it is the commonest way motivation budgets disappear without a trace.

The theory has limits, and honest use states them. Real people do not climb the levels neatly; some value esteem above comfort, and one person's feeling of belonging is another's distraction. The hierarchy is also hard to test precisely, so it should be used as a checklist of questions - what is unsatisfied for these particular workers? - rather than as a ladder every worker climbs in the same order. Because workers differ in what moves them, the manager benefits from understanding why each one works before choosing any method of motivation: the reward that lifts one worker can leave another untouched.

### Taylor and scientific management

Frederick Taylor, an American engineer working around 1900, watched steelworks and concluded that workers are motivated mainly by money. His recipe: divide the work into simple, repeated tasks; study each task with a stopwatch and teach the one fastest method; and link pay to output, often piece by piece, so more effort earns more pay. His stopwatch studies of men shifting pig iron at Bethlehem Steel in the United States are the classic illustration, and they did raise the tons moved per worker per day.

Taylor was right that pay is a powerful lever, especially where wages are low and effort visibly buys income. The approach has limits, though: it treats boredom and monotony as costs to ignore, it offers nothing to workers whose pay is already adequate, and pushing effort up can push loyalty down. Piece-rate pay can also reward speed at the cost of quality, because the worker who is paid per piece has a reason to make each piece fast rather than each piece good.

### Herzberg: two sets of workplace factors

Frederick Herzberg studied accountants and engineers in the 1950s and found that the factors involved in job attitudes split into two sets. Hygiene factors - pay, working conditions, job security, the way the business is run - behave like plumbing: when they are poor, workers are dissatisfied, but improving them only removes the dissatisfaction; it does not create motivation. Motivators - a sense of achievement, recognition, responsibility, interesting work, personal growth - are what actually generate effort.

The distinction matters because the two sets answer different problems. A pay rise in a workshop whose roof leaks may end the complaints without adding a single extra unit of effort, because it works on the hygiene side only. The test to apply: ask whether a proposed change removes something workers dislike or adds something they value. Removing a disliked thing and adding a valued thing are different operations, and businesses that confuse them spend on pay rises that do not change effort.

### Three confusions to keep apart

First, motivation is not the same as payment. Pay is one influence among several, and its power depends on which needs are already met - a point where learners often wrongly treat a wage rise as the universal answer.

Second, Herzberg is not Maslow. Maslow arranges needs in a ladder and asks which level is unsatisfied; Herzberg splits workplace factors into those that prevent dissatisfaction and those that motivate, and asks whether a change removes a dislike or adds something valued. Related ideas, different questions, different tests.

Third, Taylor is not Herzberg. Taylor puts pay at the centre of motivation; Herzberg found pay mainly a hygiene factor that stops motivating once expectations are met. The test for Taylor: does more effort here mean more pay? The test for Herzberg: is this change fixing a grievance or feeding a motivator?

## Financial and non-financial methods, and recommending in circumstances

### The five financial rewards

A wage is pay by the hour or day for time worked, so it suits work whose hours move about. A salary is a fixed sum per month or year whatever the hours, so it suits stable roles. A bonus is an extra payment for reaching a target, such as a month-end sales figure. Commission is a share of the value of each sale, so the seller's pay rises and falls with what was sold. Profit sharing gives workers a stated share of the business's profits, so every worker gains when the whole business does well.

Each has its place. A Walvis Bay freight forwarder pays commission to its sales staff because selling is what it wants more of. A Keetmanshoop wholesaler might bonus its drivers for a quarter without a damaged delivery, because the target names exactly the behaviour the business needs. The set runs from no link between pay and performance, for a salary, to a tight link, for commission.

### What money can and cannot do

Financial rewards are concrete, easy to compare and directly meet the money needs at the bottom of Maslow's hierarchy, which is why managers reach for them first. Their limits: a rise is quickly accepted as the new normal pay, so its motivational effect tends to fade; expectations ratchet upward and rarely back down; and money says nothing about whether the work itself is satisfying. Herzberg would file pay among the hygiene factors - enough of it prevents dissatisfaction, but a business whose jobs are boring cannot buy its way out of boring.

Used well, money goes where money is the problem: pay that has fallen behind rivals, or a bonus that names a target the workers can actually reach this month. Used badly, it becomes the answer to every complaint, including the ones it cannot reach. A useful habit before approving any financial reward is to ask which need it serves and what will happen once the payment is taken for granted: if the honest answer is that nothing will change, the money is aimed at the wrong problem, and the same budget would buy more as training, variety or recognition.

### The non-financial methods

Job enrichment redesigns a job so it carries more responsibility, challenge and variety - the worker plans and checks the work, not just performs it. Job rotation moves a worker between different tasks on a schedule, so no shift is a full day of the same motion. Teamworking organises a group around a shared goal with shared responsibility for the result. Training raises skills and signals that the business is investing in the person. Opportunities for promotion open a path from the job held to the job wanted.

All five aim at the upper levels of Maslow's hierarchy - esteem and self-actualisation - where pay has little pull, which is why they can move workers a wage rise cannot. Baltyk Kuriery, a bicycle-courier firm in Gdansk, rotated its riders between street delivery, dispatch and loading; fewer riders quit within their first year once no single task filled the day. The methods cost management time and, for training, real money - and they rarely help a worker whose basic pay is inadequate, because esteem does not buy bread.

### From cause to method

Suppose the manager of a Gobabis abattoir finds quality complaints rising and effort slipping. If the cause of the low motivation is monotonous work - most line jobs there are one repeated cut - then a pay rise works the hygiene side and tends to change nothing for long. The mechanism that bites is enrichment and rotation: giving workers responsibility for checking their own output turns a cut into a craft, and the consequence is fewer defects, less supervision and workers prouder of what leaves the floor - so they are likelier to stay.

If instead the exit interviews show workers leaving for better pay at a rival abattoir, the cause is money, and the right first move is financial: bring pay up to the market level before spending anything on job design, because a beautifully redesigned job at a below-market wage still loses its people. The decisive step, either way, is diagnosis before prescription.

### Recommending in given circumstances

A sound recommendation stands on four checks. First, diagnose the cause of the low motivation - low pay, monotonous work, missing recognition or no upward path - because the method must reach the cause to work. Second, weigh the needs of the particular workers, who differ from one another. Third, weigh what the business can afford, now and in every later year the cost recurs. Fourth, check the fit with the rest of the business, such as whether a promotion path exists for the promoted to walk.

Combined methods usually beat single ones: a market-level wage plus enriched jobs plus a visible promotion path addresses hygiene and motivators together, which is what Maslow and Herzberg each point to. The recommendation should carry its conditions - what it assumes, what would change it - and should name the measure that will show whether it worked, such as the three indicators of labour productivity, absenteeism and labour turnover, read again after a stated period. A recommendation that cannot be checked is a guess wearing a suit; one that names its test can be repaired when the measures refuse to move.
