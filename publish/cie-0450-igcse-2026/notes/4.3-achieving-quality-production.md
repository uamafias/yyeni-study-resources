## Quality, quality control and quality assurance

### What quality means

Quality is the degree to which a product meets the customer's expectations. The customer judges a product on fitness for purpose - does the gate hold, does the bread taste fresh - measured against the price paid: expectations rise with price, so a cheap product that does its basic job is a quality product, while a shoddy expensive one is not. Quality is decided by the customer, not by the producer: a Windhoek panel beater may believe a repair is well done, but if the customer expected an invisible finish and can see the weld, the job failed the quality test. That is why businesses talk about meeting a specification: the written standard the product must satisfy, drawn from what customers expect.

### Why quality matters to every business

Start from the condition: a business produces output that keeps meeting what its customers expect. The mechanism is that satisfied customers come back and tell others, so repeat sales and word-of-mouth tend to hold revenue up. The consequence is a stronger market position: steady revenue and a reputation that supports the price.

Run the chain the other way and the costs appear. Where output misses expectations, customers are likely to drift to rivals, so revenue falls, and shoddy output must be reworked or scrapped - lost revenue and extra repair cost, twice over. Profit still depends on what the quality costs to achieve, so quality is pursued to the point where meeting expectations costs less than failing them. And every type of business faces the same test - manufacturer, retailer, bank - because in each case the customer asks: did I get what I paid for?

### Quality control

Quality control is the checking of finished output against a standard. It is performed at the end of the production process, usually by appointed inspectors whose job is to examine completed products and separate the defective from the acceptable, so that faults are caught before the goods reach the customer.

A business implements quality control in three steps. It sets a standard: the specification each finished unit must meet, such as a maximum weight tolerance. It inspects finished output against that standard, either every unit or a sample, at the end of the line. It then disposes of failures: units that miss the standard are rejected, reworked if repair is worthwhile, or scrapped. The action happens after production, which is the defining feature of the method.

### Worked example: control at Keetmanshoop Bolt Works

Keetmanshoop Bolt Works makes steel bolts. Its standard: every bolt must carry the correct thread and no cracks. Implementation: an inspector examines finished bolts at the end of the line each afternoon. Last month the line produced 6 000 bolts and inspection found 300 with damaged threads, which is 300 out of 6 000, or 5 per cent of finished output. Those 300 were re-threaded; 20 beyond repair were scrapped.

The figures show the method's strength and its cost. Strength: 280 defective bolts were stopped before shipping. Cost: every one of the 300 faulty bolts had already absorbed its full share of steel and machine time before inspection spotted it, so the firm paid to make defects and then paid again to repair them. When a stage near the start drifts out of adjustment, a whole day's output can be faulty before the end-of-line inspection catches it, so the firm also samples each morning, catching a drifting stage within hours.

### Quality assurance

Quality assurance is the prevention of quality problems across the whole production process, built on the commitment of every worker to quality at every stage. Instead of catching defects at the end, the business arranges its process so that defects are unlikely to occur at all, and treats quality as the responsibility of everyone who touches the product.

A business can implement quality assurance through methods such as these. Quality circles: small groups of workers meet regularly to discuss quality problems in their own area and propose solutions, drawing on the people closest to the work. Training: workers are taught to do their stage correctly and to check their own output as they go, so each stage hands on work that already conforms. Quality systems: documented procedures that set how each stage must be performed and recorded, so the process is repeatable and auditable.

### Worked example: assurance at Swakopmund Seafoods

Swakopmund Seafoods packs frozen fish for export to a Norwegian wholesaler. On a prevention-first system, quality is arranged into the process rather than left to a final check. Implementation: each packer is trained to reject damaged fish at the packing stage and to check the seal of every tray before it moves on; a quality circle of six packers meets each week to review complaints and adjust the handling routine; the cold-store log records temperature at each stage so a drift is visible within the day. A German kitchen-appliance maker runs the same logic: any worker who sees a defect halts the line rather than letting the fault travel downstream.

The effect at the packers: rejection of whole export batches is rare, because faults are prevented or caught at the stage where they arise - an error stopped at packing has absorbed some materials and labour, an error found in a sealed batch has absorbed all of them. The cost sits at the start: training and the weekly circle hour are paid before any saving appears, which is why smaller firms sometimes delay the switch and why the return depends on the fault rate the old system was allowing through.

### Quality control and quality assurance side by side

Quality control checks finished output at the end of production against a standard, and the checking is usually done by appointed inspectors. Quality assurance works across the whole production process, preventing faults at each stage through the commitment of every worker. The boundary between them is where the quality effort sits: after the product is made, or before and during its making. The test: ask who acts and when - inspectors sorting finished units is control; trained workers and quality circles stopping faults during production is assurance.

The two share an aim - the customer receives output that meets expectations - and a business may run both together, prevention during production plus a final check before dispatch. Because a defect caught at the end has already absorbed full materials and labour, assurance tends to cost less per fault prevented than control costs per fault caught - though assurance carries its own training costs, so the cheaper approach depends on the fault rate and how far the workers are trained already.

### The confusion the papers see: control is not assurance

Candidates often write about these two ideas as if they were interchangeable, and lose the marks that turn on the difference. The error is naming quality control where the question asks for quality assurance, or describing an inspection of finished goods as a prevention system. In fact, if the activity happens after production to products already made, it is control; if it changes how production happens so faults are prevented at each stage, it is assurance. The test: ask when the quality effort occurs and who performs it. A business can have both; a sentence that calls a final inspection a quality assurance system confuses the pair.
