## Product decisions: new products, brand, packaging, the life cycle

### Why develop new products

New product development is the work a business does to create a product new to its range or new to its market - an improved version of something it already sells, or a product it has never sold before. The benefits are real: a fresh product gives customers a new reason to buy, because tastes and fashions move and a range that stands still loses attention; and a genuinely different product sets the business apart from rivals, because buyers who want what the new product does must come to it. A Swakopmund juice maker that adds a baobab flavour to its line gives shelf browsers something new to notice; a Norwegian salmon smoker that develops ready-to-eat lunch packs reaches buyers who would rarely buy a whole side of fish.

The limitations sit close beside the benefits. Development is paid for up front - ingredients, testing, redesigns and launch promotion all cost money before the first sale - and the demand is unproven: until customers vote with their wallets, the business cannot be sure they want the new product at all, and a product that fails leaves the development money spent with nothing coming back. Sensible managers treat new product development as an investment with uncertainty attached, not as a guaranteed route to growth.

Weighing the two sides is a judgement about the business's position. A business with cash reserves, a loyal customer base and a clear view of what its customers want can absorb a failed launch; a business with none of those three is betting its survival on unproven demand. That is why the decision is usually made on evidence - what the market research in topic 3.1 found, what rivals have managed to sell, and what the development will cost against what the business can fund - rather than on enthusiasm for the idea itself.

### Brand image and what it does

Brand image is the set of ideas, feelings and expectations customers hold about a product or business - the reputation attached to the name in the customer's mind. It is built slowly, by consistent quality, consistent promotion and a consistent look, and once built it belongs to the business like any other asset.

Its effect on sales runs through risk. A buyer choosing between a known name and an unfamiliar one takes a smaller chance on the known name, so a recognised and trusted brand tends to sell more easily, at a higher price, with less persuasion needed. Its effect on loyalty is the same force working twice: a satisfied buyer re-chooses the name that satisfied them, and loyal buyers tend to stay with the brand even when a rival undercuts its price by a little, because they weigh quality and habit against the small price gap.

The costs of building an image are easy to overlook. Consistent quality, promotion and packaging over years cost money, and one widely-shared failure - a bad batch, a public complaint - can damage in days an image that took years to build. An image is an asset the business keeps maintaining, not a project it finishes.

### Packaging at work

Packaging is the material that contains and protects a product - the wrapper, box, bottle or packet the product is sold in. Protection is its base job: a sealed, cushioned pack means the product reaches the customer unbroken and uncontaminated. But the pack does more work than that. It carries information - contents, weight, how to use it; it presents the brand name at the point of sale, where the customer actually decides; and a well-designed pack can persuade on its own, because the pack is often the closest advertisement the product has.

Consider two Windhoek businesses. A muesli producer packs in plain plastic bags: cheap, but the bags crush in transit and show no brand on the shelf. Moving to printed, resealable pouches costs more per unit, yet the pack now survives the trip, states the contents, and carries the brand at the moment of choice. A craft brewery switches to sturdier bottles with a clear label: fewer breakages on delivery, and the label does the persuading where the sale happens. The limits: better packs cost more per unit, and wasted packaging can put environmentally-conscious customers off, so the business weighs the gain in sales against the cost and the waste before choosing the pack.

### The product life cycle and its stages

The product life cycle is the pattern of sales a product passes through from its development to its final withdrawal, drawn as a curve of sales against time. The main stages: development, where the product is designed and tested and sales are zero because nothing is for sale yet; introduction, where sales begin slowly because few customers know the product; growth, where sales rise quickly as awareness spreads and word carries the product further; maturity, where sales flatten because most of the customers willing to buy it already have it and rivals crowd in; and decline, where sales fall as tastes and technology move on. A Namibian example: a reusable-sanitary-pad producer in Ongwediva, or a Danish firm that made single-use plastic straws and watched demand fall as regulations and customer attitudes changed.

Extension strategies are the actions taken at maturity to push decline further away: an updated version, new packaging, a new market segment, a price promotion on the established product. The logic: a familiar product given a fresh reason to buy recovers attention, so sales hold up instead of sliding. The condition: the development and promotion an extension needs are paid up front, so a strategy the customers reject spends money and speeds the decline it was meant to delay.

### Reading and drawing the life cycle diagram

The product life cycle diagram is drawn with sales on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. To draw one, label both axes first, then sketch the curve: flat at zero through development, low through introduction, climbing steeply through growth, flattening at the peak through maturity, and falling through decline. Label each stage along the curve. To interpret one, compare what the curve does in one stage against what it does in the next: sales that have flattened after a steep climb mean maturity, which means rivals, price pressure and a decision about extension; sales that are falling mean decline, which means either withdraw the product or spend on an extension and accept the risk.

The meaning of the shape is what matters: the diagram is a decision map, not a picture. Where the product sits tells the business what to do next with promotion, price and development.

### How the stage guides the marketing decisions

The marketing mix is the combination of decisions a business makes about product, price, place and promotion. The stage a product has reached influences those decisions, and the chain runs from stage to fact to decision. Condition: at introduction, almost no customer knows the product exists. Mechanism: promotion must therefore inform - make the product known and explain what it does - because persuasion is wasted on someone who has not heard of the product; and the price is set high to take a margin from the earliest buyers, or set low to build share quickly. Condition: at maturity, by contrast, awareness is already built and rivals sell close substitutes. Mechanism: promotion shifts to reminders and loyalty, keeping the established name in front of buyers who know it, and pricing turns competitive, standing near the rivals' level because a wide price gap sends a comparing customer elsewhere. Consequence: the business spends its money where the stage needs it, and the objective - profit and survival - is served by decisions matched to the facts of the stage.

Reading the stage correctly matters because decisions suited to one stage fit another badly: launch-scale spending on a declining product wastes money, and cutting the price of a growing product gives away margin the demand would have carried.

## Price: methods, recommendations and how demand responds

### Five pricing methods and their trade-offs

Price is the amount of money a customer is asked to pay for one unit of a product; of the four marketing mix elements it is the one that brings revenue in rather than costing the business money. The main pricing methods, each with its benefit and its limitation:

Cost-plus pricing works out the full cost of one unit and adds a percentage mark-up on top. Its benefit: the business knows every sale covers its cost, and the arithmetic is simple. Its limitation: the price may sit far above or below what customers will pay, because the customer's willingness is nowhere in the sum.

Competitive pricing sets the price at or near the level rivals charge for similar products, because buyers can see the going rate and compare. Its benefit: the price stays inside the range customers expect. Its limitation: a business with higher costs than its rivals can be squeezed towards a loss, and the price tells the market nothing special about the product.

Penetration pricing sets a deliberately low launch price to win market share quickly. Its benefit: customers arrive fast, and rivals have little margin to chase. Its limitation: the low price may not cover costs, and a later price rise can drive away the very customers the low price won.

Price skimming sets a high launch price for a new product with no direct rival, taking a high margin from the customers who want to be first. Its benefit: development costs are recovered quickly from buyers who pay for newness. Its limitation: the high price invites rivals in, and the price typically has to come down as they arrive.

Promotional pricing offers a temporary price reduction - a money-off week, a two-for-one offer - rather than changing the standing price. Its benefit: a short, visible lift in sales. Its limitation: the lift fades when the offer ends, and buyers can be trained to wait for offers instead of buying at the standing price.

### Choosing a method in given circumstances

Choosing a pricing method means fitting the method to the circumstances, because the right price depends on facts about the market, not on the business's preference. The facts to check: what the product's costs are, how many rivals sell close substitutes, how new the product is, and how strong the brand is.

Worked cases. A Gobabis leather workshop launches a phone-charging solar stand no rival in the region yet sells: skimming fits, because early adopters are likely to pay for newness, and the high launch margin repays the development work before imitators arrive. A Windhoek bakery sells bread against three other bakeries on the same streets: competitive pricing fits, because buyers see all four prices daily and a gap sends them next door. A new car-wash app enters a city where customers already use two established apps: penetration pricing fits, because a low launch price gives trying customers a cheap reason to switch, and share is worth more at the start than margin is.

The recommendation always carries a condition. A skim price holds only while no close rival exists; a penetration price works only if the business can survive the low-margin period and raise the price later without losing the share it won.

### How strongly demand responds to price

Customers respond to a price change, and how strongly they respond is the decisive fact in pricing. Demand for a product is price elastic when the quantity demanded responds more strongly than the price change that caused it, so a small price rise can lose a large share of sales. Demand is price inelastic when the quantity demanded responds less strongly than the price change, so a price rise loses few sales. The boundary between the two is the buyer's alternatives: where close substitutes are easy to buy, demand runs elastic, because customers simply switch; where the product is needed, rare or habitual, demand runs inelastic, because customers keep buying through a rise.

The significance in pricing decisions: raising the price of a product whose demand is price inelastic tends to raise revenue, because sales barely fall while each remaining sale earns more; raising the price of a product whose demand is price elastic tends to reduce revenue, because the sales lost outweigh the higher price each remaining sale earns. A Keetmanshoop fuel station has little pricing freedom on a brand-name product buyers need weekly - inelastic - so the price moves little; a stall selling identical craft bracelets beside five other stalls faces elastic demand, so a small rise sends buyers one stall along. Price and cost stay different figures in all of this: price is what the customer pays, cost is what the business pays to make and sell one unit, and a price set below cost loses money on every sale.

### Telling the pricing methods apart

Three pairs of pricing methods are easily confused, and each pair has a test that separates it.

Skimming and low pricing point in opposite directions. Skimming sets the launch price above the going rate for a new product with no close rival; penetration pricing sets it below the going rate, and competitive pricing sets it at or near the going rate. Ask where the launch price sits against rivals' prices, and whether close rivals exist at all.

Penetration and competitive pricing both look at what rivals charge, but they differ in level, aim and timing. Penetration is a low launch price meant to buy market share, usually raised once customers are won; competitive pricing matches the going rate as a standing policy. Ask whether the price undercuts the market for a launch period or simply keeps in line with it.

Promotional pricing and sales promotion are easy to merge because both are short-term. Promotional pricing cuts the price itself for a while; a sales promotion such as a competition or a free gift adds an incentive and leaves the price unchanged. Ask whether the amount paid for one unit falls. A Swakopmund ice-cream kiosk that sells its N$25 cone for N$20 on Fridays is using promotional pricing; one that keeps the N$25 price and enters each buyer into a prize draw is running a sales promotion.

## Place: distribution channels and their recommendation

### The channels and their trade-offs

A distribution channel is the route a product takes from the business that makes it to the final consumer - for example producer to wholesaler to retailer to consumer, or producer direct to the consumer. Direct-to-consumer selling means the producer sells straight to the final customer with no wholesaler or retailer between them, through its own shop, market stall, website or delivery round. Each channel has advantages and disadvantages.

A wholesaler buys in bulk and stores stock, sparing the producer transport and storage, and its disadvantage is that it takes a share of the final price. A retailer reaches customers where they shop, and its disadvantage is that it chooses which products to stock, so a product can be dropped. Selling direct keeps the whole price and the customer relationship, and its disadvantage is that the business carries the delivery, storage and selling work itself. The product matters too: perishable and fragile products suit short, fast channels, because each extra stage adds handling and time, while long channels suit low-value products sold in many small outlets.

The trade-off underneath all of it is reach against margin against work. Every intermediary widens reach - the wholesaler's lorries and the retailer's shelf reach customers the producer could seldom reach alone - but every intermediary takes a share of the final price, and dropping an intermediary keeps the share while loading its work onto the producer. No channel choice escapes that arithmetic; the business picks the point on it that its product, its customers and its own capacity can carry.

### Choosing a channel in given circumstances

Choosing a distribution channel means fitting it to the circumstances - what the product is, where the customers are, and what size the business is - because a channel the business cannot operate or afford does not reach customers no matter how good the product is. Selling direct tends to earn a higher margin per unit, because no intermediary takes a share of the price, but the business then carries the storage, delivery and selling work the intermediary would have done.

Worked cases. An Otjiwarongo yoghurt producer faces a short-life product: a short channel fits - its own refrigerated stall and door-to-door rounds - because two intermediary stages would add days the product does not have. A Tsumeb candle maker selling low-value crafts countrywide uses a wholesaler and retailers, because the share of price the intermediaries take is cheaper than running delivery to every town itself. A two-person jewellery workshop sells direct through its own page: the whole price reaches the business, and the pair can carry the posting work themselves.

Notice what each case checked first: the product's nature, the customers' location, and the business's size. A recommendation that names the channel but not the facts it rests on is a preference, not a judgement; a recommendation that shows why this product, these customers and this business fit the chosen route is one a marker can follow and a manager could act on. The same three checks apply whatever the product - a recommendation grows out of them, never out of a favourite channel.

## Promotion: aims, forms and cost-effectiveness

### The aims of promotion

Promotion is the communication a business uses to make customers aware of a product and to persuade them to buy it, covering advertising, sales promotion and the messages a business sends about its products. Its aims differ with the situation. A new business needs awareness first, because customers cannot buy from a business they have not heard of; an established business defending its position aims to keep existing customers buying out of habit and satisfaction, because keeping a customer tends to cost less than winning one from a rival; and a business with a new product aims to explain what the product does and why it is worth trying, because persuasion is wasted on someone who does not yet understand the product.

The aims also stack: a single campaign can inform new customers and remind old ones at the same time, provided the message is built for both - a poster that names the business and states what it sells teaches the town the name while telling regulars it is still there. What promotion cannot do is replace the rest of the mix: promotion raises awareness and persuasion, but a product that does not work or a price the market rejects will not be saved by a message, however well it is delivered. That boundary is why promotion is one of the four mix decisions and not the whole of marketing.

### Forms of promotion and how they move sales

Advertising is paid promotion through a medium the business does not own - radio, print, posters, television or online placements - carrying a message to customers without a salesperson present. It influences sales by building awareness and familiarity: a name a buyer recognises feels less risky to try, and a message that gives a reason to buy can move the buyer to a first purchase. Its limitations: the costs are paid before any revenue arrives, the effect on sales is hard to measure, and repetition is needed because a single exposure is soon forgotten.

Sales promotion - short-term offers such as money-off vouchers, free samples and competitions - works differently: it lowers the cost of trying, which wins quick trial and a temporary lift in sales. Both sides of the boundary matter: the lift fades when the offer ends, and repeated offers can train buyers to wait for them rather than buy at the standing price. Advertising and sales promotion are different forms - advertising builds the message, sales promotion lowers the trial price - and the two are often used together, the advertisement announcing the offer. The test that separates them is whether the activity is the message or the offer itself: a radio spot announcing a free gift with each purchase is advertising, and the free gift it announces is the sales promotion.

The medium matters as much as the form. A radio spot reaches drivers and kitchens across a region; a poster reaches whoever walks past it; a printed notice reaches the readers of one paper; an online placement reaches whoever scrolls the page it sits on. Each medium sells attention in a different place, so the choice follows the customers: promotion lands where the target customers already spend time, which is why a market-stall business can waste money on a glossy magazine and a national brand can waste money on one town's noticeboard.

### Cost-effectiveness in the promotion budget

Cost-effectiveness in promotion spending means comparing what each promotion costs with the result it produces, so that the money spent brings in more than it cost and the best option is the one with the strongest result for its price. The chain runs: condition - the marketing budget is a fixed amount, so every Namibian dollar spent on one promotion is not spent on another. Mechanism: measuring the cost of each option per new customer, or comparing money spent against extra sales won, shows which option works and which wastes. Consequence: the business moves the budget towards what works, and the objective - profit - is served rather than starved, because promotion money spent without measuring results leaves whether or not the promotion is working, and can squeeze profit while sales look busy.

Worked case: a Rundu lodge can spend N$8 000 on a radio week or N$2 400 on a season of sponsored community posts. If the radio week brings 40 bookings and the posts bring 55, the posts deliver more result for a third of the cost - the cost-effective choice is measurable, not a guess. Compare the two against each other per Namibian dollar: 8 000 / 40 = 200 against 2 400 / 55 = 44 per booking, rounded - the sponsored posts win on cost per booking.

Two cautions complete the picture. Cost per booking says what the evidence shows, not what next year holds: a radio week that failed this year may reach visitors the posts never touch, so the measure is repeated before the budget is committed. And cost-effectiveness is a test of the promotion, not of the person who chose it - the point of measuring is to move money toward what works, which becomes hard once nothing is ever measured. A budget with a figure per result is a budget under control; a budget spent on habit is a cost the business carries blindly.

## Technology: e-commerce and online promotion

### E-commerce and its features

E-commerce is buying and selling goods or services over the internet, with orders placed and paid for online and the product delivered or provided digitally. Its features: a website or app showing products and prices; online ordering with payment taken there; order confirmation; and delivery of a physical product or digital supply of a service. The shopfront is open around the clock wherever the customer has a connection, which is the feature every other difference grows from.

Why it works: the buyer cannot be served by a salesperson, so the website does that work - the product pages answer questions, the basket collects the order, and the payment step completes the sale without a till.

The distinction worth holding onto is between online display and e-commerce. A picture page that shows baskets and takes orders by phone message is online marketing with manual sales; the sale itself - choosing, paying, receiving confirmation - still happens off the internet. E-commerce names the version where the transaction is carried out online end to end. Businesses slide between the two without noticing, and the difference matters because the online sale is what brings the around-the-clock reach: display alone merely advertises. E-commerce is the transaction itself, moved online - the order, the payment and the confirmation all carried by the website.

### Opportunities and threats

For businesses, e-commerce opens opportunities: sales outside the home town around the clock without opening shops; scattered specialist buyers gathered in one online place - a Karakul craft seller reaching buyers no shop town could collect; and data on what customers look at, which guides what is stocked next. For consumers: wider choice, easier price comparison, and shopping from home at any hour. A German specialist tea shop selling online shows the opportunity on the business side and the consumer side together - a Windhoek buyer can order teas no local shop imports.

The threats run alongside. For businesses: online rivals can reach a town shop's own customers at lower cost; price comparison pulls buyers to the cheapest listing, squeezing margins; delivery costs and returns eat what the sale earned; and a website that fails loses the sale it was handling. For consumers: the product cannot be touched or tried before paying, waiting for delivery replaces taking it home, and payment details pass to the seller's systems. Neither the opportunities nor the threats decide alone - the business's product and customers decide which side weighs more for any single business.

For an existing shop the practical question is rarely whether to adopt e-commerce but how far to go: a page that lists the stock and takes orders protects the local base while testing demand further out, while a full webshop commits the business to packing, dispatch and returns handling it never had. For a business born online the question runs the other way - whether a physical presence would earn trust the screen cannot. The opportunities and threats are therefore facts to weigh per business, not a verdict about the technology.

### The internet and social media for promotion

Social media promotion is the use of platforms where people share posts, pictures and short messages to present a product where followers see it, respond and pass it on to their own contacts. Promoting this way can cost less than paid advertising per customer reached, because each follower who shares a post carries the message to contacts the business did not pay to reach, and a share arrives with a recommendation's trust attached. A Windhoek baker posting each morning's loaves can reach more buyers per Namibian dollar than a paid notice, because the followers do part of the distributing.

The condition: the approach needs regular, worth-sharing content, because a silent or dull feed loses followers and a share needs something worth sharing. Social media promotion is promotion in a new place, not a new activity - the aims from 3.3.4, awareness and persuasion, are the same aims with a wider reach.

Running it well has a rhythm. The feed needs regular posts, because silence loses followers and the platform shows posts to fewer people when nothing is shared; the posts need content worth passing on - a photo of the morning's loaves, a behind-the-scenes video of the loom - because a share is what carries the message beyond the following; and the replies matter too, since a question answered in the comments is service delivered where other buyers can watch. The limits to hold: a follower count is not sales, and a post that reaches many people but moves none is spending attention rather than earning it - which is why the cost-effectiveness habit of 3.3.4 applies here as everywhere.
