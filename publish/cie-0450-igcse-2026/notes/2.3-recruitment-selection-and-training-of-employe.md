## Recruitment and selecting employees

### Recruitment, selection and the two routes to candidates

Recruitment is the process of identifying that the business needs a new employee and attracting suitable people to apply for the vacancy. Selection is the second half: choosing the candidate who best fits the vacancy from the people who applied. The two are often run together in one phrase, but they answer different questions - recruitment gathers candidates; selection decides between them.

Recruitment comes in two forms, and the syllabus treats them as a named distinction. Internal recruitment fills the vacancy with a candidate who already works for the business - the opening is advertised on a staff noticeboard or an internal vacancy list. External recruitment attracts candidates from outside the business, through a job advert in a newspaper or online, an employment agency or a government labour office such as Namibia's Employment Services desk. Most of what a business says about recruitment - cost, speed, fresh ideas - depends on which of the two forms is used, so never write "recruitment" alone where the form changes the answer.

Selection methods include application forms and CVs, interviews, references and practical tests such as a typing test for a receptionist or a trial lesson for a teacher. A structured interview or a practical test tends to improve the fit between the person appointed and the job, because it gathers evidence the business can check against what the role actually requires. But selection is not free: advertising, agency fees and management time are spent before any output is produced, and a poor appointment is likely to mean repeating the whole process, so the cost of getting it wrong is usually far larger than the cost of choosing carefully.

### Internal versus external recruitment

Internal recruitment and external recruitment differ at the boundary of who the candidate is: internal means the person already works for the business; external means they do not. Everything else follows from that.

Internal recruitment is likely to be quicker and cheaper, because the candidates are already known, no advertising to strangers is needed and references exist in-house. Seeing a colleague promoted tends to motivate other staff, because it shows a route upwards inside the business, and an internal appointee already knows the people and procedures. The limitation is that the pool is small: only existing employees can apply, no fresh outside ideas come in, and promoting one person usually leaves another vacancy to fill further down.

External recruitment widens the pool of candidates and can bring new skills and fresh ideas into the business - useful where the business has a gap nobody inside can fill. But it tends to be slower and more expensive, and it carries a higher risk that the appointee does not fit the people or the culture, because the business knows less about them at the point of appointment.

### The main stages, worked through

A recruitment and selection process runs through recognisable stages, and the order matters because each stage feeds the next. Etosha Rest Camp, a lodge outside Outjo, needs a new front-desk supervisor:

Stage 1, identify the vacancy and draw up the documents. The manager writes a job description - what the supervisor is responsible for - and a person specification - the skills, experience and personal qualities the business believes the role needs. Stage 2, advertise the vacancy, internally on the staff noticeboard (a current receptionist may apply) and externally in a Windhoek newspaper, because the camp wants a wider choice. Stage 3, receive applications and shortlist: the manager keeps the applicants who meet the specification and rejects the rest. Stage 4, interview and test: shortlisted candidates are asked structured questions, and each is asked to handle a mock booking at the desk. Stage 5, take up references from previous employers. Stage 6, appoint the chosen candidate and agree the contract. Stage 7, induct them - introduce them to the camp's procedures and people (induction is developed in section 2.3.2).

The input to the whole process is the given vacancy; the output is an appointed, inducted employee. Notice how the weak early stage weakens what follows: if the person specification is vague, the advert is vague, the wrong people apply, and the interview is left choosing between candidates nobody has defined. A Cambridgeshire hotel chain runs the same sequence for a head housekeeper, which shows the stages are not a Namibian invention but the shape of the process itself.

### Recommending who to employ

A person specification is the list of skills, qualifications, experience and personal qualities a business believes a candidate needs to do the job well. It is the criterion a business uses to decide which applicant to employ - and it is what turns "I liked her" into "she met the three things the role actually needs".

Worked example. Ondangwa Hardware needs a store manager. The person specification says: retail experience, able to lead four staff, able to use stock software. Candidate 1 is the current assistant manager (an internal candidate): three years' experience, known to the staff, wants a N$9 000 monthly salary, but no stock-software background. Candidate 2 applied from outside: eight years at a Gaborone chain, stock-software experience in full, wants N$13 500. If the decisive criterion is cost, the internal candidate wins on the monthly figure; but N$4 500 a month is roughly N$54 000 a year, and the software gap is a training cost of perhaps N$8 000 plus a month of learning. If the store's losses come from stock going missing - a stock-control problem - the external candidate's software skill addresses the loss directly, so the wage gap can pay for itself, provided the losses really are that size. The recommendation therefore names its condition: appoint internally if stock losses are small, appoint externally if they are not.

The cheapest candidate is not necessarily the best appointment. Skills and reliability usually matter more than a small wage difference where the role directly affects quality or customers, so the decisive criterion is which candidate best covers the work the vacancy exists to do. A counterargument worth writing out: the external candidate is a risk the business knows less about, so if the two candidates were close, the known quantity - the internal one - would be the safer recommendation.

### Part-time and full-time employees

A part-time employee works contracted hours that are fewer than the business's normal full-time hours; a full-time employee works the full contracted week. The boundary is contracted hours - not how long the job lasts (that is temporary versus permanent work) and not how demanding the work is.

Part-time employees tend to give a business flexibility: staffing can be matched to busy periods without paying for quiet ones, so a Windhoek coffee shop that is busy from 06:30 to 09:00 and again at lunch can employ part-timers for those windows rather than paying five people for a full day. Openings also appear for people who cannot work full time, such as students or parents, which widens the pool of people who can be recruited. The limitations: part-time cover can mean more people to train and communicate with, and handovers between shifts can let messages or half-finished jobs slip.

Full-time employees tend to give continuity - the same people present most days, easier teamwork and more time to learn the role - which is why roles that hold responsibility, like the store manager above, are usually full-time. But full-timers cost more per person than part-time cover when demand is uneven, because off-peak hours still have to be paid for. A Norwegian fjord-cafe chain staffs summer kiosks almost entirely with part-timers and its year-round harbour café with full-timers - the same business choosing each form where its demand pattern suits it.

### Four pairs that are easily confused

Four pairs of ideas in this section sound alike and are easily confused. Each pair has a test that separates the two ideas.

A job advert is not a product advert. Advertising a vacancy is a stage of recruitment: it speaks to people who might apply to work for the business and tells them the job, what it requires and how to apply. Advertising a product is marketing: it speaks to customers and tries to persuade them to buy. The test is what the reader is being asked to do - apply for a job, or buy something. A bakery's poster headed "Delivery driver wanted" is recruitment, however appealing it makes the bakery sound.

External recruitment is not external training. External recruitment brings a new person into the business from outside. Off-the-job training, sometimes called external training, sends someone who already works there away from the workplace to learn, at a college or training centre. The test is whether the business ends up with a new employee or a better-skilled existing one.

A job description is not a person specification. The job description describes the job: its title, duties, responsibilities and who the post-holder reports to. The person specification describes the person wanted for it: the skills, qualifications, experience and qualities needed. The job description comes first, and the person specification is written from it. The test: does the line describe a task to be done, or a quality the applicant must have?

Part-time is not temporary. Part-time describes hours per week; temporary describes how long the contract lasts - a fixed period or a particular task. The two vary independently: a harvest worker can be full-time and temporary, and a school librarian can be part-time and permanent. The test is which question the word answers - how many hours a week, or for how long.

## The importance of training and the methods of training

### Training and why it matters to both sides

Training is the process of teaching an employee the knowledge and skills needed to carry out their job to the standard the business requires. Its importance runs in two directions, which is why the syllabus asks for the business and the employee.

To the business: employees who know what they are doing work faster and make fewer mistakes, which lowers wasted material and rework and tends to keep customers; training also eases the adoption of new technology, and a business that trains can promote from within, so fewer external recruits are needed for senior posts - internal recruitment and internal promotion are the same idea applied at different moments. To the employee: training raises the skills carried to future jobs and the pay those skills can command, and being invested in tends to raise motivation - though training done at the employer's expense may come with an expectation of staying. The costs sit alongside: training takes time away from production, costs money to deliver, and a newly trained employee is more attractive to other employers, so retention is part of the calculation.

### Induction, on-the-job and off-the-job training

Three training methods matter at IGCSE, and each has a benefit and a limitation worth developing. Induction training happens in the first days of employment: it introduces the new employee to the people, procedures and rules of the business, so they settle in faster and know who to ask - but it carries little new-skill depth, because its job is orientation, not expertise. On-the-job training is learning while doing the real job at the normal workplace, usually from a colleague or supervisor: it is directly relevant, immediately practised and cheap to arrange, which is why a Keetmanshoop panel-beating shop teaches panel work at the bench - but the trainer is producing less while teaching, the trainee practises on real customers' cars, and existing bad habits are passed along with skills. Off-the-job training happens away from the workplace, often at a college or training centre: it brings up-to-date expertise, outside perspectives and often a certificate - useful for a Walvis Bay freight clerk learning new customs software - but it costs time away from the job, travel and course fees, and what is learned in a classroom can still need translating back to the workplace.

The best method tends to depend on the need: rules and people are learned by induction, day-to-day practical skill is often learned on the job, and broader or safety-critical skills often need off-the-job training. Most businesses mix the three, and a Scottish distillery that inducts, benches and college-trains its warehouse staff is choosing each method where its learning suits it.

### Choosing a method in practice

The three methods answer different needs, and a real business usually meets all three in one year. Worked example: Otjiwarongo Fresh Meat, a small abattoir and butchery, hires a new counter assistant. Her first two days are induction - the manager walks her through the cold-chain rules, the till, the hygiene checks and introduces her to the four colleagues, so she knows who to ask before she is left alone with customers. Her next two weeks are on the job: she serves under an experienced butcher, learning the cuts and the weighing while real orders are being filled, because the skill is a practised one that a classroom cannot reproduce. When the new food-safety regulations arrive, the manager and the senior butcher attend a two-day off-the-job course at the Meat Board training centre in Windhoek - the regulation is safety-critical and the business wants it taught from the standard itself, not from the habits already in the building. One business, three methods, each chosen because of the kind of learning it carries.

## Why reducing the size of the workforce might be necessary

### Dismissal and redundancy - with examples

Dismissal is when an employer ends an employee's contract because of the employee's own conduct or capability. Redundancy is when an employer ends a job because the role itself is no longer needed. The boundary is where the cause sits: dismissal traces to something the employee did or cannot do; redundancy traces to the work disappearing. The person has done nothing wrong in a redundancy - which is why redundancy usually carries compensation and a farewell that dismissal does not.

Examples make the boundary concrete. Dismissal: a warehouse assistant at a Gobabin cold store is dismissed for repeated lateness after two written warnings - the cause is conduct, and a fair dismissal needs a valid reason and a fair procedure. Redundancy: the same assistant's second job ends because a new scanning system removed the need for two people in that aisle - the cause is the role shrinking. Redundancy, retirement and dismissal are three different exits, and a business that dresses a dismissal up as a redundancy is likely to face a claim of unfair dismissal, because the two carry different procedures and different consequences.

### Why downsizing happens

Condition: where the amount of work a business has no longer matches the number of people it employs. Mechanism: if demand for the product falls, fewer workers are needed to make it; if a machine does the work of several people, those people are surplus to the output required. Consequence: keeping them means paying wages the revenue no longer covers, so the business loses money and may cut the workforce - downsizing, a deliberate reduction in the size of the workforce, usually to cut costs.

The situations are worth naming. Falling demand: a Tsumeb brickworks selling 40 per cent fewer bricks after a construction slowdown needs fewer kiln staff. Automation: an Oshakati grain mill installs an automatic bagging line and no longer needs three baggers. Cost survival: a chain of hair salons closes two of its five branches to stop losses. Each situation is a mismatch, not a judgement about the workers - which is why downsizing is carried out through redundancy rather than dismissal.

### Recommending which employees to make redundant

The recommendation is judged against criteria, and the criterion you choose decides the answer. Worked example. Aukekas Logistics in Otjiwarongo loses its biggest contract and must cut one of its three drivers. Driver M has 12 years' service, knows every client site and earns N$9 800 a month; Driver R is on N$7 800, reliable on the remaining route; Driver K, on N$6 900, is a trainee who also maintains the loading dock software that schedules the remaining runs.

The cost criterion - release the most expensive - points at Driver M: N$9 800 saved monthly against N$6 900 for K. But M's client knowledge is what the remaining work runs on, and K's software skill schedules it; releasing either would be a poor trade, because the cheapest employee to release is not the same as the cheapest skill to lose. The fair-and-balanced criterion is common in practice: last in, first out, which would point at K - yet K's distinct skill is exactly what the smaller business needs. Weighing the criteria, the recommendation is to release R: the work that remains does not uniquely depend on R's skill, the saving is N$7 800 a month, and the distinctive skills - client knowledge and scheduling software - both stay. Provided the business has checked that R's route truly folds into the others (if it does not, the answer changes), that is the recommendation to write, with the criterion named and the counterargument - that M's wage is the biggest saving - answered rather than ignored.

### Two confusions to keep apart

The redundancy question asks which employees to make redundant, not which employees the business should have hired - and answers that argue for retaining someone have still answered it wrongly if they never say who is released. The error is treating "why keep them" as the recommendation: the wrongly-focused answer praises the skills of the person it likes and stops, which is a retention argument, not a redundancy choice. The test that tells a proper recommendation apart: it names the person or group to be made redundant, states the criterion that selected them - the skills the remaining work needs - and answers the counterargument that the cheapest release is not the same as the cheapest skill to lose. A retention case is one half of that reasoning; it becomes a recommendation only when the release is named too.

## Legal controls over employment issues and their impact on employers and employees

### The five legal controls and their impact

Employment law controls are the rules a government places on the relationship between employers and employees; the syllabus names five areas. Employment contracts: the terms of employment must be set out in writing, so both sides know pay, hours and duties and disputes have a document to refer to. Unfair dismissal: an employer usually cannot end a contract without a fair reason and a fair procedure, which protects employees from arbitrary exit - the flip side of the dismissal idea in 2.3.3. Discrimination: people must not be treated worse in hiring, pay or promotion because of personal characteristics such as gender or ethnicity, so selection and promotion must rest on job-related criteria, not preference. Health and safety: the employer must keep the workplace and its equipment safe, provide protective gear and train people to use it, which protects employees and customers alike. The legal minimum wage: pay must sit at or above the floor the law sets, which protects the lowest-paid from being undercut and lifts the cost of every hour the business buys.

The impact runs both ways. These controls tend to protect employees - and businesses that respect them tend to gain trust and fewer disputes - but they raise business costs: wages at or above the legal minimum, safety equipment and training, and procedures before any dismissal. Employment law differs from country to country, so state what the controls aim at rather than quoting one country's figures.
