## The role of market research and methods used

### Market research and the market-orientated business

Market research is the process of collecting and analysing information about a market - the customers, actual and potential, for a product: what they want, what they would pay, where they shop and what competitors offer. A business is market-orientated when it lets this information shape its decisions, designing products, prices and promotion around what customers want rather than around what the business finds easy to make.

The opposite approach is product orientation. A product-orientated business develops a product first, often from a skill or a material it already has, and then goes looking for customers. A jeweller in Windhoek who designs what pleases her and then hunts for buyers works this way; a market-orientated jeweller asks what customers in her area are asking for - affordable everyday pieces or one-off wedding work - before deciding what to make. Most real businesses sit somewhere between the two, but the market-orientated side of the scale is the side market research feeds.

The information is used to reduce the risk of decisions. It identifies customer wants and needs, shows where a gap in the market exists, reveals what people think of the price, and measures how well promotion is reaching them. It cannot remove uncertainty - tastes change and research can be wrong - but it replaces guesses with evidence.

### What the research is used for

Kalahari Chillies, a small sauce maker in Mariental, wanted to move from farm stalls onto supermarket shelves. Before redesigning its bottles it spent two weeks on research: it counted the sauces already on the shelf in three Windhoek supermarkets, read the labels of the two brands selling best, and asked 60 shoppers outside a grocery store what stopped them buying hot sauce. The answers were unexpected - the heat was not the problem, the bottles were. Shoppers wanted smaller bottles they could finish before the sauce dried out, and they would pay more for them. The firm redesigned around a 250 ml bottle, and the listing talks with a supermarket chain began from evidence rather than hope.

A Norwegian crispbread maker tells the same story in a different market: its research showed families in Oslo giving up on large boxes that went stale, so it introduced small sealed packs and lifted its sales. The use of the information is the same in both places - research turns a guess about what customers want into a tested idea before money is committed, and the finding can be shown to a retailer or a bank as evidence that the plan is worth backing.

### Primary research and secondary research

Primary research means gathering first-hand data: the business goes out and collects the information itself, straight from customers or potential customers, for the question it faces right now. Counting the shoppers outside a grocery store is primary research; so is ringing ten past customers to ask why they stopped ordering.

Secondary research uses data that already exists. Someone else collected it, for their own purposes, and the business reads or buys it: government statistics, trade association figures, competitor websites, newspaper reports or a commercial market research report. Reading the sales figures a statistics agency published is secondary research.

The two are complements rather than rivals. The boundary between them is who collected the data and for what question: first-hand, for your own question, is primary; already existing, collected by someone else, is secondary. Price, freshness and fit do not move data across that line: an agency's report bought for N$9 500 on exactly the firm's market is still secondary, because the agency collected it and anyone may buy it.

### The benefits and limits of primary research

Primary research fits the question. Because the business writes its own questions, the answers address exactly what it needs to know - the view of 16 to 24 year olds in Khomas of a new sneaker design, not adults in general. Freshly gathered data is also up to date, and competitors do not have it.

The costs sit on the other side. Designing a survey, training people to give it and analysing the returns all take time and money, so primary research tends to be the dearer route. A small sample can mislead if the people asked are unlike the wider market, and respondents sometimes say what they think the researcher wants to hear. For a small firm the practical limit is often size: a two-person business can interview 30 customers, not three thousand.

### The benefits and limits of secondary research

Secondary research is fast and cheap. A morning on the internet can produce population figures, income statistics and competitor prices, and a government agency's tables usually cost nothing to read. Because large organisations collected it, secondary data often covers a whole industry or a whole country - a reach no small business could afford to build itself.

But existing data was collected for someone else's question. It may be out of date, with figures several years old; it may not fit the business's exact product or area; and it is equally available to every competitor. Secondary research tells a sauce maker how the Namibian condiment market grows, but not what Windhoek shoppers think of a 250 ml bottle.

### Choosing the route that fits the question

The usual sequence starts secondary and adds primary where it pays. When Tsumeb Tents wanted to know whether demand existed for wedding-equipment hire, the owner first spent two evenings on free secondary sources: the tourism agency's visitor numbers for the region, the count of weddings registered the year before, and what the one existing hire firm advertised. That cost almost nothing and showed the market was worth a closer look.

Only then did she commission primary research - 40 short interviews with couples booking venues - because the secondary sources could not answer the question that decided her prices: what couples in Tsumeb expect to pay for a marquee package. The mechanism is worth naming: secondary research settles the cheap questions first, so the money spent on primary research goes where existing data runs out. Where the risk of a wrong decision is small, secondary sources alone may be enough; where the decision commits the firm - a new product, a new price, a new town - first-hand answers tend to earn their cost.

### The four primary methods

A postal questionnaire is a printed set of questions mailed to chosen people, who post their answers back. It reaches people at home, needs no interviewer and costs little per person reached - but response rates tend to be low, returns come back slowly, and the firm cannot see a respondent hesitate over a question.

An online survey puts the same questions on the internet and collects answers as people click through. Distribution is close to free, answers arrive within days, and the totals are counted automatically - but it reaches the people who are online and willing to answer, so older or rural respondents can be under-represented, and a careless respondent may click anything to be done.

An interview is a conversation with one respondent, face to face or by phone. The interviewer can follow up - "you said the price was high; what would you expect to pay?" - so answers are deep and misunderstandings are caught on the spot. Each interview takes time, so it is the costliest method per person reached, and the interviewer's manner can shape the answers.

A focus group brings six to ten customers together to discuss a product or an idea, guided by a moderator. It shows the reasons behind opinions - why the bottle feels wrong, what a gift set says about the giver - and lets one customer's point be tested against another's. A strong voice in the group can sway the rest, and venue, refreshments and moderation make each session cost real money.

### Matching the method to the problem

Each method earns its place in a different job. Where the question is simple and numbers matter - which of four flavours, yes or no - an online survey is hard to beat on speed and cost. Where the question is "why" - why the trial pack failed - a handful of interviews or one focus group teaches more than a thousand tick-boxes, because the researcher can dig into the reason behind each answer. Where the people to ask are scattered and offline - farmers across the Otjozondjupa region - a postal questionnaire sent through associations can reach them where an online survey cannot.

Depth and reach trade against each other: interviews and focus groups give depth on few people, while postal and online questionnaires give thin answers on many. A sensible plan often mixes them - a focus group to find the right questions, then an online survey to count how widely those answers hold.

### Sampling and why it is needed

A sample is the part of a target market selected to take part in the research. Sampling means collecting data from that part and using it to draw conclusions about the whole market, rather than asking every potential customer.

The need for sampling is arithmetic and cost. A business selling to the 200 000 households of a city cannot knock on 200 000 doors; the survey would cost more than the product launch it is meant to protect. A sample of 400 households can be questioned for a fraction of the cost, and if it is chosen to resemble the market - a mix of ages, areas and incomes like the city itself - its answers are likely to reflect the whole market closely enough to act on. Sampling is a compromise rather than a lazy shortcut: the smaller and less representative the sample, the more careful the business must be about how far its conclusions travel.

### A sampling plan in practice

When Ongwediva Uniforms wanted to know what schools across the four northern regions thought of a new sewing service, it could not visit every one of the 240 registered schools. The owner selected 24 of them - one in ten - spread deliberately across the regions and across town and rural schools, so the sample resembled the market rather than clustering in the easy-to-reach towns. Interviewing 24 principals took two weeks and two days of fuel; interviewing 240 would have taken most of a term.

The gain is not just saved money: results arrive while the decision still waits. If 19 of the 24 principals said they would use the service, the firm had a tested basis for buying the extra sewing machines - and a figure it could quote to the bank manager as evidence of demand. The risk is the mirror image: had the sample been 24 town schools alone, the answer would have flattered the idea. Representativeness is what turns a sample into evidence.

### The three main secondary routes

Online research comes first for most firms because it is free and immediate: competitor websites show prices and ranges, social media show what customers say unprompted, and trade news shows what is launching. The weakness is quality - anyone can publish anything, so figures found online are checked against their source before a decision leans on them.

Government sources - the statistics agency's census tables, tourism numbers, trade figures - are methodical and usually free or cheap, and a census aims at the whole population rather than a sample. But they are broad: they count the country or the region, rarely one product's buyers, and the newest figures may already be years old.

A commercial market research report is written and sold by a specialist agency that has already done the surveys. It offers a depth of industry analysis a small firm could not gather alone - market size, growth, segments, competitor shares - at a price. The catch runs with the strength: competitors are able to buy the same report, so it is shared knowledge rather than an edge, and it still answers the industry's questions, not the firm's own.

### What makes research data accurate - or not

Accuracy in market research data means the data reflects the truth about the market closely enough to act on. Five factors influence it. The sample: a small or unrepresentative sample produces confident-looking numbers that are wrong about the market. The questions: a leading question - "how much do you love our friendly service?" - pushes respondents toward a flattering answer, and an ambiguous one collects mixed answers into a single pile. The respondents: people may not know their own future behaviour, or may answer to please. The age of the data: last year's preferences can be this year's mistake. The handling: careless recording, mistaken totals and selective reading all bend results after they are collected.

The cost of getting it wrong is usually bigger than the cost of checking. A launch built on flattering data fails in the market, where the truth arrives anyway; a price cut offered because a misread table suggested weakness hands away margin the business never needed to lose. Treating findings with a little suspicion - asking how the sample was drawn, who wrote the questions and when - is part of using research well, not a failure to trust it.

## Presentation and use of market research results

### Reading a diagram of results

Analysing market research data means comparing the numbers against each other before saying anything about them: which bar is tallest, which line fell, by how much, and against what. A reading that names the pattern - "mango was chosen by 96 of 200 shoppers, ahead of every rival flavour" - is analysis; a number copied out alone is not.

A conclusion then says what the pattern means for the business: which product to back, which price looks accepted, which group to target. A sound conclusion compares at least two figures, states its size, and stays inside what the data shows. "Mango is the most popular flavour" is a conclusion the table supports; "because mango reminds people of home" is a guess the table cannot prove, because the figures counted choices, not reasons.

### A worked reading

Oshakati Smoothies asked 200 shoppers which flavour they would buy next month. The diagram of results: mango 96, guava 52, baobab 32, no purchase 20. Reading: mango drew 96 of the 200, which is 48 per cent, ahead of guava on 52 and baobab on 32, and the three flavours together accounted for 180 of the 200 asked. Conclusion: mango is the flavour to feature at launch, chosen by close to half the shoppers, while baobab stays a secondary option rather than a headline; the one in ten who would buy none is a reminder that promotion still matters. A Dutch bike-share firm read its rider survey the same way, and built its campaign around the annual pass its table showed commuters preferred.

### Where this skill goes next

The same reading skill - compare, size the pattern, conclude inside the data - carries into topic 3.3, where research results shape price and promotion choices, and into the longer examination questions, where a table or diagram is often the evidence an answer must use. This worked example is the pattern to practise.
