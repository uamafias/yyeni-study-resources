## Environmental and ethical issues

### How business activity affects the environment

Every stage of business activity draws on the natural world. Extraction takes minerals, timber, water and fossil fuels out of the ground and the sea. Production burns fuel, and the smoke, fumes and greenhouse gases released add to air pollution and global warming. Waste leaves the business as effluent into rivers, smoke into the air and refuse into landfill sites.

The five impacts most often examined are: air pollution, such as exhaust fumes from a delivery fleet; water pollution, such as chemical discharge from a tannery into a river; land pollution, such as dumped waste that contaminates soil; loss of natural habitat, such as forest cleared for a plantation; and the depletion of non-renewable resources such as coal, oil and minerals that cannot be replaced on a human timescale. Green warming, the rise in average world temperature driven by greenhouse gases, is the cumulative result: a single delivery lorry matters little, but millions of business decisions together change the climate the world shares.

A fish processing plant at Walvis Bay shows the chain in one business. Boats burn diesel to fish, processing uses electricity and water, off-cuts may be dumped rather than reused, and refrigerated trucks burn fuel to move the catch. No stage is illegal, yet each leaves an imprint on the environment the business does not pay for.

### Externalities: external costs and external benefits

An externality is a cost or benefit of a business decision that falls on people outside the business and is not reflected in the prices the business pays or receives. The business buys its inputs and sells its output through prices; an externality is the part of the decision that slips past those prices and lands on third parties.

An external cost makes third parties worse off. If a tannery releases chemical waste into the Kavango river, people who fish and wash downstream bear a cost the tannery's accounts never record: their health, their catch and their clean water are all reduced without compensation. Other examples are traffic congestion around a new shopping centre, or noise suffered by residents near an airport.

An external benefit makes third parties better off. If a bus company opens a route past a village so its workers can commute, other residents and businesses can ride it too; if a firm trains its workers in safety, every later employer inherits a safer-trained workforce. The business cannot charge for these gains, which is the other side of the externality idea.

### Tracing an externality

Take Okahandja Meat Packers, which processes beef for export. Its decisions create an external cost and an external benefit at once. The cost: processing water is pumped from the local aquifer and cleaning effluent is piped to a soakaway that slowly reaches the boreholes of nearby farms, raising their water-treatment bills - a cost on third parties, not on the packers' price of inputs. The benefit: the plant's cold-chain contract keeps a refrigerated-truck operator running a daily route past small vegetable growers, who now use the same trucks to reach the Windhoek market cheaply.

The reasoning a learner should be able to reproduce: identify the decision, then ask who outside the business is made better or worse off by it, and check whether any price carried that effect. The farm boreholes and the vegetable growers are both outside the packers' accounts, so both effects are externalities - one negative, one positive.

### Two confusions to keep apart

Externalities are not business costs, and external costs are not the same as threats. An external cost sits outside the business's own cost figures - that is the whole idea: the tannery pays for leather chemicals but not for the river's loss, whereas wages and rent are internal costs that appear in its accounts. The test: ask who pays. If the business pays, the cost is internal; if a third party pays, the cost is external. A threat, in turn, is something the environment or a pressure group might do to a business, while an externality is what the business does to others - the direction of the effect is the difference. Keeping these apart matters, because the responses differ: a business manages its own costs directly, but an externality usually draws outside attention - a regulator or a campaign - before it changes what the business does.

### Sustainable development

Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. For a business, the question becomes whether the way it produces today leaves the resources, the environment and the society it will need tomorrow.

A business can contribute to sustainable development in four main ways. It can switch to renewable or recycled materials, such as furniture makers using reclaimed wood or printers using recycled paper. It can cut energy and water use, such as a guesthouse in Swakopmund fitting solar panels and low-flow showers. It can reduce and recycle its waste, such as a brewery sending spent grain to cattle feed instead of landfill. And it can design products that last longer or can be repaired, so fewer replacements are produced. A Dutch clothing brand that leases jackets and repairs them for free is one European example: the incentive to make garments that survive years of wear is written into its revenue model.

Sustainability can pay for itself: using less energy and material tends to lower running costs, and a segment of customers may pay more for a product they trust. But some sustainable choices cost more now - solar panels, recycled stock, better design - so the business weighs the saving and the possible price premium against that outlay.

### Environmental pressures and opportunities

Businesses do not face the environment in silence. A pressure group is an organisation that campaigns to change business or government behaviour on an issue its members care about, without seeking power itself. Groups such as a river-clean-up association or an international wildlife charity use publicity, boycotts and social-media campaigns; their leverage is usually indirect - they may persuade customers to act rather than compel the business directly, though a large, organised campaign can make sales fall sharply enough that the business changes course.

Pressure reaches a business through several channels: customers asking for greener products, pressure groups campaigning against its practices, competitors marketing their own green credentials, and governments legislating. But the environment also offers opportunities: a market for greener products, cost savings from wasting less energy and material, and a reputation that attracts customers and workers.

A business is more likely to respond when its customers, its reputation or its costs are genuinely at stake, because those channels reach profit. Where a campaign has no visible effect on sales and no law applies, the temptation is a token response - a poster campaign rather than a changed process.

### From pressure to business response

Work the chain in four steps. Condition: a pressure group launches a campaign - say, a national consumer association publishes the water usage of every soft-drink bottler in Namibia and calls for a boycott of the heaviest user, which is Snyman Bottling of Otjiwarongo. Mechanism: the campaign reaches customers through news and social media, so some buyers start choosing a rival brand; the same coverage makes retailers ask questions, and the bottler's image - and with it the credibility of its other marketing - is at risk. Consequence: sales and possibly staff recruitment weaken, so profit is threatened - recruitment through both its internal and external routes, as existing staff grow reluctant to recommend the bottler to friends and outside candidates stop applying. Response, and why: the bottler invests in a water-recycling plant and publicises the figures, because doing so answers the campaign, protects the sales that fund profit, and may itself lower the water bill - an environmental opportunity created by environmental pressure. The same chain shows why a smaller firm with fewer customers may respond more slowly: the pressure has to reach profit before it changes decisions.

### Pressure is not compulsion

A learner writes: "Once a pressure group targets it, a business has no choice but to comply." The error is treating pressure as law. In fact, a pressure group has no legal power over a business: it can campaign, publicise and call for boycotts, but the business may wait the campaign out, especially if its customers are indifferent. The test that tells the two apart: ask who can compel what. A pressure group can change what customers think; a government can change what the business is legally allowed to do. That is why some campaigns change nothing, while a single new law usually does.

### Legal controls over business activity

Legal controls are laws that require or forbid behaviour. A government concerned about pollution may set maximum permitted emission levels, restrict where waste may be dumped, require an environmental impact study before a major project is approved, or ban certain chemicals outright. These are pollution controls, and they turn an external cost into a cost the business itself bears, through fines, limits or required equipment.

Why governments legislate: voluntary restraint and market pressure are not dependable everywhere, so legislation exists to force the cost back onto the decision-maker - though how far a government uses this route is a choice each country makes.

What this means for a business is mainly cost and constraint. Installing cleaner equipment, paying for licensed waste disposal or paying fines all raise costs, which may push up prices or crowd out other spending. And because what a country's environmental laws require, and how strictly they are enforced, differs from country to country, a business needs to check the rules that apply where it operates. A miner working in Namibia and in Chile may face stricter discharge rules in one market than in the other.

### Who gains and who pays when law tightens

A new pollution limit distributes effects unevenly. The community downstream of the plant gains clean water it was never paid for - the external cost shrinks. The business pays: filters, licensing and monitoring raise its costs, and the money spent there is not spent on wages or expansion. Customers may pay part of the bill through higher prices, and if the firm is near break-even the limit may even threaten jobs. Employees keep a healthier workplace but face a less secure employer. A judgement about legal controls is therefore a weighing across stakeholders, not a single verdict - and the balance depends on how heavy the cost is, how strictly the rule is enforced and how easily the business can pass costs on.

### Ethics and the profit conflict

Business ethics are the moral principles that guide how a business behaves towards its stakeholders - the accepted standards of right and wrong in its decisions, rather than the standards the law requires. The law draws the floor; ethics asks what is right above it.

The issues a business might face: paying wages too low to live on while owners profit; selling products that may be unhealthy; advertising that misleads; testing products on animals; and trading with suppliers whose own behaviour is questionable. The heart of the topic is that ethics and profit pull against each other. The ethical choice often costs more - higher wages, cleaner processes, honest advertising - while profit rises from paying less, so a business may be tempted towards the unethical option precisely because it is cheaper.

The pull is not one-way. A reputation for fair dealing tends to keep customers, workers and suppliers loyal, which serves the business in the long run; but the link is not mechanical, because ethical choices cost money now and the payback, if it comes, comes later. A supermarket chain that pays its shelf-stackers above the going rate may find them staying longer and serving better - or may find rivals undercutting its prices.

### Ethical is not the same as green, or as legal

Two slips blur business ethics into neighbouring ideas. The first treats acting ethically as the same as being environmentally friendly. The two overlap - poisoning a village's water supply is both an environmental and an ethical failure - but they are not the same. Environmental friendliness concerns one kind of effect: what a decision does to air, water, land and wildlife. Ethics is wider: it asks whether the business treats its stakeholders fairly - workers, customers, suppliers and communities as well as the places they live. A solar-powered packing shed that underpays its pickers is green and still unethical. The test: ask whose treatment the decision is about. If the effect falls on the natural environment, the question is environmental; if it concerns whether people are dealt with fairly, the question is ethical.

The second slip treats obeying the law as acting ethically. The law sets the minimum a business must do, backed by fines and other penalties; ethics is a choice about what is right above that minimum. Paying the legal minimum wage when it is too low to live on is lawful and may still be unethical. The test: ask whether the business could be punished for not doing it. If it could, the matter is a legal duty; if it could not, it is an ethical choice.

### Responding to ethical issues

A business that decides to act on ethics has several instruments. An ethical code of practice sets the standards the business claims to hold itself to, from honest advertising to equal pay. Supplier audits check whether overseas factories meet those standards, including on child labour. Fair prices to suppliers, written into long-term purchase contracts, are a response to the opposite pressure: buyers squeezing small producers below the cost of production. A business may also simply refuse to trade with suppliers whose behaviour it cannot accept.

Against child labour specifically, a business can inspect its suppliers, fund schooling programmes in the sourcing regions, or move its orders elsewhere - noting that removing orders outright can leave the affected families worse off, which is why many firms choose the audit-and-fund route. In pricing, a Namibian craft retailer might guarantee its basket weavers a fixed floor price per basket rather than haggle each season, and a Japanese tea buyer might contract small growers at a fixed rate above the local market's.

The constraint on all of it is cost: ethical responses raise costs in the short run, so a business practising them may need customers who will pay for them. Where price-sensitive buyers dominate, the business may instead absorb lower margins or lose sales.
