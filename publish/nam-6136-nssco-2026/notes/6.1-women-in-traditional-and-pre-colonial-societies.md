## Women’s productive and social roles

### Women’s productive and social roles

Women’s labour in rural societies has included food production, farming, processing, household provision and community activity. Roles vary across time, place and households. Unpaid work contributes to well-being even when it is absent from wage records; a role description is not a justification for unequal opportunity.

### Labour

Women contribute physical and mental labour in paid and unpaid activities. Counting wages alone can conceal food preparation, care or productive household work, so assess tasks and time rather than assuming no earnings means no contribution.

Applied example: Women may perform paid work such as selling produce. Unpaid farming or household production also supplies goods and services even when no wage is recorded.

### Food production

Women can contribute through planting, tending, harvesting, processing and distributing food. Access to land, tools, time and decision-making affects this contribution. Work performed does not establish control over its product or income.

Applied example: Planting and tending crops help produce the harvest. Processing and preparing harvested food make it usable for households or sale.

### Family welfare

Care, food preparation, water collection and household organisation support family welfare. Unequal caring burdens can restrict time for learning or paid work. Sharing responsibilities or improving services can expand choices without devaluing care.

Applied example: Preparing food supports members’ daily nutritional needs. Caring for children or ill relatives supports their well-being and enables other necessary activities.

### Read the practical connection

A household food diary in a fictional settlement records vegetables grown for consumption, meals prepared and hours of care alongside cash earnings. Selling no produce does not make the vegetables valueless: they meet needs that would otherwise require purchases. Care also supports other members’ participation. Assess the contribution without assuming that the person doing the work necessarily controls the household’s income.

## Culture and position

### Culture and position

Cultural values and practices influence expectations about work, property and participation. They can support cooperation or restrict opportunities depending on their form and use; culture is diverse and changing, not a fixed explanation of all women’s experiences.

### Values

Values concern what people regard as desirable or acceptable. A value placed on mutual support may encourage shared work, while an expectation that leadership belongs to one gender can restrict voice. Explain the mechanism rather than stereotyping a community.

### Customs

Customs and practices shape how social decisions are carried out. A meeting timetable or speaking arrangement can affect who participates. Consultation can reveal whether a practice supports or restricts access and what affected people want changed.

### Taboos and myths

A taboo restricts an activity or discussion; a myth may be used to explain or justify a belief. If a belief excludes women from a productive role regardless of skill, it narrows opportunity. Describing that effect does not endorse the belief or claim it is shared by all members.

### Read the practical connection

In a fictional savings group, important decisions are made while some members are collecting children from school. Written membership rights do not give those members an effective voice at that time. Consultation over meeting times and a way to submit views can improve participation. Such arrangements should respond to the actual members’ duties rather than assume that all women share the same responsibilities.

## Assess colonial changes

### Assess colonial changes

Colonial arrangements could reshape education, land access, wages, migration and commercial production, with differing effects on women. Evaluate access, workload and control separately. New wage activity can coexist with exclusion from land or training; one opportunity is not proof of overall equality.

### Education access

Exclusion from schooling or training can restrict access to skills and recognised opportunities. Where selection favours men despite comparable ability, women’s productive choices can be narrowed; a later formal admission rule does not automatically remove fees, distance or time barriers.

### Land entitlement

Access to and control over productive land influence what a person can grow and decide. Where women work land but lack secure decision rights, their labour contribution need not give control over investment or returns. Distinguish labour performed from entitlement.

### Wages and labour

Paid employment can provide income while discriminatory selection, pay or conditions limit its gains. Compare access, earnings and workload together. Entering wage work does not remove unpaid responsibilities unless their arrangement changes.

### Migratory labour

Labour migration separates work locations and can change household income and task allocation. Remittances may help, but absent workers can leave others with more farming and care. The balance depends on actual transfers, timing and available support.

### Commercial farming

Commercial expansion can alter land access, labour demand and what households produce for sale. Women may gain earnings while facing reduced food-growing access or control of proceeds. Assess whose opportunities and resources change rather than assuming a common benefit.

### Read the practical connection

In a fictional historical comparison, a household gains wage income after moving closer to an employer, but now buys food formerly grown on its own plot. The wage gain must be compared with replacement food costs and changed work and care time. Employment can expand opportunities while another change reduces control over resources; neither wage access nor the loss alone establishes the combined welfare result.

### Historical mechanisms in Namibia

Colonial land dispossession reduced access to productive resources in Namibia. Contract labour and migrant work could separate wage earners from rural households, increasing the farming and caring tasks left to women. Discriminatory access to education and formal jobs restricted some women’s alternatives, while cash-crop and wage relationships changed how households met needs. Do not describe women as passive or all households as identical: women also produced food, traded, organised and adapted to these pressures. Judge control of land and income as well as labour performed; a heavier contribution need not bring stronger decision rights.
