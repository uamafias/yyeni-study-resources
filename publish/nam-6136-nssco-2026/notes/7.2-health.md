## Conditions for health

### Conditions for health

Clean water, hygiene, an adequate diet, prevention and treatment of disease, and social conditions can influence health and life expectancy. Life expectancy is an average under the mortality conditions used, not an individual promise. Poverty can restrict food, housing and service access; stress and illness can interact with those constraints.

### Water and hygiene

Safe water and appropriate hygiene reduce exposure to some contamination pathways. A supply needs reliable access and protection after collection; a new water point does not help equally if distance, breakdown or unsafe handling prevents use.

Applied example: It can reduce exposure to contaminated supplies and reduce time lost obtaining water. With fewer interruptions to health and available time, people may attend school or work more consistently.

### Adequate diet

A nutritionally adequate diet provides suitable energy and nutrients for health. Availability, affordability and varied intake matter. More food by weight does not necessarily establish adequate nutrition, and a household label does not diagnose nutritional status.

Applied example: People need suitable nutrients as well as enough energy. A household may obtain a large amount of one food while lacking the variety needed for an adequate diet.

### Disease prevention and care

Prevention and effective accessible care can reduce illness and its consequences. Facilities, trained staff, supplies and timely referral must work together. A building without operating support does not establish useful health provision.

Applied example: A building provides space, but qualified workers deliver and refer care. Without operating staff, the extra physical facility need not provide usable treatment.

### Poverty and stress

Material hardship can limit adequate food, housing and service access, while insecurity and stress can affect well-being. Illness can also reduce earnings and add care costs. Explain interacting pathways without assuming poverty establishes an individual’s health or assigning blame.

Applied example: Limited income can restrict food and access to care. Illness can then interrupt earnings and add care costs, making those material constraints harder to overcome.

### Follow the mechanism

In an invented settlement, a reliable protected water supply is paired with maintained toilets and handwashing facilities. These measures can reduce exposure to some water-borne infections through different routes. They do not prevent every disease: malaria, for example, has a mosquito transmission route. Match the prevention measure to the route and check whether the facilities remain usable.

### Disease, prevention and productive participation

Illness can interrupt attendance at school and work, add caring time and absorb household income. Malaria is caused by a parasite transmitted through bites of infected mosquitoes; it is not caused by drinking dirty water. Safe water, sanitation and hygiene instead reduce exposure to infections associated with faecal contamination, including cholera. Mosquito control and reducing bites address a different transmission route. Match prevention to the route rather than presenting one measure as protection against all diseases. Immunisation uses vaccines to develop protection against particular infections; it is not treatment for every existing illness. Programmes need trained personnel, dependable supplies, suitable storage and reachable services. This is a development explanation, not personal diagnosis or a vaccination schedule.

### Worked percentage change

Percentage change compares a change with the starting value. Subtract the old value from the new value, divide that difference by the old value, then multiply by 100. If attendance rises from 80 to 100 users, the increase is 20 users; 20 divided by 80 is 0.25, giving 25% growth. If it falls from 100 to 80, the change is −20 users; dividing by the original 100 gives −0.2, or a 20% fall. The different denominators explain the different percentages. Percentage points instead subtract two percentages, such as coverage rising from 40% to 55%, a gain of 15 points. Starting from zero makes this percentage-change division undefined; report the absolute change.

## Evaluate health programmes

### Evaluate health programmes

Primary health care links accessible care, prevention and community needs; hospitals, trained doctors and supporting workers provide complementary capabilities. Trained auxiliaries need suitable roles, supervision and referral support; immunisation, community programmes and safe-water provision address different needs. Compare accessible coverage, quality, staffing, maintenance and cost rather than simply counting buildings.

### Follow the mechanism

A fictional health budget can expand a staffed mobile service or equip a fixed centre for more specialised care. Outreach can reduce journeys for routine needs; a fixed centre may handle cases needing equipment that cannot travel. Neither replaces an effective referral route. Priorities depend on the pattern of need, coverage and operating capacity, with benefits and limitations weighed for both choices.

### Programme evidence and limits

[WHO’s primary-health-care overview](https://www.who.int/health-topics/primary-health-care) describes community-centred services and broader health conditions. Its [2024 Namibia account](https://www.afro.who.int/news/namibia-reaches-key-milestone-eliminating-mother-child-transmission-hiv-and-hepatitis-b) reports integration of maternal-child and primary health services and progress on reducing vertical transmission. These show linked programmes, not proof that every household is served. Checked 20 October 2026; exact clinical procedures and schedules are outside this lesson.

## HIV and development

### HIV and development

HIV-related illness can affect work, household care and public resources in Namibia and other SADC economies, while effective care improves opportunities to remain well and participate. Accessible testing, treatment and support form part of a public-health response. Avoid treating HIV status as incapacity or using a fictional illness series as actual national prevalence evidence.

### Economic pathways

Illness or premature death can reduce available labour, increase caring work and divert household resources. Treatment access and support can reduce these burdens; stigma and exclusion can add avoidable economic costs independently of health status.

Applied example: Illness can reduce paid working hours and income. Caring responsibilities can reduce another household member’s time for work or education.

### System responses

HIV responses can combine prevention information, confidential testing, ongoing treatment, maternal-child services and community support. Continuity, trained services and non-stigmatising access matter. Individual treatment decisions belong with qualified health professionals, not a classroom model answer.

Applied example: Confidential testing and continued professional care support timely access to services. Accurate education and community support can reduce misinformation and barriers to participation.

### Follow the mechanism

A fictional household loses paid hours during a period of illness and spends savings on care and transport. A second adult’s caring responsibilities can reduce earnings further. Accessible professional services and income support may reduce some pressures, but the particular household’s circumstances matter. Do not infer an individual’s health or ability to work from an HIV diagnosis alone.

### Use accurate HIV language

[WHO’s HIV fact sheet](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hiv-aids), checked 20 October 2026, explains that treatment can control HIV and that ordinary contact such as sharing food does not transmit it. Do not use a diagnosis as a reason to exclude someone from schooling or work. This lesson does not prescribe individual treatment.
