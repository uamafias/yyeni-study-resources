## Patterns, sites and the settlement hierarchy

### The three settlement patterns

Every settled landscape shows one of three patterns, or a mixture of them. Nucleated means the buildings are clustered together round a centre, such as a church, a well, a market square or a road junction, so the settlement occupies one compact patch of ground. Dispersed means the buildings are spread out across the land, each homestead standing alone on the ground it works, so the settlement is everywhere and nowhere in particular. Linear means the buildings are strung along a line, such as a road, a river bank or a valley floor, so the settlement is long and thin. The distinction is the shape the buildings make on the map, not their number: a straggling line of fifty houses is linear, while a tight cluster of twenty around a church is nucleated. Each shape tells you something about the land and the livelihood that produced it, which is why reading a pattern comes first in this topic.

### Why each pattern forms

Each pattern forms because of what the land and the livelihood ask of the people. Nucleation suits a place where one point holds what everyone needs: a reliable spring in a dry country, a river crossing, a defensible hill in unsettled centuries, or a church and a market round which people chose to live. Dispersion suits farming where each family works its own land: on the commercial farms of Namibia's central plateau the homesteads stand on their own farms, kilometres apart, because the farm is the livelihood and the homestead belongs beside it. Linear settlement appears where a line supplies what the surrounding land lacks: along a river through dry country, along a road that carries trade, along a valley floor hemmed by slopes. The example worth holding onto is the Namib's ephemeral rivers: the Kuiseb and the Swakop carry thin ribbons of settlement along their banks while the desert either side stays empty, because in a dry land the water is found along a line. The pattern is not an accident of taste; it is the shape that water, relief and livelihood make sensible.

### Site, situation and function

Three words keep their senses in this topic, and answers turn on them. Site is the land a settlement is built on and its physical features: the water supply beside or beneath it, the relief of the ground, the soil, the building materials within reach and, in older centuries, the defensive value of the spot. Situation is the settlement's position relative to the area around it: the routes that pass or meet there, the other settlements within reach, the resources of the surroundings. Function is what the settlement does now: its main economic and social activities, whether market town, port, mining settlement, administrative centre or commuter town. A town can change its function twice in a century; its site, once chosen, is fixed.

### Good sites, and the reasons settlements grow

A site worth building on solved the problems of daily life: reliable water, because every household and its livestock drink daily; flat or gently sloping ground, because building and moving on it are cheap; soil fertile enough to feed the settlement; stone, timber or clay within reach; and safety, which in the old centuries meant rising ground or a river loop. Where water was scarce, the wet-point site beside a spring or river won; where floods were the danger, the dry-point site above the flood line won. A good site explains a beginning, however, not a whole history. Growth follows the situation: settlements grow where their position lets them serve routes and resources, at a ford or bridge where travellers must pass, at a harbour where ships load, at a junction where roads meet, beside a mine that employs, or in a capital where administration gathers work. Because situations change, functions change too: a market town swallowed by a city's commuter belt becomes a dormitory; a port left behind by bigger ships declines.

### Windhoek: a site and a situation

Windhoek shows both words at work. Its site offered water in a semi-arid land: the hot springs in the valley the Nama called Ai-Gams gave the place its first reason to exist, on a plateau basin at about 1 650 metres above sea level, temperate and level enough to build on. Its situation made more of it. The valley sits near the crossing of the routes that ran between the north and the south of the country, so the colonial administration chose it as its seat in 1890, and government brought roads, offices and the railway, which brought work, which brought people. At independence in 1990 the town became the capital of the new Namibia, and it now holds around 486 000 people (2023). A settlement founded on a spring grew into a city because its situation kept adding reasons to live there.

### The settlement hierarchy

Rank the settlements of a country by the number and order of their services and a hierarchy appears: hamlet, village, town, city. Two ideas drive it. A service needs a threshold population, the smallest number of people it must draw on to stay in business: a bread shop supports itself on a few hundred customers, a secondary school needs hundreds of children, a hospital tens of thousands of patients. And each service serves a sphere of influence, the area people travel from to use it: a village shop draws its village, a town's hospital draws its district. Low-order services are used often and need small thresholds, so they sit in small settlements; high-order services are used rarely and need large thresholds, so they sit in the largest settlements, and the people of the small ones travel to them. That is why the hierarchy exists: not because big places are grander, but because the sums of customers balance there.

### The hierarchy in Namibia

Namibia shows the ladder plainly. Windhoek, about 486 000 people (2023), holds the country's main hospital, its university and its international airport, services of the highest order. Oshakati, about 59 000 people (2023), holds an intermediate hospital and a large open market that draw patients and traders from across the north. A village of the communal lands holds a primary school, a clinic and a shop or two, services its few hundred people can just support. People travel up the ladder: the villager shops at home daily, in Oshakati monthly, in Windhoek for what neither lower rung offers.

### Ranked by services, not by size

The common error is to treat the hierarchy as a league of physical size or population alone, or to swap goods for services. The ranking counts services and their order: a spread-out settlement with one shop is not higher than a compact town with a hospital, whatever their areas. And the distinction between goods and services matters when naming what a place has: a good is something bought and carried away, such as bread, a blanket or a bicycle, whereas a service is work done for the customer, such as teaching, medical care or hair cutting. The test of a ranking: is it counting services, and is each thing named a service rather than a good?

## Case study: the Oshana corridor

### The Oshana corridor

Three fast-growing towns sit within about 30 kilometres of each other in the Oshana region: Oshakati, about 59 000 people (2023); Ongwediva, about 34 000; and Ondangwa, about 30 000. Around and between them lies one of the densest farmed landscapes in Namibia, a rural population of about 230 000 (2023) whose homesteads and mahangu fields fill the ground the towns have not taken. The towns live by serving that countryside. Oshakati began as an administrative and market centre and was proclaimed a town in 1992; Ondangwa grew at the railway and road junction; Ongwediva is the newest, an administrative and shopping centre. Each town's sphere of influence runs deep into the surrounding villages, and each day the buses and pickups of those villages bring people to the towns' services.

### Services and thresholds in the corridor

Walk up the corridor's ladder and the services sort as the hierarchy predicts. The villages keep a clinic, a primary school and small shops, services a few hundred people can support. Each town holds secondary schools, filling stations, banks and wholesalers. The highest order concentrates: only Oshakati holds the intermediate hospital, which serves patients from across the north, and only Ondangwa holds the airport. Oshakati's open market, hundreds of stalls trading grain, clothes and livestock goods, shows a large threshold at work: no village could gather enough traders and buyers to pay for it, so the market stands where the whole sphere of influence can reach it in a day. Service provision here is the hierarchy of the first half of this topic, printed onto one small area of the map.
