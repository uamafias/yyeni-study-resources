## Industrial systems and where factories locate

### An industrial system has three parts

Every factory, cannery or workshop is an industrial system with three parts. Inputs are what goes in: raw materials, labour, energy and capital - the money and machines the system needs. Processes are what happens inside: the making, heating, assembling and packing that change raw materials into products. Outputs are what comes out: the products the system sells, and the waste it creates while making them - gases from chimneys, waste water released or dumped, and solid waste such as offcuts and packaging. The parts connect in one direction: the processes consume the inputs and release the outputs.

### Processing changes the material

Processing means changing a raw material into a new product, and the change is what makes it processing: clay becomes bricks, pilchards become tinned fish, logs become planks. Any flow in a system diagram can be sorted with one test: does it go into the factory, or come out of it? Fish landed at the quay is an input; sealed tins on a pallet are an output. Defining the processes as "the processing" explains nothing - write what is done to the material, in what order, and what leaves at the other end. The flows balance, too: what enters the system leaves again, either as product or as waste.

### A worked system: a fish cannery

The pilchard canneries of Walvis Bay show the system in rounded figures. Inputs: about 40 000 tonnes of fish a year, tins, oil and salt, labour from the town, and energy for the canning lines. Processes: the fish are cleaned, cooked, packed into tins, sealed and labelled. Outputs: around 32 000 tonnes of canned fish and roughly 8 000 tonnes of waste - heads, skins and trimmings sold on as fishmeal, plus waste water and steam. Four fifths of the intake by mass leaves as product, one fifth as waste. Reading the three rows together is the point: the table is a system, and the numbers on either side of the processes must add up.

### The factors that decide where a factory goes

Factories locate where the balance of factors favours them: raw materials, labour, energy supply, transport routes, markets, flat land and government policy. Each factor should be given its direction in an answer. A bulk-losing industry such as copper smelting or cement sits beside its raw material, because most of the material's mass is discarded in processing and hauling ore is costly. A bakery sits in its market town, because loaves are bulkier and perishable compared with the flour they come from. A footloose industry using light, valuable components may prefer a motorway junction or a city with skilled workers. Government policy can tip the balance: tax breaks and serviced industrial estates have drawn factories to particular towns that raw materials alone would not have.

### Location is not impact

Two errors appear again and again in answers on this topic. The first is answering a location question with the factory's impacts - jobs created, noise made, dust raised - which describes what the factory does here rather than why it is here. The second is choosing an example at the wrong scale: a whole country cannot serve as an industrial zone, because a case study needs a named factory or estate with place-specific detail - small enough to be marked on a town map.

## Case study: the Ohorongo cement works

### The works and its place

The Ohorongo cement works stands beside Otavi, a small town on Namibia's north-south railway in the Otjozondjupa region. Production began in 2011 after an investment of about N$2.5 billion, one of the largest private industrial projects in the country's history, and the works employs around 300 permanent workers (2010s figure), with many more during the building phase. Its raw material is local: marble-rich rock is cut at a quarry beside the works and fed to the kilns. The works is thus a factory with its inputs at hand - the quarry on site, the workforce in the town next door, and the railway a short haul away.

### Why Otavi?

Each location factor earns its place. Raw material: the quarry sits beside the works, and cement-making is bulk-losing - well over a tonne of rock goes into each tonne of cement - so the works belongs at the rock face, not hauling stone for hundreds of kilometres. Transport: Otavi is a railway junction, so fuel and equipment arrive by rail and cement leaves the same way, north to the towns of the region and south towards the coast. Labour and services: the town supplies workers, water and power connections without new building. Market: northern Namibia's growing towns and building trade lie close by, and cement is costly to carry far, so a works near its buyers keeps delivery journeys short.

### The works as a system

The same factory drawn as an industrial system: inputs of quarry rock, kiln fuel, electricity, water and labour from Otavi; processes that crush and grind the rock, burn it into clinker in the kilns, then grind it again with additives into cement; outputs of bagged and bulk cement for the building trade, plus the waste streams, kiln gases, dust caught by filters, and warm air. The three-part pattern from the first section, now filled with a real place.

### Where this connects

The kilns burn fuel, which ties the works to the energy sources of topic 3.5, and the dust and gases raise the environmental risks of topic 3.7. Its jobs are part of the employment structure of topic 3.1.
