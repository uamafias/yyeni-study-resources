## Population structure and the pyramid

### What structure means

Population structure is the make-up of a population by age groups and sex, shown on an age/sex pyramid. The bars are age groups, youngest at the bottom, with males on one side and females on the other. The shape is a record of the birth and death rates a country has had: a high birth rate builds a wide base of children, and an old, low-mortality population builds a top-heavy pyramid. A wide base comes from a high birth rate and marks a growing population; a narrow base with a top-heavy shape comes from a low birth rate and an ageing one, and as a falling birth rate works through, the base narrows first and the ageing follows decades later. Describing structure means naming the broad groups and their proportions, young dependants aged 0-14, the economically active aged 15-64, and elderly dependants aged 65 and over, not just the outline of the drawing.

### Reading a pyramid

Two reading errors are common. The first is describing the shape, wide base, narrow top, and stopping there, when the structure is the groups and their shares. The second is reading one bar or one side: an age group's share is males plus females in that group, so a single bar undercounts it. A pyramid answer names the groups, gives their proportions and adds both sexes.

### Dependancy and its weight

The dependent population is the people too young or too old to work, who rely on the economically active, and the dependency ratio compares them with the people of working age. A high ratio means each worker supports more people, and the burden lands differently by kind: a mostly young dependent population needs schools, clinics and feeding, and a mostly old one needs pensions, health care and carers. In both cases the active group must fund its own households and the dependants' too, which is why a high dependent population shifts a country's spending whether the dependants are at the bottom of the pyramid or the top.

### Where this connects

The birth and death rates that build a pyramid are the ones defined in topic 1.1, and Niger, this topic's case study, is the high-growth case study there too. Population density and distribution, topic 1.4, show where the people in the pyramid actually live.

## Case study: Niger, a high dependent population

### The structure and its cause

Niger, in the Sahel south of the Sahara, has one of the world's highest dependent populations: nearly half its people are under 15, around 44 per cent in the early 2020s, against a small economically active share, so the dependency ratio is high and each worker supports many children. The cause is the birth rate, around 45 per 1000, which keeps adding large cohorts of children while the adult group stays thin, because fertility has not yet fallen: girls marry young, average only a few years of school, and children support their parents on the family farms. The pyramid this builds has a very wide base and a very narrow top, but the marks are in the groups and shares, not the silhouette.

### The implications

The implications of Niger's structure follow from the ratio. A school-age cohort too large for the classrooms and teachers available means children are taught in shifts and classes of dozens, so learning thins out. Clinics are stretched by child and maternal care, because the patients are mostly young, and too few adults exist to fund it all: the tax-paying, farming, working group is the thin band in the middle of the pyramid. So the country faces a double task, educating and caring for the widest generation it has ever had with the narrowest workforce it has ever had.

### Where this connects

This case study answers any 7-mark question that names a country with a high dependent population, and Niger is also the high natural growth case in topic 1.1: the same birth rate that grows the population builds the young structure here. A contrasting ageing case is Japan, in topic 1.1's case studies.
