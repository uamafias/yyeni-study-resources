## Weather: instruments, calculations and graphs

### The instruments and how they are used

A weather station reads its instruments at the same time each day, usually the morning observation at 08:00. The rain gauge stands in open ground, sunk so its rim is level with the surface and well away from buildings or trees that would shelter it; each morning the water it has collected is poured into a measuring cylinder and read in millimetres. The day's temperatures are read from the maximum-minimum thermometer, kept inside the Stevenson screen: the maximum thermometer holds a small steel index that the rising mercury or spirit pushes up and leaves in place, so it marks the highest temperature since the last reset; the minimum thermometer works the same way in the opposite direction, marking the lowest. After reading, both indexes are drawn back - by shaking the thermometer or with a magnet - so the next day can record afresh. Humidity is read from the wet-and-dry bulb thermometer: two matched thermometers stand side by side, one with its bulb wrapped in a damp muslin wick, and the drier the air, the faster the wick's water evaporates and the cooler that bulb reads, so the difference between the two is a measure of how humid the air is. Sunshine is measured by the sunshine recorder: a glass sphere focuses the sun's rays onto a paper card, burning a trace whose length is read off in hours of bright sunshine. The barometer measures air pressure in hectopascals or millibars. Outside the screen, the anemometer measures wind speed as its cups spin, and the wind vane shows wind direction, named for the point of the compass the wind blows from - an easterly wind is one blowing from the east. Simple digital instruments now record several of these readings automatically, but each still needs siting and reading with the same care.

### The Stevenson screen

Thermometers must read the air, not the sun-warmed wall or the baked ground, and the Stevenson screen is the box that makes that possible. It is a white wooden box standing on legs about one metre high, sited in the open, well away from buildings and shade. Its features each do one job, and the reasons are worth naming exactly. The white paint reflects sunlight, so the box itself is not heated by the sun. The sides are louvred - slatted with gaps - so air flows freely through the box while the slats keep the thermometers in the shade. The legs hold the box about one metre above the ground, so the thermometers read the air temperature rather than the heat given off by the sun-warmed surface beneath. It is animals, splash or sunshine that a screen is sometimes said to guard against in vague answers; the precise jobs are shade, free-flowing air and height above the ground.

### How it is used, not only how it works

A question that asks how an instrument is used is often answered with how it works - the mechanism of the steel index or the burning card - and the answer earns little, because it answers a different question. Using an instrument means saying what is done with it: where it is sited (the rain gauge sunk in open ground, the screen a metre above it), when it is read (the morning observation), how it is read (the cylinder for the rain, the indexes for the temperatures), and how it is reset for the next reading (drawn back with a magnet or by shaking). The test is one question: does the answer tell the reader what to do with the instrument? A mechanism describes the instrument; a use describes the observation.

### Calculations from weather readings

Most weather calculations are one subtraction, one addition or one division, and each answer carries its unit. The diurnal temperature range is the day's maximum minus the day's minimum: a day that peaks at 31 degrees Celsius and falls to 13 degrees Celsius has a diurnal range of 31 - 13 = 18 degrees Celsius. The annual rainfall total adds the twelve monthly figures: a year whose wettest month brings 82 mm adds its months the same way, two at a time, to reach the annual total in millimetres. A mean temperature divides the total by the number of readings: five days totalling 95 degrees Celsius give 95 / 5 = 19 degrees Celsius as the mean. And the wet-bulb depression, the key humidity figure, is the dry bulb minus the wet bulb: a dry bulb of 24 degrees Celsius against a wet bulb of 19 degrees Celsius gives 24 - 19 = 5 degrees Celsius, which the humidity table turns into a relative humidity figure. The rule behind all four: the calculation uses the readings the question names, and the answer carries its unit.

### The wrong pair of readings

The commonest error in a weather calculation is subtracting the wrong pair of readings: the maximum from one day against the minimum from the next, the wet bulb from the wrong thermometer, or the ends of the wrong month. A calculated value is only as good as the readings it uses, so the working should name them. The test is to ask which two readings the calculation uses: the diurnal range is one day's maximum and the same day's minimum; the wet-bulb depression is the dry bulb minus the wet bulb read at the same time; the annual temperature range is the hottest month's mean minus the coolest month's mean. Name the readings, do the arithmetic, give the unit - in that order.

### Reading weather and climate graphs

Weather and climate data reach you as graphs and diagrams, and each has its reading rules. A climate graph puts the months along the horizontal axis, the mean temperature as a line against one scale on the right, and the rainfall totals as bars against a scale on the left; read the values with their units before saying anything about them. A wind rose shows the share of the time the wind blew from each point of the compass, so the longest spoke names the prevailing wind. Where a graph shows cloud cover or pressure over a run of days, the reading is the trend: the highest value, the lowest value, and the direction of change between them. Describing the pattern means quoting those values - "rainfall rises from 2 mm in June to a peak of 180 mm in January" - because a pattern described without figures is an impression, and an impression cannot be marked.
