## Earthquakes and volcanoes

### Types and features of volcanoes and earthquakes

An earthquake is the shaking of the ground when energy stored in rock is released along a fault. Two points fix its position and size. The focus is the point underground where the rock breaks and the shaking starts. The epicentre is the point on the surface directly above the focus, and it is where the shaking is usually felt most strongly. The magnitude of an earthquake is the amount of energy it releases, measured on a scale: each step up the scale marks an earthquake releasing many times more energy than the step before, so a magnitude 7 event is far more powerful than one of magnitude 6, not a little larger.

A volcano is an opening in the crust through which molten rock reaches the surface. Its main features are the magma chamber below, where molten rock collects; the vent, the channel up which it rises; and the crater, the bowl-shaped opening at the top. Two types of volcano matter here. A strato-volcano, also called a composite cone, is a steep-sided cone built of alternating layers of sticky lava and ash, which erupts explosively and grows tall and narrow. A shield volcano is a wide, gently sloping mountain built of runny lava that flows a long way before it sets. Namibia's flat-topped Etendeka lavas in Damaraland record runny lava flooding across the land when the South Atlantic was opening, more than 100 million years ago.

### Telling the pairs apart

Two pairs of terms are often swapped, and each swap is a real error rather than a slip of spelling. First, focus and epicentre: the focus is underground, where the earthquake starts; the epicentre is at the surface, directly above. Second, strato-volcano and shield volcano: the strato-volcano is steep, layered with ash and sticky lava; the shield is wide and gentle, built of runny lava. Two quick tests sort them out. Underground or at the surface? Steep or gentle? Before writing either pair, ask which one the question means.

### The global pattern

Earthquakes and volcanoes are not scattered evenly over the Earth. They run in long, narrow belts along the boundaries where plates meet, and between those belts the interiors of plates are largely quiet. The greatest cluster lies around the Pacific Ocean, where the Pacific plate collides and grinds with its neighbours: the belt is nicknamed the Ring of Fire, and it holds about three quarters of the world's active volcanoes and many of the largest earthquakes. A second belt runs from the Mediterranean through the mountains of the Middle East and southern Asia. Namibia sits well inside a plate, far from any boundary, which is why strong earthquakes are rare there and no volcano has erupted there in recent geological time.

### Plate movement and its three settings

The Earth's crust is broken into large plates that move slowly over the mantle. It is the plates that move: a plate boundary is simply the line where two of them meet, and the kind of meeting decides the hazards.

At a constructive, or divergent, boundary two plates move apart. Magma rises into the gap and builds new crust, which usually means gentle shield volcanoes and shallow earthquakes: Iceland sits on such a boundary beneath the Atlantic, where the plates are pulling apart. At a destructive, or convergent, boundary plates move towards each other, and where an oceanic plate meets a continental one it is bent downwards into the mantle in a subduction zone. The descending plate melts at depth, and the rising magma feeds steep strato-volcanoes inland of the trench; the largest earthquakes also happen where these plates snag and jolt. At a conservative boundary two plates slide past each other sideways, as along the San Andreas fault in California: the plates grind and stick, which stores energy until it is released as an earthquake, but no magma rises, so there are earthquakes and no volcanoes.

### Boundaries and patterns

Two errors appear again and again in answers about distribution. The first is making the boundary the moving thing: plates move, and the boundary between them stays where the plates meet, so say "the plate moves towards...", and name the setting - constructive, destructive or conservative - that fits the example in front of you. The second is stopping at "they occur on plate boundaries". A distribution answer must describe the pattern: where the events are (long, narrow belts), how they are grouped (the Pacific cluster above all), and where they are not (the quiet plate interiors).

### From movement to damage

Why the ground moves. An earthquake begins with plate movement. As two plates push past each other, their rough edges catch and lock, and the rocks near the boundary bend and store energy like a compressed spring. When the stress grows beyond the strength of the rock, the fault snaps: the focus is the point where the break happens, and the stored energy races out through the rock as seismic waves. The waves reach the surface at the epicentre, and the ground shakes, hardest near the epicentre and near the focus itself. When the fault lies under the sea, the jolt can throw up a sea wave that races across the ocean as a tsunami.

Why a volcano erupts. Where plates pull apart, magma rises easily and pours out quietly. Where a plate subducts, the melted rock is sticky and full of gas: the trapped gas builds pressure until the mountain bursts open, throwing out ash and pyroclastic flows.

From event to damage. The shaking topples weak buildings, gas and electricity lines burst into flame, and slopes give way as landslides. How much damage follows depends on the size of the event and on the people: a shallow focus near a crowded city shakes harder than a deep, distant one; weak brick buildings collapse where reinforced frames stand; and a place with trained emergency services recovers faster than one without.

### What volcanoes give

Volcanoes are far from being a danger and nothing else; the communities around them are usually there because of what the mountain gives. Ash thrown out by eruptions weathers into some of the most fertile soil there is, because it is rich in minerals and holds moisture well, and the lower slopes of volcanoes in Java, Italy and the Virunga range of central Africa carry dense farming for that reason. Steam rising from hot underground rock can be tapped as geothermal energy, a renewable source that warms buildings and drives turbines without burning fuel. And a smoking crater draws visitors: tourism around volcanoes such as Vesuvius in Italy brings guides, guesthouses and food sellers an income.

### What volcanoes take

A volcano's dangers come in several forms. Lava flows bury farmland and roads, though runny lava often moves slowly enough to be walked away from. Pyroclastic flows are deadlier: avalanches of hot gas, ash and rock fragments that sweep down the slopes at storm speed, destroying everything they cross. Ash falls far from the vent, and a thick, wet fall can crush roofs, smother crops, close airports and choke the lungs. On the slopes, loose ash and heavy rain combine into lahars - rivers of mud that pour down valleys and bury bridges, fields and houses. Gases released at the crater poison the air close by.

### Problems or benefits?

Questions about volcanoes name the side they want, and the commonest error in this topic is answering the other one. If the question asks for the problems, give hazards: lava, pyroclastic flows, ash, lahars, gases. If it asks for the benefits or opportunities, give fertile soil, geothermal energy, tourism. The test is a single question: problems or benefits? Find the word that tells you which side the question names, then answer that side.

### Reducing the impacts

Nothing can stop an earthquake or an eruption, but their impacts can be reduced, and the ways differ because the two hazards give different warning.

Volcanoes usually warn before they erupt. Magma moving up through cracks shows itself as swarms of small earthquakes, swelling of the ground and rising gas emissions, and observatories watch for exactly these signs. When they strengthen, the authorities can close the mountain, warn the villages and move people out in good time - the evacuation of around 350,000 people from Merapi's slopes before its largest 2010 flows is a working example of warning turned into action.

Earthquakes give no reliable warning, so preparation is built into the structures and the people. Buildings can be designed to survive: reinforced concrete or steel frames that flex, deep foundations, and strict building regulations that enforce both. People can be drilled to act: knowing where to shelter, how to shut off gas, and where to gather. Emergency services can be trained and equipped in advance, with water, medicine and shelter stockpiled, so the help arrives on the first day instead of the fourth.

### Say what differs, exactly

When two places suffer the same size of hazard and one suffers far more, an answer must say exactly what differed - not "better infrastructure" or "building quality", which name nothing. The exact differences are reinforced frames and deep foundations, trained emergency services, warning systems and drills, and rules that enforce them. A reader should be able to picture what was built or done in one place and not the other; if the answer offers a vague phrase, it has not explained the difference.

## Case studies: the Nepal earthquake and Mount Merapi

### Case study: the Nepal earthquake, 2015

On 25 April 2015 an earthquake of magnitude 7.8 struck Nepal, a poor, mountainous country between India and China along the Himalaya. Its focus lay about 15 kilometres deep beneath Gorkha district, roughly 80 kilometres north-west of the capital, Kathmandu; the epicentre sat among steep, farmed valleys. The boundary is destructive: the Indian plate pushes northwards beneath the Eurasian plate, and the same collision that raises the Himalaya loaded the rocks that snapped that day. Aftershocks followed for weeks, including one of magnitude 7.3 on 12 May, which brought down buildings the first quake had cracked.

Nearly 9,000 people died and thousands more were injured. In the Kathmandu valley, weak brick towers and homes collapsed - the nine-storey Dharahara tower, a landmark of the city, fell with people inside it - and historic squares such as Durbar Square were reduced to rubble. In the hill districts of Gorkha and Sindhupalchok, landslides buried whole slopes and blocked roads, so villages went days without help. The quake shook snow from the slopes above Mount Everest and swept the base camp, killing climbers. With the monsoon weeks away, survivors camped in the open while fields, cattle and houses went untended, and the tourist season that feeds the valley's economy stopped at once.

### Case study: Mount Merapi, Indonesia

Merapi, whose name means mountain of fire, is a strato-volcano that rises about 30 kilometres north of Yogyakarta, a city of some 400,000 people on the Indonesian island of Java. Its setting is destructive: the Indo-Australian plate slides northwards beneath the Eurasian plate off Java's coast, and the melted rock feeds Merapi's sticky, gas-rich magma - the recipe for explosive eruptions.

Merapi erupts every few years, and its 2010 eruption was its largest in more than a century. From late October, pyroclastic flows of hot gas and rock swept down the valleys on its southern flank, destroying villages; about 350 people died, and around 350,000 were evacuated from the danger zone. Ash closed the airport at Yogyakarta and buried crops over a wide area.

Yet the slopes are densely farmed, because the same ash weathers into fertile soil: vegetables, rice and fruit grow in it, and the volcano's sand is dug for building. A government observatory watches Merapi day and night; in 2010 its monitoring of earthquake swarms, swelling ground and gas let the authorities order the big evacuations before the largest flows of 5 November, which is why the death toll, though heavy, was far below the losses of earlier eruptions.

### Using the pair in an answer

A 7-mark case-study answer names the case and stays with it. For an earthquake question, Nepal 2015 supplies the cause (the destructive collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates), the features (magnitude 7.8, focus about 15 kilometres deep beneath Gorkha district, epicentre about 80 kilometres from Kathmandu) and the effects (nearly 9,000 deaths, the fallen Dharahara tower, landslides that cut off hill villages, the ruined tourist season). For a volcano question, Merapi 2010 supplies the type (a strato-volcano near Yogyakarta), the hazards (pyroclastic flows, ash) and the management (observatory monitoring, staged evacuation of around 350,000 people, about 350 deaths). The place-specific details - Gorkha, the Dharahara tower, the 5 November flows - are what lift an answer from general statements to a real case study.

### Connections

The pair teaches together: the same destructive-boundary setting, two different warnings. Nepal had no earthquake warning and weak buildings; Merapi was monitored and evacuated in time. Use Nepal for questions about causes and effects, and either case study - or both - for questions about reducing impacts.
