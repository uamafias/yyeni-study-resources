## Rivers: drainage basins, processes, landforms, hazards and management

### The drainage basin and its water

A river is a channel that carries water from high ground to the sea. The land it serves is the drainage basin: all the land whose rainfall drains into one river and the streams that join it. The watershed is the ridge of high land that separates one basin from the next, so rain falling on one side of it belongs to one river and rain on the other side to another. A tributary is a smaller river that joins a larger one, and each confluence, the point where two rivers join, adds the tributary's water to the main channel. The river begins at its source, usually in hills, and ends at its mouth, where it meets the sea or a lake.

Rain reaching the basin takes several paths, and the path decides the river's behaviour. Some rain is intercepted by leaves and evaporates without reaching the ground. Some soaks into the soil by infiltration, then moves sideways through the soil towards the river as throughflow, or sinks deeper into rock as groundwater flow; both release their water slowly. Water that cannot soak in runs over the surface as overland flow, which arrives in the channel within minutes. A basin of forest and absorbent soil soaks up most of a storm and feeds its river gently for days; a basin of bare rock, baked soil or town roofs and roads sends the same storm racing into the channel as overland flow.

### Which way does the river run?

Two habits ruin map answers. The first is reading the river backwards: rivers flow from the source, where the channel begins high in the hills, downhill to the mouth at the sea or a lake - so on a map the water moves from the small upstream end towards the thicker, downstream channel, and contour lines tell you which way is downhill. The second is swapping the pair that begins with the same letter: a tributary joins the main river, whereas a distributary splits away from it, as the channels of a delta do. The test is two questions: does the channel join the river or leave it, and which way is downhill?

### How a river changes along its course

A river's characteristics are its width, depth, speed of flow and discharge. Discharge is the volume of water passing a point each second, measured in cubic metres per second, and it is the characteristic that grows most steadily: near the source a stream may carry 2 cubic metres per second, while the same river near its mouth may carry more than a hundred, because every tributary adds water. Width and depth also increase downstream as the channel grows to hold that water. Speed is less simple: the upper river plunges steeply but drags over boulders, the lower river slides gently but smoothly, so speed often falls at first and then rises again on the smoother channel. The long profile - the river's gradient from source to mouth - is steep at the top and nearly flat at the sea, which is why the upper river erodes downwards while the lower river spreads and deposits.

### Erosion: how the river wears the land

A river erodes in three main ways. Hydraulic action is the force of the water itself: fast water slams into cracks in the bed and banks, compressing the air inside until the pressure helps split the rock apart, and loose material is simply torn from the channel. Abrasion is erosion by the load: the stones, gravel and sand the river carries are thrown against the bed and banks, grinding them like sandpaper, which is what smooths and deepens the channel. Corrosion is chemical: water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone, taking it away in solution. On the outside of bends, where the water is fastest, all three work hardest and the bank wears back into a river cliff.

### Transport and deposition: the river's load

The material a river carries is its load, and it travels in four ways: dissolved in solution, suspended as fine particles clouding the water, bounced along the bed in a movement called saltation, and dragged as larger stones. The rule that governs all of it is energy: the faster the flow, the larger the particles the water can move - a torrent can shift boulders, a sluggish river can carry little more than silt and clay. Deposition is the reverse of the rule. Wherever the water slows - entering a lake or the sea, spreading over a flood plain, or drifting into an eddy on the inside of a bend - the river drops its load, heaviest particles first. The bend shows the rule in miniature: fastest water on the outside erodes, slowest water on the inside deposits a slip-off slope of sand and gravel.

### Landforms of the upper course

The upper river, falling steeply over rock, cuts downwards. Vertical erosion carves a steep-sided gorge with a narrow channel. Where the river crosses a band of resistant rock over softer rock beneath, the softer rock erodes faster, which leaves the hard band as a step: water pours over it as a waterfall, and the plunging water and its stones scour a plunge pool at its base by hydraulic action and abrasion. The pool undercuts the hard lip until it collapses, so the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-walled gorge of its former positions. On the rocky bed, pebbles trapped in eddies grind circular potholes that deepen with every flood.

### Landforms of the middle and lower course

As the gradient eases, the river stops cutting down and starts to wander: bends - meanders - swing across a wide, flat flood plain, the flat land beside a river that is covered by water when it floods. The flood plain itself is the river's own work: each flood leaves a layer of silt, and the layers build up over centuries. In flood the river spills over its banks, and the first water to leave the channel slows at once, dropping its coarsest sediment along the channel edge; flood after flood this builds a levée, a raised natural bank beside the river, with finer silt spread further out. In the middle course the bends grow until the neck of land between two loops narrows; in a large flood the river cuts straight across the neck, then deposition seals the ends of the abandoned loop, which is left as an oxbow lake. At the mouth, the river meets the sea, the current is checked, and the dropped load builds a delta, flat land crossed by distributaries pushing the coast seaward.

### Describe the feature, or explain its formation?

Landform questions come in two angles. Describe asks what the feature is like: its shape, its size with a scale, and its position in the landscape. Explain asks how it formed, step by step through the processes. A learner who answers a describe question with the formation sequence has answered a different question. For a waterfall, the features are a vertical drop of water over a rock step, a plunge pool at its base and a gorge below; the formation is differential erosion, undercutting and collapse, in that order. Read the command word first: what it is like, or how it formed?

### Where erosion, where deposition?

Two errors appear constantly in answers about meanders and oxbow lakes. The first puts erosion on the inside of the bend: erosion works on the outside, where the water is fastest, and deposition on the inside, where it is slowest - so the river cliff is on the outer bend and the slip-off slope on the inner. The second says an oxbow lake or a delta is formed by erosion alone: both are made by deposition - the oxbow when dropped sediment seals the cut-off neck, the delta as the river's load piles up where the water slows at the sea. The test is to ask where the water is fastest and where it slows, and to name deposition wherever the river drops its load.

### Causes or impacts?

A flood question names the half of the story it wants. The causes of a flood come before it: heavy or prolonged rainfall, rapid snowmelt, deforestation that cuts interception, and urban surfaces that seal the soil - the things that put more water into the channel than it can carry. The impacts come after it: drowned crops, ruined homes, people made homeless, disease in stagnant water. A learner who answers a causes question with impacts - or who lists how useful the river is instead of either - has answered a different question. The test is to ask of every point: does this come before the flood or after it?

### From a storm to a flooded town

Follow a chain. A catchment that was forested is cleared for farmland. Rain that was once caught by leaves now reaches the ground whole, and where the soil is baked or the surface is sealed by roads and roofs, infiltration is small. Overland flow begins, and the water runs down the bare slopes, which means it reaches the tributaries in minutes rather than soaking through soil for days. The tributaries pour their arriving water into the main river together, so the river's discharge climbs fast, past bank-full, which means the flood peak overtops the banks. Downstream the river spreads over the town's flood plain: crops drown and topsoil is stripped, homes and their contents are ruined, people are made homeless, and disease follows in stagnant water. Every step of the chain is a process this topic names - interception lost, overland flow, rising discharge, bank-full, flood - and an explanation question asks you to walk it in order.

### Why people live beside rivers

People have settled beside rivers throughout history, because a river's land is generous. The flood plain is flat and easy to build and farm on. Its silt is fertile, renewed each time the river floods, which means harvests are strong without heavy fertiliser. The water itself is the opportunity that matters most: pumped onto fields it is irrigation, in homes it is washing and drinking, in factories and turbines it is power and process water. Rivers carried trade for centuries, and fishing still feeds communities along them. These are reasons many of the world's great cities grew on flood plains - and also why the hazard stays close at hand, because the same flat land the river made fertile is the land it returns to claim.

### Managing the flood

Flood management measures work in two ways. Engineering works fight the water directly. A dam and reservoir built upstream stores the storm's water and releases it slowly, which lowers the peak passing the settlements below. Raised embankment walls lift the banks so the channel holds more before it spills. Straightening, widening and deepening the channel lets the river push its flood through faster. The other way works with the basin instead. Flood-plain zoning steers new building off the lowest ground, so the flood spreads over parks and fields instead of houses. Afforestation across the basin restores interception and infiltration, which sends less water quickly to the channel. Flood warnings, from river gauges and radio alerts, give people time to move stock and themselves. Each choice has limits: dams and embankments are costly to build and hold back floods where they stand, while zoning, planting and warnings change the damage but not the water - so the right mix depends on the settlement's size, the money available and the warning time the river gives.

## Case study: the Orange River

### The Orange River: opportunities

The Orange is southern Africa's longest river. It rises as the Senqu in the mountains of Lesotho and flows westward about 2,200 kilometres across South Africa, forming the border with Namibia over its last stretch and entering the Atlantic at Alexander Bay; at Augrabies it plunges about 60 metres through a rocky gorge. Its lower valley is desert, with rainfall often under 100 millimetres a year, so the river's water is the opportunity that matters most. At Aussenkehr and Noordoewer on the Namibian stretch, irrigation schemes pump river water onto vineyards whose table grapes are exported abroad, and along the South African bank at Vioolsdrif the same water feeds smallholdings. Far upstream, the Gariep Dam, completed in 1971 and South Africa's largest reservoir, stores water for towns and farms and generates hydro-electric power.

### The hazard and its management

The Orange floods in the summer rainy season, when storms over its huge basin gather into a high pulse downstream. In 1988 one of the largest floods of the twentieth century on the river inundated camps and vineyards along the lower Orange, damaging the irrigation schemes around Kakamas and Aussenkehr and sweeping away pumps and piping. Management has partly succeeded since. The Gariep Dam (1971) and the Vanderkloof Dam (1977) upstream store and release water steadily, which lowers the flood peaks reaching the schemes, and embankment walls and rebuilt pump stations protect the irrigated land. Summer floods still interrupt farming, so the hazard is reduced rather than removed.
