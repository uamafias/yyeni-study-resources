## Mapwork: the skills of the extract

### Grid references

A map is divided into squares by eastings, the vertical lines numbered west to east, and northings, the horizontal lines numbered south to north. A four-figure reference names a whole square by the lines at its bottom-left corner: square 4664 is the square whose corner is easting 46 and northing 64. A six-figure reference locates a point inside the square by adding a third figure to each pair: the tenths of the way across, then the tenths of the way up. The order is fixed, eastings first, then northings, along the corridor, then up the stairs, and the figures come from the grid lines, never from road numbers or other labels on the sheet. A point halfway across square 4664 and three-tenths up is 465463: the easting 46 becomes 465, the northing 64 becomes 643.

### Directions and bearings

A compass direction is a point of the compass, given as the direction of one place from another: north-north-east, south-west, east. A bearing is different: an angle measured clockwise from grid north, in degrees, so east is 090, south is 180 and west is 270. A bearing of 135 is south-east; the two answers name the same direction in different systems, and a question asks for one or the other by name. Bearings are measured clockwise from grid north with a protractor, its centre on the starting point and its zero line along grid north.

### Distances along a route

To measure a straight-line distance, lay the paper's edge between the two points, mark both, and set the marks against the scale line. To measure a distance along a route, a road, a river, a path, follow the route with the straight edge of a sheet of paper, marking each bend on the paper as you go, then lay the whole chain of marks on the scale line and read the total. A straight-line measurement between the ends underestimates a winding route, sometimes badly. On a 1:50 000 map, 1 centimetre on the map is 0.5 kilometres on the ground; on a 1:25 000 map, 1 centimetre is 0.25 kilometres. A route measuring 6.4 centimetres on the 1:50 000 sheet is 6.4 x 0.5 = 3.2 kilometres on the ground.

### Heights from contours

Contours join points of equal height, shown on most extracts every 10 metres. Read them in three ways. The height of a place is the contour it sits on or between, or the spot height printed at the point, which is exact where the contour is approximate. The difference in height between two places is the highest contour crossed minus the lowest: from the 40-metre contour to the 120-metre contour is 120 - 40 = 80 metres of climb. The spacing of the contours gives the steepness: bunched contours mean a steep slope, widely spaced ones a gentle one, and a cluster of contours close together against a contour far apart marks a cliff. A triangulation pillar marks a surveyed high point.

### Cross-sections

A cross-section shows the shape of the land along a line drawn on the map. Draw the line, mark every contour it crosses, and plot each crossing against distance and height on a grid: the line joining the plotted points is the profile of the slope. The vertical scale is usually drawn larger than the horizontal scale to make the shape readable, which is vertical exaggeration, and the exaggeration should be stated so the reader knows the slopes look steeper than they are. A section that rises in steps is a gentle slope becoming a steep one; a section with a broad low flat stretch between two rises is a valley with a flood plain in the middle.

### Translating the scale

A feature's real size is its map measurement multiplied by the scale. On a 1:50 000 map, a wood measuring 3 centimetres across is 3 x 0.5 = 1.5 kilometres wide on the ground; a 4-centimetre stretch of coast on a 1:25 000 sheet is 4 x 0.25 = 1 kilometre. The same conversion turns a real distance into a map distance for planning: 2 kilometres on the ground is 2 / 0.5 = 4 centimetres on the 1:50 000 sheet. Describing a feature in real terms, the width of the estuary, the length of the ridge, is this multiplication, and the answer carries its unit, kilometres.

### The key

The key is read before the map, because the symbols are the map's vocabulary. Each symbol carries a precise category: the key says national road, not highway; coniferous plantation, not trees; nonconiferous woodland, not wood. An answer that names a feature with the key's own term is right, and a general term is not. The symbols carry more than names: the width of a road symbol shows the road's importance, the style of a building symbol shows its use, a school, a church, a hospital, and the colour of an area shows its cover, blue for water, green for woodland, white for open ground.

### Drawing inferences from the map

A map is evidence, and the skill is to draw what it supports. Settlements cluster where the land is flat and water is near, so a line of villages along a valley floor is no accident; roads avoid the steepest gradients, so a main road that bends round a spur is taking the gentle way; land use follows the relief, so the rough grazing sits on the steep slopes and the arable fields on the flat. Every inference is drawn from stated evidence, the feature read from the map, the pattern it makes, and the conclusion the pattern supports, and an inference that names no evidence is a guess.

### Describing landscape features

A landscape description names the features and their character with geographical terms and simple adjectives: a wide flat-floored valley, a steep convex slope, a rocky headland, a nucleated village on the valley floor. The terms are the syllabus's own, the adjectives are simple, and together they say what the landscape is like without explaining it. Relief is the height and shape of the land; drainage is the water on it, the rivers and streams, their pattern, density and flow direction; the two are described separately, and a drainage feature that is not on the map cannot be described from it.

### Drainage and the relief

Drainage density is the length of stream channels per unit area. A wet upland carries closely spaced streams between bunched contours, high density; a dry lowland carries a few streams in wide valleys, low density. Stream patterns read from the shape of their joining: a dendritic pattern, branches like a tree, suits uniform rock; streams bending round parallel spurs mark a valley side; a stream flowing between two spurs sits in the syncline the contours draw. The flow direction comes from the heights: water flows from the high ground to the low, so the contours at the stream's two ends settle which way it runs.

### Coastlines and river channels

On a large-scale map a coastline reads three ways: cliffs show as a hard edge with contours bunched against it, a beach as a flat strip at sea level beside the low-water mark, and a marsh as flat ground with the wet symbols, reeds or tidal marks. A river channel changes downstream: a narrow, straight channel in the upper square, meanders and a broader channel in the middle, and a wide mouth with marsh in the last, because the discharge grows as tributaries join.

### Land use

Land use follows the land. Improved pasture and arable fields sit on the flat lowland where the ground can be ploughed; rough grazing climbs the slopes that machinery cannot; woodland clothes the steepest ground, where nothing else pays; settlement strings along the main routes. The map shows the pattern, the field shapes large and rectangular on the planned lowland, small and irregular on the old common land, and the inference comes from matching the use to the relief that suits it.

### Settlement patterns

Settlements sit in three patterns. Nucleated: the buildings grouped round a centre, a church, a green, a junction, the old defensive and market shape. Linear: the buildings strung along a line, a road or a valley floor, each house taking its frontage from the route. Dispersed: farms scattered across the area with no centre, the pattern of enclosure country where each holding stands on its own land. The pattern is described from the map, and its reason inferred: nucleation suits a village with a church and services, linear suits a route through enclosed ground, dispersal suits large farms worked from the homestead.

### Urban morphology

A town's shape reads on the large-scale sheet in zones. At the centre, a dense grid of tightly packed blocks, the commercial heart, with the market square, the chain stores and the offices. Around it, terraced streets and semi-detached rows, the nineteenth-century growth, small regular plots along straight streets. At the edge, detached housing with gardens and the post-war estates, where the streets curve and the plots widen, and beyond them the retail park and the bypass, the newest growth with its car parks. Reading the zones is reading the town's history, and the pattern of one zone into the next is the urban morphology the map shows.

### Functions, services, site and growth

A settlement's function is what it does, its main economic and social activities: a port town with docks and warehouses, a market town with a livestock market and a coaching inn, a resort with a promenade and a golf course. Its services are what it provides, the school, the hospital, the college, and the two together tell the region what the settlement is for. The site, the land the town stands on, was chosen for what it offered: dry flat ground, a water supply, a bridging point, a sheltered harbour or a defensive spur. Growth then followed the routes and the work: the town on the bridging point grew where the roads converged on its bridge.

### Communication networks

Communications are read in type and density. Type: motorway, dual carriageway, main road, minor road, track, mainline railway, branch line, navigable river, each with its own symbol and its own speed. Density: how close the routes stand, thick on the lowland where the settlement is dense, thin in the upland where each valley carries one road. The network follows the land, because routes take the cheapest gradient: the motorway runs along the flat, the minor road threads the valley, and the paths cross the ridge at the saddle where the climb is least.

### Grid references, worked

A worked extract to practise on; each cell is one grid square, with north at the top.

| northing \\ easting | 62 | 63 | 64 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 65 | ford, stream | village of Okapuka, church | plantation, track |
| 64 | steep slope, contours 40-80 m | main road junction | saw mill |
| 63 | marsh, reeds | farmstead, borehole | quarry |
The ford sits in the square at easting 62, northing 65, so its four-figure reference is 6265. The church stands in the middle of square 6365, which is five-tenths across and five-tenths up, so its six-figure reference is 635655: the easting 63 gains its fifth to make 635, the northing 65 gains its fifth to make 655, and the easting is written first, along the corridor before up the stairs. A learner who writes 655635 has the two halves in the wrong order and points somewhere near the marsh instead. Paper 2 sets these skills on a large-scale map extract with a printed key, or on a graph, table, photograph or diagram, and each part is usually worth one to three marks.

### Grid references: the common error

The error the registers record is northings written before eastings, or a figure lifted from a road number. The correct move is the fixed order: eastings from the top or bottom of the grid first, then northings from the side, and the third figures estimated inside the square, never borrowed from a label on the map. The test that tells them apart: along before up, and the figures come from the grid lines alone.

### Direction and bearing, worked

On the worked extract above, the farmstead sits in square 6363 and the quarry in 6463, one square to the east, so the quarry's direction from the farmstead is due east. The saw mill, in square 6464, is one square east and one square north of the farmstead, so its direction is north-east, and its bearing, measured clockwise from grid north, is 045 degrees. A learner who gives 45 degrees as a compass direction has confused the two systems: a direction is a point of the compass such as north-east, a bearing is degrees clockwise from grid north, and a question asks for one by name. Paper 2 usually gives one mark for each, and both are read from the grid, not estimated from the drawing.

### Distance, worked

The paper-edge method, worked. A track runs from the farmstead in square 6363 to the saw mill in 6464, bending twice. Lay the straight edge of a sheet of paper along the first straight stretch and mark the bend on the paper; turn the paper at the bend, mark the next; mark the end. The chain of marks now holds the whole route as one straight line. Lay it against the scale line of a 1:50 000 extract, where 1 centimetre is 0.5 kilometres, and read: the marks span 4.4 centimetres, so the track is 4.4 x 0.5 = 2.2 kilometres on the ground. A learner who rules one straight line from farmstead to mill measures 3.6 centimetres and answers 1.8 kilometres, which is the crow's flight and not the track.

### Heights, worked

Contours, worked on a line of them. A footpath leaves the 40-metre contour, crosses the 60, the 80 and the 100, and ends at a spot height of 112 metres. The height gained is 112 - 40 = 72 metres. The spacing tells the steepness: the 40 and 60 contours stand a centimetre apart, the 80 and 100 a finger's width, and where the path meets the 100-metre line the next contours crowd together, so the lower slope is gentle and the upper slope steep. Reading height off a map is this pair: a number from the contours or the spot height, and a gradient from the gaps between them.

### Cross-section, worked

A section line runs from a spot height of 90 metres across a valley to a spot height of 130 metres. Along the way it crosses the 80-metre contour, then the 60 at the stream, then 80 again on the far side. To draw it: rule a horizontal axis for distance and a vertical axis for height, plot each crossing where it occurs, and join the points. The shape that emerges is a valley, a dip to 60 metres at the stream between two shoulders of high ground, and if the vertical axis is drawn larger than the horizontal the slopes look steeper than they are, which is vertical exaggeration, and the honest section states it.

### Scale translation, worked

A reservoir measures 2.8 centimetres long on the 1:50 000 extract: 2.8 x 0.5 = 1.4 kilometres of water. Its dam is 0.3 centimetres thick, which is 0.3 x 0.5 = 0.15 kilometres, about 150 metres, so the drawing's hairline is really a wall 150 metres across. The skill runs both ways: a planner who must show a 3-kilometre stretch of road on the same extract works 3 / 0.5 = 6 centimetres and knows the strip will fill most of the sheet. Describing a feature in real terms is this multiplication said in words, a lake a kilometre and a half long, a dam 150 metres thick.

### The key, worked

A key reads before the map, worked. On the worked extract above the key would carry entries such as ford, marsh with reeds, plantation, quarry, spot height. Asked about the marsh, an answer that calls it a wet place or some water has used a general term; the key's own category is marsh, with the reeds as its symbol. Asked to identify the plantation, the answer names plantation, the key's word, and adds the symbol that proves it, the tree cluster. The habit to build is to match every named feature back to its key entry, because the key's vocabulary is the map's own.

### Inference, worked

Inference from the worked extract, run as evidence then conclusion. Evidence: the village of Okapuka, square 6365, sits on the stream at the ford square, with the main road junction one square south. Inference: the settlement chose the crossing, because a place where a road meets a river needs a ford and a ford gathers travellers, and the village grew where the two routes cross. Each step names what the map shows and what it means, and an inference that skips its evidence reads as a guess, which the resource does not support. Paper 2 asks for this in short parts, a feature, then the pattern, then the conclusion it supports.

### Landscape description, worked

Describing a landscape from the worked extract, in the terms it asks for. The south-west corner: a marsh with reeds on low ground beside the stream, flat and wet. The middle: a village nucleated on its church, a ford on the stream, a road junction serving it. The east: a quarry cut into the rising ground, a saw mill beside the plantation, and the contours climbing from 40 to 80 metres towards the steep slope. The description names each feature with a term and an adjective, marsh, nucleated, steep, and stops at what is shown, which is what a one-mark or two-mark part on Paper 2 rewards.

### Drainage and relief, worked

Drainage worked on the extract above. The stream enters at the ford, square 6265, and flows south-west through the marsh of square 6263, so the flow direction is read from the heights: the ground falls from the 40-to-80-metre steep slope down to the marsh, and water runs downhill. The drainage density is low, one main stream and a marsh on twelve squares, which suits dry country with one valley. Relief is the land, drainage is the water on it, and the two are described separately: the relief climbs from marsh level to 80 metres, the drainage gathers it into one channel.

### Relief is not drainage

The recorded error is describing contours or a road when the drainage is asked for, or naming a channel the map does not show. The correct move: relief is the height and shape of the land surface, drainage is the water on it, its streams, their pattern, density and flow, and both are read from the squares the extract gives. The test: is the answer about the land surface or the water on it, and is the feature actually printed on the extract?

### Coast and channel, worked

A river channel read on a large-scale sheet changes down its course, and the worked table shows it: in the upper squares the channel is narrow and straight between bunched contours; in the middle squares it meanders on a wider flood plain; on the lowest squares it splits into distributaries across a marsh by the sea. Each change is stated with its evidence, narrow and straight, meandering, distributaries, and the reason follows: the discharge grows as tributaries join, so the channel widens and wanders. A coastline reads the same way on the sheet, cliffs as a hard edge with contours against it, a beach as flat ground at the low-water line, and the terms come from the key.

### Land use, worked

Land use worked on the extract above. The marsh square carries reeds, unimproved wet ground. The village square carries settlement. The plantation square carries forestry, and the quarry square carries extraction. The pattern is the ground's own logic: the wet lowland is left as marsh, the dry flat ground holds the village, the steep slope is too poor for fields and wears the plantation, and the quarry sits where the rock outcrops. A Paper 2 part asks for the variation, and the answer gives the uses with their squares and the reason each sits where it does.

### Settlement patterns, worked

The three settlement patterns, worked. Nucleated: the village of Okapuka, its buildings grouped round the church and the ford. Linear: the farmstead strung along the track where it crosses square 6363. Dispersed: the borehole farmstead standing alone in square 6363's neighbours, a single holding on its own land. A sketch map of the patterns is drawn as three boxes, dots clustered, dots in a line, dots scattered, labelled, because the sketch is the fastest way to show a pattern a paragraph would labour. Each pattern is named from the map before it is explained, and the reasons follow, the ford for the village, the route for the line, the holding for the farm.

### Urban morphology, worked

Urban morphology, worked on a town sheet. From the centre outwards: the market square and chain stores in tight blocks; the terraced streets in small regular plots around them; the detached housing with gardens where the plots widen; the retail park and school on the edge by the bypass. The zones are read from the symbols, tight blocks, rows, gardens, car parks, and the order is the town's history, because the middle was built first and each ring followed as travel reached farther. A description on Paper 2 follows that order, and each zone is named with the symbols that mark it.

### Function, services and site, worked

Function and site, worked. A settlement whose symbols are a quay, warehouses and a ferry slipway has the function of a port; one whose symbols are a cathedral, a college and a hospital has the function of a service town; the same grid square can hold both a chapel and a shop, and the larger set of symbols names the dominant function. The site is read the same way: a town on a dry flat spur above a river crossing was sited for defence and the bridge, and its growth followed the routes that converged on the crossing. Function is what it does, services are what it provides, site is the ground it stands on, and the map answers all three by its symbols.

### Communications, worked

Communication networks, worked. On the extract above the main road runs north to south through the junction square, a track links the farmstead to the saw mill, and the footpath crosses the ford. The types are named from the key, sealed main road, track, footpath, and the density is counted: three routes on twelve squares, with the heavy route on the dry ground and the footpath the one route that risks the wet crossing. Relief explains the pattern, because the main road keeps to the gentle ground and the steep slope square carries none. Type, density and the relief behind them are the three things a network answer names.

## Data skills: graphs, tables and patterns

### Population pyramids

A population pyramid shows the age groups of a population as bars either side of a central axis, males left, females right, youngest at the bottom. The broad features are read by group. The width of the base shows the child share: wide, a high birth rate. The taper of the top shows survival into old age: a tall thin top, a long-lived population. The bulges and narrow waists show the cohorts history left: a waist at the young adult ages records a birth dip decades ago. Male and female bars are compared side by side, and a cohort's share is males plus females, both bars together.

### Significant features and patterns

A resource question starts with what matters: the river and its valley, the steepest slope, the main route, the settlement's site, the highest value on the table, the turning point of the graph. Significant features are the ones that explain the rest. A relationship is deduced by matching two patterns: where the settlements sit on the valley floors, and where the roads bend round the spurs; the pattern of one is laid against the pattern of the other and the link stated as a finding, not a guess.

### Extracting and describing data

Extraction means finding what the question asks for, the value, the place, the year, and giving exactly that, with its unit: the 2010 figure, the highest region, the wettest month. A description of data states the overall pattern first, then the main change and any exception, then supports each with figures read from the resource, units attached. Describing a graph year by year is not describing its trend: the overall rise, fall or stability is the answer, and the yearly values are evidence for it. A trend answer without figures is a shape without scale; figures without the overall statement are a list.

### Plotting

Plotting is accuracy: read the value along its own axis, follow the guide line to the crossing point, and mark it precisely, with a sharp pencil and a cross that does not straddle two squares. A value plotted against the wrong scale lands whole intervals from its place, and a point read on the wrong axis of a triangular graph puts a whole category in the wrong corner. On a triangular graph each axis is read along the angle of its grid lines, and the three readings for one point add up to 100 per cent, which is the check that the point was plotted right.

### Choosing a graph

The data decides the graph. Change over time: a line graph. Comparing categories, regions, months: a bar chart. Shares of a total: a divided bar or a pie chart. A relationship between two measures, one against the other: a scatter graph. A distribution over space: a choropleth or dot map. Three shares that total 100 per cent, employment structure for instance: a triangular graph. Movement between places: a flow diagram. The choice is part of the answer, and a graph that distorts the data, a pie chart for a time series, for example, loses the mark even when accurately drawn.

### Where this connects

The pyramids here are the population structure of topic 1.3, the data is the themes of every Paper 1 topic, and the arithmetic below turns raw figures into means, ranges and percentages that the graph then shows.

### Pyramids, worked

A pyramid worked from its numbers. A table gives, as percentages of the whole population: 0-14, male 18, female 17; 15-64, male 30, female 31; 65 and over, male 1, female 3. The broad groups: children total 18 + 17 = 35 per cent, the working band 30 + 31 = 61 per cent, the elderly 1 + 3 = 4 per cent, so the structure is a wide base and a thin top, a young population pointed at growth. The sexes are close in the young groups and the women outnumber the men among the elderly, 3 against 1, which longer female survival produces. Reading a pyramid is adding the two bars of each group and comparing the groups, and Paper 2 asks for exactly that comparison in two or three marks.

### Significant features, worked

Significant features, worked on a small extract: a river valley with a nucleated village at the one bridge, a main road that bends round the valley's steep side, and a reservoir above the village on the high ground. The significant features are the ones that explain the rest: the bridge, because the village sits on it; the steep side, because the road avoids it; the reservoir, because the village's water comes from it. Choosing significant features is the skill, and the choice is defended by what each feature explains. Paper 2 asks for the features of a landscape in a short part, and the answer names the few that carry the pattern rather than a list of everything the square holds.

### Patterns and relationships, worked

Deducing a relationship, worked. A choropleth map shades a region's provinces by income, darkest on the coast, lightest inland; a second map marks the provinces' ports. Laid together, the dark provinces each hold a port and the light ones hold none, so the relationship is that income follows the ports, and the mechanism is trade: a port brings work, work brings income, and the shading traces it. The answer states the two patterns, then the link, then the mechanism, in that order, and every part of it comes from the two resources. Paper 2 marks the deduction, the stated link between two named patterns, and not the guesswork a learner might substitute for it.

### Extraction, worked

Extraction, worked. A table gives the monthly visitors to a park: January 2400, February 1900, March 2800, April 3600. Asked for the busiest month, the answer is April, 3600 visitors, and nothing else. Asked for the increase from March to April, the answer is 3600 - 2800 = 800 visitors. Asked for the month with the smallest total, February at 1900. Extraction is finding the cell the question names and giving its value with its unit, and it is worth its mark exactly when the right figure and unit appear, which is why the habit is worth building early.

### Trends, worked

A trend, worked. The visitors table above, read as a trend: totals rise through the period, from 2400 in January to 3600 in April, with a dip in February to 1900 that breaks the climb. The answer states the overall direction first, the rise; sizes it with figures, 2400 to 3600; then names the exception, February, and does not hide it, because the exception is real and the reader needs to know it is there. Describing the graph month by month, 2400, then 1900, then 2800, then 3600, is not a trend answer, it is the table read aloud, and the marks go to the overall statement the figures support.

### The trend told wrong

The recorded error is describing a graph value by value instead of the overall trend, or quoting figures with no description attached. The correct move: an overall statement first, the main change and any exception next, each supported by figures read from the graph with their units. The test: is there an overall statement before the detail, and do the figures serve it rather than replace it?

### Plotting, worked

Plotting, worked on a line graph whose vertical axis runs 0 to 4000, marked every 500, with the months across the bottom. The April value, 3600, is plotted by finding the April column, following it up to the 3500 grid line, and marking the point 100 units higher, two small squares if each small square is 50. The check is to read the point back: from the mark, come down to the axis and confirm 3600, and across to April. A learner who plots 3600 on the 3500 line has dropped one small square, and the read-back catches it before the answer is written. On a triangular graph the same discipline reads each axis along the angle of its own grid lines, and the three readings must total 100 per cent, which is the built-in check.

### Choosing a graph, worked

Choosing a graph, worked on three datasets. Monthly rainfall at one station, twelve values in time order: a bar chart or a line graph, because time-ordered categories are shown by bars or a line. A country's employment in three sectors, shares of a total: a pie chart or a divided bar, because shares of a whole need parts of one figure. The distances travelled and the fares paid by forty shoppers, two measures against each other: a scatter graph, because the relationship between two measures is a cloud of points. The data decides, and the wrong graph, a pie chart for a time series, say, loses the mark even when drawn neatly, because it hides the very pattern the data holds.

### A second table, read three ways

A second worked resource, a climate table for a southern-hemisphere station.

| month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| temperature / °C | 24 | 23 | 21 | 17 | 13 | 10 | 9 | 11 | 15 | 19 | 22 | 24 |
| rainfall / mm | 120 | 110 | 80 | 40 | 15 | 5 | 4 | 6 | 20 | 55 | 90 | 130 |

Extraction: the wettest month is December at 130 millimetres, the driest July at 4, the warmest January and December at 24 degrees. Trend: temperature runs a southern-hemisphere year, warm at the year's ends and cool in the middle, from 24 degrees in January down to 9 in July and back; rainfall follows the same swing, wet summers and dry winters, 130 millimetres in December against 4 in July. Relationship: the two patterns move together, the wet months being the warm months, so the rain is a summer rain, which is the geography the table holds. Plotting: on a climate graph the temperature is a line and the rainfall is bars, and the whole year is one page.

### A dispersion graph, worked

A dispersion graph, worked. Ten farms' maize yields, in tonnes per hectare, are plotted as dots against one vertical scale: 1.2, 1.8, 2.0, 2.1, 2.3, 2.4, 2.4, 2.6, 3.4 and 4.1. The graph shows the spread itself: the dots cluster between 2.0 and 2.6, so the typical yield sits there, one dot low at 1.2 and one high at 4.1, and those two outliers are farms with something different, irrigation perhaps, or poor soil. A dispersion graph is chosen when the individual values matter, because every dot stays visible, whereas a mean of 2.43 tonnes per hectare would hide the two unusual farms entirely. The range, 4.1 - 1.2 = 2.9 tonnes per hectare, is read straight off the graph, and the cluster shows where the ordinary farm sits.

How the paper sets the data skills. ## Photographs, images and combined resources

### Describing a photograph

A photograph is described from what it shows, organised from the foreground through the middle distance to the background, with the position words doing the organising. In the foreground, a flat field system with rectangular plots; in the middle distance, a steep slope under grass with a track climbing it; in the background, a flat-topped ridge against the sky. The description names landforms, vegetation, land use and buildings, with suitable geographical terms and simple adjectives. It adds nothing the photograph does not show, and it explains nothing either: features that cannot be pointed to in the image are invented, and reasons are explanation, which a describe question has not asked for.

### Adding detail to a map

Applying knowledge to a map means adding what the symbols imply. A weir on the river means a head of water, and a mill beside it means the head was used. Contours bending upstream in a V mark the valley the river has cut, so the flow direction is read off the Vs. Land use follows the relief, so the flat ground by the stream is the settlement's best land, and the inference is tied to the evidence on the sheet: each added detail names the symbol it comes from.

### Using material together

A map and a photograph read together let one source check the other: the photograph shows what the contour pattern means on the ground, and the map places what the photograph shows. A valley that looks broad in the image is measured on the map; a slope that looks steep on the sheet is checked against the photograph's horizon. Aerial photographs and satellite images extend the same skill, and a GIS layer, a data theme drawn over the base map, joins a distribution to the ground it lies on.

### Where this connects

The photographs described here show the landforms of topics 2.2 and 2.3, and the map skills they sit with are the ones of the first two units of this topic.

### A photograph, described

A described photograph, worked from front to back. In the foreground: a dry stony river bed, a channel of rounded boulders and sand with no water in it. In the middle distance: flat open grazing land, tan and dusty, with a single dirt road running to the horizon and one windmill beside it. In the background: a line of low rocky hills, bare of vegetation on their upper slopes. The description uses the position words to organise it and names what is there, landforms, vegetation, land use and buildings, and it adds nothing: no farmer, no explanation, no crop the eye cannot see. The test is whether each thing named can be pointed to in the image.

### The invented photograph

The recorded error is adding features the photograph does not show, or explaining when a description is asked for. The correct move: describe only the visible, landforms, vegetation, land use, buildings, with position words doing the organising, and leave reasons for a question that asks for them. The test: can the feature be pointed to in the photograph, and does the question ask what or why?

### Two sources, one answer, worked

Supporting material beside a map, worked. A map extract shows a village on a river at a bridge; an accompanying photograph, taken from the bridge, shows the flood plain upstream under water after a storm. Read together: the map shows the village's site on the flat ground beside the river, and the photograph shows that flat ground flooded, so the joint conclusion is that the village stands on a flood plain that the river occupies after storms. Neither source carries the conclusion alone, the map has the site but not the hazard, the photograph the water but not the position, and using them together is the skill the syllabus names. Aerial photographs, satellite images and GIS layers extend the same pairing, one source checked against the other.

### Annotating a photograph, worked

Annotating, worked. Take the dry-riverbed photograph described earlier and add the geography the image implies, each label naming what it points at. The channel: a dry bed of rounded boulders, so the river flows after rain and not before it, an ephemeral channel. The boulders' rounding: worn in the wet season's flow, because angular rock rounds as it travels. The windmill in the middle distance: pumping groundwater for stock, so the grazing is ranched land, not wilderness. The bare upper slopes of the hills: too dry for cover, so the vegetation stops where the water does. Each annotation is a label the image supports, and the labels together are a field description written on the photograph itself, which is the technique's whole point.

### A map and photograph of one coast

The coastal extract and a photograph of the same shore, worked together. The map shows the cliff square, the bay with its sand beach, the headland with the beacon, and a photograph taken from the headland shows the cliff line falling to the bay in the middle distance and the hotel's promenade beyond. The joint reading places what the image shows, the cliff is the square 2174 edge, and confirms what the map draws, the beach is the flat strip in the bay, so a description of one can be checked against the other. Paper 2 pairs resources this way, and the skill is to use each for what it does best: the map for position and pattern, the photograph for texture and detail the map cannot hold.

## The arithmetic of geography

### The four operations

Data work is the four operations used as the data requires. Totals by addition: the district's population is its age groups added. Change by subtraction: the growth of the town is this census minus the last. Rates by division: the density is the people divided by the area. Scaling by multiplication: the real distance is the map measurement times the scale. The answer is rounded to suit the figures, because a mean of individual measurements reported to five figures claims a precision the data does not have.

### Scale and representative fractions

A representative fraction such as 1:50 000 means 1 unit on the map is 50 000 of the same units on the ground: 1 centimetre on the map is 50 000 centimetres, which is 500 metres, on the ground. The conversion is multiplication by the denominator and a change to sensible units, and the scale line printed on the map does the same job graphically, which is what the paper edge method uses. On a 1:25 000 sheet, 1 centimetre is 250 metres on the ground.

### Mean and range

The mean is the total of the values divided by how many there are. Five daily rainfalls of 0, 2, 10, 6 and 2 millimetres total 0 + 2 + 10 + 6 + 2 = 20 millimetres, so the mean is 20 / 5 = 4 millimetres. The range is written from the lowest value to the highest, 0 to 10 millimetres, or their difference, 10 - 0 = 10 millimetres, when a single figure is asked for. A range keeps both its ends, its sign and its decimal places: one value alone is not a range, and a minus sign or a decimal dropped loses the precision the question asked to keep.

### Percentages, ratios and decimals

A percentage is the part divided by the whole, multiplied by 100. A town of 40 000 people with 9 000 under 15 has 9 000 / 40 000 x 100 = 22.5 per cent children. A ratio compares parts of a total: 9 000 children and 31 000 others stand in the ratio 9:31. A decimal or fraction converts to a percentage the same way, multiply by 100, so 0.225 is 22.5 per cent. The three forms answer the same question in different clothes, and the data question names which it wants.

### Standard notation and significant figures

Standard notation writes large and small numbers as a figure between 1 and 10 times a power of ten: 8 000 000 000 people is 8 x 10 to the power 9. A negative index marks a fraction: 0.001 is 1 x 10 to the power minus 3. Significant figures count from the first non-zero digit, so 30 400 is 3 to two significant figures but 30 000 to three, and the answer carries the precision the data supports: rainfall gauge readings to the nearest millimetre are not reported to three significant figures as if they were exact.

### Scatter relationships

A scatter graph shows the relationship between two measures, one on each axis. A positive relationship: the points rise together, more of one goes with more of the other, distance from the CBD against house price. A negative relationship: one falls as the other rises, distance from the CBD against land value in most cities. No relationship: the points scatter without a slope, a cloud with no direction. Reading the slope is reading the sign of the relationship, and the points, not the line of best fit alone, are the evidence.

### Drawing graphs

Graphs are drawn to their type. Bars for categories, equal widths, gaps between. Lines for time series, points joined. Divided bars or pie charts for shares of a total. Scatter for relationships. Each point is plotted accurately and each plotted value checked against its scale, because one misread axis scrambles the whole series. The axes are labelled with what they show and the units they show it in.

### Choosing scales and axes

A good graph uses most of the axis length, starts each axis at a value that lets the data fill the grid, and uses simple intervals, 0, 10, 20, 30, that a reader can count in. An axis squashed into the bottom third of the grid wastes the paper and flattens the trend; an axis starting at 0 when the data runs 490 to 510 crams every point into one square. The scale serves the data, and the choice of it is marked as part of the drawing.

### Ruler and protractor

A ruler measures to the nearest millimetre along the line being measured, the zero of the scale set on one end of the line, the reading taken square-on, not at an angle. A protractor's centre sits exactly on the point of the angle, its zero line along one arm, and the reading is taken on the scale that runs clockwise from that zero, the outer scale from the lower arm, the inner from the upper. Bearings use the same discipline: centre on the starting point, zero line along grid north, degrees read clockwise.

### Where this connects

These are the number skills the Paper 4 enquiries of topic 5 need: means and ranges of field data, percentages of survey results, and graphs drawn and scaled for the results tables there.

### The four operations, worked

The four operations, worked on a farm budget. A farm holds 240 cattle and sells 85 in the year: 240 - 85 = 155 remain. It buys 30 breeding cows: 155 + 30 = 185. Each cow drinks about 45 litres a day, so the herd needs 185 x 45 = 8325 litres a day. Watered by tanker loads of 5000 litres, that is 8325 / 5000 = 1.67 loads, so two loads a day in practice. Addition totals, subtraction finds change, multiplication scales, division shares out, and the worked chain shows all four in one farm's morning. Paper 2 sets one-mark parts on exactly these moves, and the unit follows the question.

### Representative fractions, worked

A representative fraction, worked. A map carries the fraction 1:50 000, which says 1 unit on the map is 50 000 of the same units on the ground: 1 centimetre stands for 50 000 centimetres, which is 500 metres, which is 0.5 kilometres. A ridge 6.2 centimetres long on the sheet is 6.2 x 0.5 = 3.1 kilometres of high ground. A planner who must fit a 4-kilometre canal onto the same sheet needs 4 / 0.5 = 8 centimetres of paper and knows it fits. The fraction converts both ways, ground to paper and paper to ground, and the scale line printed on the sheet does the same job for a ruler laid along it.

### Mean and range, worked

Mean and range, worked on five daily rainfalls: 0, 4, 12, 6 and 8 millimetres. The total is 0 + 4 + 12 + 6 + 8 = 30 millimetres, so the mean is 30 / 5 = 6 millimetres a day. The range is written from the lowest to the highest, 0 to 12 millimetres, or given as a single figure, 12 - 0 = 12 millimetres. The range keeps both its ends and its unit: an answer of 12 without the millimetres, or 6 alone offered as the range, loses the mark, because the range is the spread and not a value in it. Paper 2 asks for one or the other by name, and the words mean and range are not interchangeable.

### The range dropped or trimmed

The recorded error is giving one value as the range, or dropping a minus sign or a decimal place the question asks to keep. The correct move: the range is written from the lowest value to the highest, or their difference when a single figure is asked for, and the precision and sign stay as the data gives them. The test: are both ends of the range there, and is the answer exactly as precise as the question demands?

### Percentages and ratios, worked

Percentages and ratios, worked on a town of 30 000 people whose workforce of 12 000 splits: 5400 in services, 4800 in industry, 1800 in farming. The service share is 5400 / 12000 x 100 = 45 per cent, the industry share 4800 / 12000 x 100 = 40 per cent, the farm share 1800 / 12000 x 100 = 15 per cent, and the three total 100 because they are shares of one workforce. As a ratio, services to industry to farming stands at 54:48:18, which simplifies by dividing through by 6 to 9:8:3. The percentage and the ratio are two dressings of one fact, and the question names which it wants, so the answer gives that form and shows the working that produced it.

### Standard notation, worked

Standard notation, worked. A country's population of 2 500 000 is written as 2.5 x 10 to the power 6, because the figures from the first non-zero digit are 2, 5, and the decimal point has moved six places. A river's dissolved load of 0.003 grams per litre is 3 x 10 to the power minus 3, a negative index marking a value below one, with the point moved three places the other way. The form is a figure between 1 and 10 times a power of ten, and the power counts the moves. Comparisons become easy once the form is standard: 2.5 x 10 to the power 6 against 9.8 x 10 to the power 5 is a country a little over two and a half times the other, read off the powers at a glance.

### Significant figures, worked

Significant figures, worked. A census count of 47 128 people, given to two significant figures, is 47 000, because the third figure, 1, is below 5 and the 7 stands; given to one, it is 50 000, because the second figure, 7, lifts the 4 to a 5. A mean rainfall of 23.48 millimetres, itself the average of readings taken to the nearest millimetre, is honestly given as 23.5 to one decimal place, and 23.48 claims a precision the gauge never had. The rule counts from the first non-zero digit and rounds by the figure after the cut, and the honest answer carries no more figures than the data supports.

### Scatter relationships, worked

A scatter graph, worked. Shop floor area is plotted against daily takings for ten shops, and the points run from the lower left to the upper right in a loose band: the bigger the floor, the bigger the takings, a positive relationship, because both rise together. A second graph plots the same shops' distance from the bus station against their takings, and the band runs the other way, upper left to lower right: the farther the shop, the smaller the takings, a negative relationship. The sign of the slope is the finding, and one point that sits far off the band is an outlier, worth naming and worth explaining if the resource allows, because an outlier is a shop with something else going on. Paper 2 gives a mark for naming the relationship and a mark for reading a value off the best-fit line.

### Drawing a graph, worked

Drawing a graph, worked through one dataset: four towns' annual visitors, 9000, 6400, 4300 and 7200. The type: categories compared, so a bar chart, one bar per town, equal widths, gaps between. The axes: towns named across the bottom, a vertical axis scaled 0 to 10 000 marked every 1000, which lets the four bars fill the grid. The drawing: each bar's height read against the scale, 9000 nine units up, and drawn with a ruler. The labels: what each axis shows and the units, visitors and towns. A bar drawn freehand, an axis with no units or a bar of the wrong width each lose the drawing mark, because a graph is a measurement displayed, and the display has rules.

### Scales and axes, worked

Scales and axes, worked. Data run from 420 to 490 millimetres of annual rainfall, and a learner scales the axis from zero: the whole dataset crowds into the top fifth of the grid, and the differences the study exists for vanish. The better axis starts at 400 and runs to 500, marked every 20, so the values spread across the paper and the variation shows. The axis that does not start at zero says so on its labels, because a reader who assumes zero is misled by the steepened slopes. The second rule is simple intervals: 0, 20, 40, 60, a reader can count in, not 0, 17, 34, 51, which nobody can. The scale serves the data, and the grid serves the scale.

### Ruler and protractor, worked

Ruler and protractor, worked. A distance on a map measures 7.4 centimetres: the ruler's zero is set on one end, not its worn edge, the eye squares over the 7.4 mark, and the reading is taken square and not at a slant. A bearing is measured with the protractor's centre dot exactly on the start point, its zero line along grid north, and the degrees read on the clockwise scale from that zero, which is the outer scale when north is up. A pie slice of 25 per cent is drawn as 25 / 100 x 360 = 90 degrees, a right angle, measured from the slice's starting radius. The instruments are simple, and their marks go to the learner who uses them exactly, centre on the point, zero on the line, eye square over the reading.

### The arithmetic of a field study, worked

The arithmetic a field study needs, worked on one river dataset. Five width readings, in metres: 2.4, 2.8, 3.1, 2.9, 3.3. The total is 2.4 + 2.8 + 3.1 + 2.9 + 3.3 = 14.5 metres, so the mean width is 14.5 / 5 = 2.9 metres. The range runs from 2.4 to 3.3 metres, a spread of 3.3 - 2.4 = 0.9 metres. A cross-section's area, roughly the mean width times the mean depth of 0.6 metres, is 2.9 x 0.6 = 1.74 square metres, and a float timed over 10 metres in 25 seconds gives a speed of 10 / 25 = 0.4 metres per second, so the discharge, the area times the speed, is 1.74 x 0.4 = 0.696, about 0.7 cubic metres per second. One dataset, and the whole of this unit's arithmetic appears in it, means, ranges, multiplications and divisions, each carrying its unit.

### A percentage chain, worked

A percentage chain, worked on a settlement's data. A village of 2400 people holds 1560 people of working age. The working share is 1560 / 2400 x 100 = 65 per cent, so the dependent share is 100 - 65 = 35 per cent, or 2400 - 1560 = 840 people. Of those 840, a survey finds 600 under 15 and 240 over 65, so the young dependants are 600 / 840 x 100 = 71 per cent of the dependent group, rounded from 71.4. As a ratio of young to elderly dependants, 600:240 simplifies by dividing through by 120 to 5:2. Every step is the same move, the part over the whole times 100, applied to whatever whole the question names, the village, the dependants, and the discipline is naming the whole before dividing by it, because the commonest slip is the right share of the wrong whole.

### Graphs drawn and read, worked

Graphs drawn and read, one last worked set. A divided bar: a country's energy from coal 45 per cent, hydro 35, solar 20, drawn on a 10-centimetre bar as 45 / 100 x 10 = 4.5 centimetres of coal, 3.5 of hydro and 2 of solar, each segment shaded to a stated key. A line graph: the river study's five width readings against distance downstream, 2.4, 2.8, 3.1, 2.9, 3.3 at sites one to five, plotted and joined, and the line shows the channel widening downstream with a dip at site four. Reading it back: the trend rises overall, from 2.4 metres to 3.3, with the site-four dip as the exception worth explaining, a boulder field slowing the flow perhaps. Drawing and reading are the two halves of one skill, and the reader reads what the drawer drew, which is why the drawing rules exist.

How the paper sets the arithmetic.
