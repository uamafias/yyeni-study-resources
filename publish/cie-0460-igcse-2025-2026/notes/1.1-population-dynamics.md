## Population dynamics: growth, resources and policy

### How the world's population grew

For most of human history the world's population changed slowly, held down by harvests that failed often and diseases that killed children before they could grow up. It reached about 1 billion around 1800, after which growth accelerated steeply, to about 2 billion by the late 1920s, 3 billion by 1960 and 6 billion by 1999. In 2022 the United Nations estimated the world's population had passed 8 billion, and it is rising by around 70 million people a year, which is close to adding another Namibia every month. The reason is not that people suddenly had more babies everywhere. From around 1750, and much faster in the twentieth century, cleaner water, better sanitation, vaccination and more reliable food cut the death rate in country after country. Birth rates fell too, but they fell later and more slowly, so for decades each country's births far outnumbered its deaths. That gap, repeated across the world, is the rapid increase. It shows up in a country's profile too: at an early stage of development, deaths and births are both high and the population is stable; when health care and food arrive, deaths fall first while births stay high, and the population grows fast; last, births fall, and growth slows again.

### A falling population in a rich country

When a wealthier country's population falls, the cause is demographic: a birth rate below the death rate, helped along by an ageing population, because the large cohorts born earlier are now old and die in greater numbers while few babies are born to replace them. Some learners reach for the causes of high death rates, famine, poor sanitation or disease, and describe those instead. That is the wrong angle: those are the reasons a low-income country's population grows slowly, not the reasons a rich country shrinks. A wealthy country has clean water, full clinics and reliable food, so its deaths are few and its problem is that its births are fewer still. The test is which countries the resource shows and what drives change there.

### Over-populated, under-populated

Over-population is too many people for the resources and technology of an area to support a good standard of living. Under-population is too few people to use an area's resources fully. Neither is a count of people, so neither follows from a large or small population alone. An area is over-populated when the land cannot feed everyone and housing, schools and hospitals become overloaded; it is under-populated when there are too few people to work the land, staff the services or make markets worthwhile, so good land or minerals sit unused. And the same resources support different numbers of people under different technology and government: a desert with pipelines and greenhouses feeds more people than the same desert without them, so the judgement always compares people with resources and with the means of using them. A change in technology, investment or government can move an area from one state to the other without a single person moving in or out.

### "A lot of people" is not over-population

Treating over-population as simply "a lot of people" is a common error, and it costs marks because it answers a different question. The Netherlands is crowded but not over-populated, because its technology, ports and trade support a high standard of living for a dense population; a drought-struck district can be over-populated with few people, because its land cannot feed even them. The test is whether the answer is about people compared with resources: numbers on their own settle nothing, and an answer that only reports a count has not made the judgement the term asks for.

### What makes a population change

A population changes through natural causes and through migration. Natural change is the balance of births and deaths. Migration is people moving in (immigration) and people moving out (emigration); net migration, immigrants minus emigrants, is the migration balance. Birth rate is live births per 1000 people per year; death rate is deaths per 1000 people per year; natural increase is the birth rate minus the death rate, a difference in the same per-1000 unit. Add net migration to natural change and you have the whole change in a population. Birth rates tend to be high where many children die young, where children work on the family holding, and where few women are in paid work or school; they tend to be low where family planning is available and educating children is expensive. Death rates fall where clean water, sanitation, health care and reliable food reach people, and rise during famine, epidemic disease and war.

### Natural increase, worked

A country has a birth rate of 32 per 1000 and a death rate of 9 per 1000. Its natural increase is 32 - 9 = 23 per 1000 per year, which is the same as 2.3 per cent a year. A second country has a birth rate of 8 per 1000 and a death rate of 12 per 1000: 8 - 12 = -4 per 1000, a natural decrease, so its population shrinks from natural causes alone. The arithmetic is a subtraction, in per-1000 units: dividing or multiplying the two rates gives a number that is not the natural increase, because the term is defined as their difference. To compare it with the country's migration, add net migration to it: if the second country takes in 6 more people per 1000 than leave, its total change is -4 + 6 = 2 per 1000, so it still grows even though its natural change is negative.

### Why the rates differ between countries

The rate of natural population change differs between countries because the falls in birth and death rates arrive at different times. In many low-income countries, vaccination and clean water have cut the death rate while the birth rate has stayed high, so natural increase is large and the population is young. In higher-income countries both rates are low: family planning is normal, children stay in education for years, and the population is older, so deaths roughly match or exceed births and natural change is small or negative. The contrast is not permanent, because the birth rate falls in time: Bangladesh in the 1980s grew the way Niger grows now, and Japan a century ago grew the way Bangladesh grows now. Natural increase is births and deaths only; net migration is a separate cause of change, and the two are added to give the total.

### Population policies

A population policy is a government's deliberate attempt to move the birth rate. An anti-natalist policy pushes it down: family planning services, education for women, taxes or penalties for large families, and in the strictest cases legal limits on the number of children. A pro-natalist policy pushes it up: paid parental leave, child payments, and better housing for families. Governments choose the direction according to their problem: a fast-growing, young population pushes a government towards anti-natalist measures, and a shrinking, ageing one towards pro-natalist payments. Describing a policy means naming its measures, clinics, payments, laws, and separately naming its impacts, such as a lower birth rate or a smaller school-age cohort. A policy answer that lists measures when asked for impacts, or impacts when asked how the policy works, has answered the wrong question, so read the command word first.

### China's one-child policy

China's one-child policy, in force from 1979 to 2015, is the best-known anti-natalist policy. Its measures were fines for a second child, permits for marriage and birth, and pressure through the workplace. Its impacts were large: the birth rate fell sharply, from around 33 per 1000 in 1970 to 18 per 1000 by 1980, and China today faces an ageing population, a shrinking workforce and, after decades of preference for a son, millions more men than women by the 2010s. Evaluating the policy means weighing the fall in births, which happened, against the later costs, a large older cohort with fewer working-age people to support it. A policy that works quickly can create problems later, and the judgement has to say which side outweighs the other, not just list both.

### Where this connects

The people who move when population pressure builds are the subject of migration, topic 1.2; the age groups a growing or shrinking population leaves behind are the subject of population structure, topic 1.3, where the dependency ratio measures the load on the working-age people; and the spread of people across the ground is population density and distribution, topic 1.4. On Paper 1, the case studies in the next section carry the 7-mark question on this topic, and the definitions practised here, birth rate, death rate, natural increase, net migration, are the same ones the Paper 2 data questions read from tables.

## Case studies: four countries, four populations

### Over-populated: Bangladesh

Bangladesh, a low-income country on the Ganges delta, had about 170 million people in 2022, most of them packed onto the farmland of that delta where three great rivers meet the sea. Its birth rate was around 18 per 1000 and its death rate around 9 per 1000 in the early 2020s, so the population still grows by roughly 1 per cent a year, and the United Nations expects it to add tens of millions more before it levels off. The pressure shows first in the land: farms average well under a hectare, and each inheritance divides fields too small to feed a family further, so more of the rural poor are pushed onto marginal ground that floods from the rivers or the sea. It shows in services and work as well: city schools run several shifts a day, rural clinics serve far more people than they were built for, and job seekers outnumber formal jobs, keeping pay low. The country has pushed back with family planning: health workers take contraception out to the villages, girls' schooling has risen, and the birth rate has come down from about 34 per 1000 in the 1980s to around 18 by the early 2020s, one of the faster falls in South Asia. Bangladesh shows that over-population is about people against resources, not people alone: the delta is fertile, but the numbers outrun it.

### Under-populated: Australia

Australia is a continent of 7.7 million square kilometres with about 26 million people (2022), an average of around 3 per square kilometre, against roughly 1125 per square kilometre in Bangladesh. Its resources are enormous: iron ore in the Pilbara, coal in Queensland, gas off the north-west coast and vast grazing land across the interior. Yet the interior is desert, so water, not space, decides where people can live, and most of the population clusters in a few coastal cities such as Sydney, Melbourne and Perth. The result is under-population relative to the resources: inland farms, mines and services cannot find workers, so shifts go unfilled and land is left undeveloped, and markets are too thin to support services that a town of ordinary size would keep. Australia deliberately recruits migrants to close the gap: a skilled migration scheme selects the workers the interior and professional services need, so immigration is designed to bring the labour the resources cannot be worked without, and migration, not births, supplies most of the country's population growth. Australia shows the other half of the definition: too few people to use what the land offers, even though the land offers a great deal, and the lesson cuts both ways, because the same country can hold crowded cities and an under-populated interior at once, so a single average density, 3 per square kilometre, hides both conditions at the same time.

### High natural growth: Niger

Niger, in the Sahel south of the Sahara, had around 26 million people in 2022 and one of the world's highest rates of natural increase: a birth rate around 45 per 1000 against a death rate around 9 per 1000 in the early 2020s, growth close to 3.7 per cent a year. At that pace the population doubles in about two decades, which means a country of about 26 million must feed, school and employ around 52 million by the mid-2040s on the same dry farmland along the Niger river and its tributaries. The birth rate stays high because children support their parents on family farms, girls marry young and average only a few years of school, and contraception reaches a small minority of women. The consequences show in age and land: nearly half the population is under 15, so schools and clinics must serve an enormous child cohort with too few teachers and nurses, and harvests fail often in the dry years, as they did for millions of people in 2021-22, when poor rains left households short of food and herders moved south with their cattle in search of grazing. Niger is the high-growth case against Japan in the next section: the two make the contrast the syllabus asks for wherever it names a high-growth and a low-growth country together.

### Low growth and decline: Japan

Japan, an island nation of cities squeezed between mountains and the sea, saw its population peak around 125 million in 2008-10 and fall every year since, to about 124 million by 2023. Its birth rate is near 6-7 per 1000 against a death rate near 12-13 per 1000, so deaths outnumber births year after year and the population shrinks. Births are few because marrying late and having one child, or none, fits the cost of city life, housing and education; few couples have a third child, and a generation raised in that pattern is now the one having, or not having, the next. The results show in ageing: about 29-30 per cent of the population is 65 or over (2023), a shrinking workforce supports the older cohort through national insurance and pensions, and rural services close, as village schools and little-used train lines have done across the mountain valleys, so whole prefectures now hold fewer children than care homes. The government pays pro-natalist benefits and parental leave and allows some immigration, though the intake is small, so policy has slowed rather than reversed the fall, and the population is projected to keep shrinking for decades, below 120 million in the 2040s on national estimates.

### Where this connects

These four countries answer any 7-mark question that asks for a named country: an over-populated one (Bangladesh), an under-populated one (Australia), a high-growth one (Niger) and a declining one (Japan). Niger and Japan are a contrasting pair, as the syllabus asks for where it names high and low growth together: one has nearly half its people under 15, the other nearly a third over 65, and the two patterns ask opposite things of a government, schools and clinics in one case, pensions and care homes in the other. The measures Bangladesh used, family planning taken to villages and schooling for girls, connect back to the population policies in the first half of this topic, and the age groups Niger and Japan carry lead into population structure, topic 1.3. When a 7-mark question names the kind of country, match it to the right case: Bangladesh for over-populated, Australia for under-populated, Niger for high natural growth, Japan for low growth or decline.
