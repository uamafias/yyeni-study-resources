## Agricultural systems and food shortages

### An agricultural system has three parts

Every farm is a system with three parts. Inputs are what goes into the farm: physical inputs such as sunshine, rain and soil; human inputs of labour, skill and money; and bought inputs such as seed, fertiliser, tools and fuel. Processes are what the farmer does on the farm: clearing, ploughing, sowing, weeding, irrigating, harvesting, feeding, milking. Outputs are what comes out: products for the family or the market, and waste such as straw, dung and run-off. The test for any item is whether it goes in or comes out: a bag of fertiliser is an input, and the maize it helps grow is an output.

### Four pairs of farming terms

Farms are described with four independent pairs of terms. Subsistence farming feeds the farmer's family, with little or nothing sold; commercial farming sells its produce for profit. Arable farming grows crops; pastoral farming keeps animals. Intensive farming uses high inputs of labour or capital per hectare, giving high yields from a small area; extensive farming uses low inputs per hectare over a large area. Because the pairs are independent, a farm can be any combination at once: a family's maize garden is subsistence, arable and intensive; a Karakul sheep run in the Namib is commercial, pastoral and extensive.

### A worked system: a northern maize garden

A maize garden beside a homestead in the north of Namibia shows the system in the round. Inputs: rain in the wet months, household labour, seed kept from last year's harvest, a hand hoe, and cattle manure. Processes: the ground is cleared and hoed, seed is sown at the first rains, the plot is weeded, and birds are scared from the ripening cobs. Outputs: cobs eaten fresh, meal ground for the family, stovers fed to cattle, and waste to the compost. The classification follows by itself: subsistence (food for the family), arable (a crop is grown), intensive (much labour on a small plot).

### The causes of food shortages

Food shortages have natural causes and human causes, and a full answer names both kinds. Natural: drought that withers crops and pasture; floods that drown fields and wash away soil; tropical storms that flatten a season's work; and pests and diseases - locust swarms and army worm can strip a district's standing crops in days. Human: war and displacement, which keep fields un-planted; poor transport, which strands food in surplus regions while deficit regions go hungry; and low investment in irrigation, storage and farming skills, which keeps yields small. The two kinds also feed one another: land degraded by overuse fails faster when the rains fail.

### From shortage to suffering: the chain of effects

The effects of a shortage follow a chain. A harvest fails or food cannot reach people, so the food available becomes scarcer and prices rise. Families can afford less, so they eat less; undernutrition rises, and children suffer first - growth falters and resistance to disease drops, so illness and death rates rise. Families then sell assets or leave their land to find work or food, and the loss of assets deepens poverty after the crisis ends. Each link is a consequence of the one before it, which is why effects are named after the shortage and are not causes of it.

### Solutions: supply and access together

Solutions work on both sides of the balance. On the supply side: irrigation cuts dependence on rain; better seed and fertiliser raise yields per hectare; pest control protects the standing crop; and roads and storage cut post-harvest losses. On the access side: grain reserves and food aid bridge a bad season; family planning slows population growth where it outstrips the land; and new trades give families money to buy food grown elsewhere. Judging a solution means asking how well it fits the place: irrigation raises yields, but it costs money that small farmers may not have; food aid saves lives, but as cheap grain it can undercut the local growers the region needs next season.

### Natural or human; cause or effect

Two sorting errors appear again and again. The first is giving human causes when natural causes are asked for - answering "war" to a question about drought - or the reverse. The second is giving causes when effects are asked: rising prices and undernutrition are what the shortage causes, not what causes it. The fix is to ask of every point: natural or human, and cause or effect? Drought is a natural cause; war is a human cause; hungry families are an effect.

## Case studies: the Hardap Irrigation Scheme, and food shortage in the Sahel

### Case study: the Hardap Irrigation Scheme

The Hardap Irrigation Scheme lies on the Fish River just south of Mariental in southern Namibia. The Hardap Dam, completed in 1963, holds the river's summer floods, and roughly 1 100 hectares of irrigated land below the dam (approximate figure) grow lucerne (alfalfa) for fodder, along with maize, wheat, vegetables and grapes. The scheme is a commercial agricultural system: its inputs include the dam's water delivered by canal and pump, fertiliser, heavy machinery and a resident workforce; its processes are land preparation, sowing, irrigation through the dry months, weed and pest control, and harvest; and its outputs are fodder, grain, vegetables and grapes sold to dairy and beef farmers and to produce markets, with run-off and crop residues as its waste streams.

### Why Hardap is classified as it is

Each classification earns its place. Commercial: nearly everything the scheme grows is sold for profit, which distinguishes it at a stroke from a family food plot. Intensive: the scheme concentrates high inputs on each hectare - pumped water, fertiliser, machinery and full-time labour - because the area's rainfall, under about 200 millimetres a year, is far too low for dry-land cropping; those inputs buy high yields per hectare, which is the definition of intensive farming. Arable in its crops, and pastoral indirectly, since its lucerne feeds livestock elsewhere. The classification is the analysis: a farm in a dry land must be intensive precisely where water is engineered to be plentiful.

### Case study: food shortages in the Sahel

The Sahel is the dry belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, across countries such as Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso and Chad. Its people farm and herd on thin soils with rain that is low and unreliable - under about 400 millimetres a year in places - and when the rains fail, the region starves: the drought of 1984 brought failed harvests across the belt, and the crisis of 2010-2012, following another drought, left over 18 million people at risk of hunger (approximate figure, 2012). Between the droughts, human pressures build: rapid population growth strains the land, overgrazing and tree clearance degrade it, and conflict - as in Mali in 2012 - drives farmers from their fields.

### Effects and solutions, traced

The shortage's effects run in the chain this topic teaches: harvests fail, grain prices rise, families eat less, and undernutrition follows - measured across the Sahel in the tens of millions of people in bad years; herders move south with their cattle as pastures fail, which strains the regions they enter; and families sell assets to buy food, which deepens poverty after the crisis. The solutions tried show both sides of the balance: grain banks and reserves run by cooperatives bridge bad seasons; tree-planting and terracing hold soil and water on the land; improved seed and small-scale irrigation raise yields; and food aid flows in the worst years - saving lives, but as cheap grain it can undercut the local growers the region needs next season. Judged against scale, cost and reliability, the Sahel's solutions work best together: the grain bank bridges the crisis while the land work slowly rebuilds the base.
