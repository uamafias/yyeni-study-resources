## Development: indicators, inequalities, sectors and globalisation

### Measuring development with indicators

Development means the improvement in people's quality of life, and no single number captures it, so a variety of indicators is read together. GNI per head - a country's total income in a year divided by its population - measures the economic side. Life expectancy (the average number of years a newborn can expect to live), the birth rate, infant mortality (the number of children per 1000 who die before their first birthday), the literacy rate, access to safe water and the number of doctors per 1000 people measure the social side. The indicators usually move together: a country with high GNI per head typically also has long life expectancy and low infant mortality. Reading several together is what makes the judgement fair, because any one indicator can mislead on its own - a country may earn much from minerals while few of its people can read.

### Two countries, read together

Norway and Niger show how wide the gaps can be (all figures approximate, around 2022). Life expectancy at birth is about 83 years in Norway but about 62 years in Niger. Infant mortality is about 2 deaths per 1000 live births in Norway against about 60 per 1000 in Niger. Norway's GNI per head is many times Niger's, and its literacy rate is close to full, while in Niger roughly one adult in three can read and write. Notice how the indicators agree: whichever one is read, the same ranking appears, and that agreement is what allows a confident statement about which country is more developed. Where indicators disagree - a high GNI per head beside a low literacy rate - the disagreement is itself telling: it suggests the wealth is concentrated in few hands or comes from one export industry.

### Words and figures together

A common error in describing development from a resource is quoting statistics alone - a list of figures with no statement of the pattern - or giving words alone with no figures to back them. The correct answer does both: it states the pattern in words and supports each statement with a figure from the resource, with its unit. The test for an answer is whether every statement has a figure that backs it, and whether every figure has been turned into a statement. In fact the two halves are one skill seen from both ends: the figure proves the statement, and the statement gives the figure its meaning.

### Inequality between countries

The world's countries are far from equal, and the differences show on every indicator: north-west Europe, North America and parts of east Asia sit near the top of most tables, while much of sub-Saharan Africa and parts of south Asia sit near the bottom. The reasons are tangled together. Physical barriers such as deserts, mountains and a landlocked position raise the cost of trade, which deters industry. A colonial history stripped many regions of raw materials and left single-crop economies that earn little when world prices swing. Diseases such as malaria burden hot, wet regions and keep workers and investors away. Political instability and war deter the investment that would create jobs, and debt repayments can swallow money that would otherwise fund schools and health care. Few of these causes work alone: each tends to feed the others, which is why an answer that names several linked causes scores better than one that names a single cause.

### Inequality inside one country: Namibia

Inequality also exists inside single countries, and Namibia shows it clearly. Jobs and services concentrate in the capital, Windhoek, and in the coastal towns of the Erongo region around Swakopmund and Walvis Bay, where the port, mining and tourism create work. The north-eastern regions - Kavango East, Kavango West and Zambezi - record the country's highest poverty rates, and rural areas in general hold fewer clinics, schools and paved roads than the towns (2015/16 survey pattern). Income distribution as a whole is among the most unequal in the world, with a Gini coefficient around 0.56 (2015/16 figure), meaning the richest households take home many times the share of the poorest. The lesson is that the same indicator tables drawn between countries can be drawn inside one country, and often show gaps just as wide.

### Trend before figures

Asked to describe a trend, some learners compare single years - 1995 was lower than 2005 - without ever stating what the whole series is doing. The correct answer states the overall trend first: rising, falling, or a widening or narrowing gap. Supporting figures and any exception follow the statement, not replace it. The test is whether the answer says, in one sentence, what the whole table shows across the whole period: that sentence is the trend, and the figures are its evidence.

### The four sectors of production

Production is classified into sectors. The primary sector extracts raw materials: farming, fishing, mining and forestry. The secondary sector manufactures or processes goods: a cement works turning rock into cement, a cannery turning fish into tins, a mill turning cotton into cloth. The tertiary sector provides services: teaching, nursing, transport, banking, shop work. The quaternary sector covers research, information and development, such as software engineering and biotech research. The test for classifying a job is what it does with the material, not the material itself: cotton is grown in the primary sector, spun and woven in the secondary, sold in a shop in the tertiary, and a firm breeding a better seed variety works in the quaternary. The same material can therefore pass through three or four sectors on its way to the user, and each job along that path belongs to a different one.

### Employment structure follows development

Employment structure means the share of workers in each sector of the economy, and it shifts with the level of development in a pattern that repeats across the world. In low-income countries, most workers are in the primary sector - typically farming, often with hand tools - because mechanisation is limited and food takes many hands. In middle-income countries, the secondary share grows as investment builds factories, drawing workers from fields to towns. In high-income countries, most workers are in the tertiary sector, with services expanding as incomes and education rise, and a small quaternary sector appears. The shift from primary to secondary to tertiary as a country develops is one of the clearest patterns in economic geography, and the explanation runs through machines, wages and markets in the next paragraphs.

### Why the primary share falls

Three linked changes push the primary share down as a country develops. Machines and better seed raise each farmer's output, so fewer farmers can feed the whole country - a falling share does not mean less food. Better-paid factory and service jobs draw workers from the land to towns, so the secondary and tertiary shares grow at farming's expense. And as incomes rise, households buy more services - shops, transport, health care, banking - so tertiary work keeps expanding. A warning about reading the figures: employment structure records shares of workers, not their numbers. A country with a large population can hold more farmers in total than a small country while having a smaller share of them, and a table figure such as "primary 63 per cent" means 63 of every 100 workers, not 63 workers.

### Globalisation and its drivers

Globalisation is the growing links between countries through trade, investment, communication and culture. Three forces have driven it. Container shipping and ever larger ships have cut the cost of carrying goods across oceans, so parts and products can be made in different countries and assembled far from where they are sold. Communications - the internet, mobile phones, video calls - allow a firm to manage offices, factories and suppliers on several continents at once. And trade agreements and transnational corporations (TNCs) - companies that operate in more than one country - have built the links themselves, moving investment to where costs are lower and selling where markets are largest. The result is that a single product - a shirt, a phone - may be designed in one country, made in a second from parts of a third, and sold in a fourth.

### Impacts traced along the chain

Globalisation's impacts differ from place to place, so a full answer traces the chain rather than listing words. For a low-income country that attracts a TNC factory, the cause is investment and the effect is jobs and wages where few formal jobs existed before, plus new skills and tax revenue - so the benefit is real. For workers elsewhere the chain can run the other way: production moves abroad, so jobs are lost in the old industrial towns, and because the TNC's profits flow to its headquarters country, part of the wealth created leaves the host country. For consumers almost everywhere, imported goods cost less, which raises living standards - though long-distance transport adds to the world's carbon emissions. And a place that depends on one industry or export is exposed when the links move on, because the same ease of movement that brought the factory can take it away.

### Weighing globalisation

Because the impacts are mixed, an evaluation states a judgement and backs it with evidence rather than deciding that globalisation is simply good or bad. A fair judgement for many low-income countries in the 2020s: globalisation has helped, because factory and service jobs have grown where investment arrived, but the gains can be fragile, because the same links let production move on when wages or costs rise elsewhere. The judgement names who gains and who loses - factory workers versus towns that lost their plants, consumers versus the environment - and the evidence is the pattern of jobs, wages and profits. That is what separates an evaluation from a list of benefits and problems: the list is the raw material, and the judgement is the answer.

## Case study: Shoprite, a transnational corporation and its global links

### The corporation and its scale

Shoprite Holdings is a transnational corporation: a company that operates in more than one country. It grew from a Cape Town supermarket chain founded in 1979 into Africa's largest food retailer, with its head office at Brackenfell in Cape Town and a listing on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange. By the 2020s it ran more than 3 000 stores in about 15 African countries (approximate figures), selling groceries to millions of shoppers a week. Namibia was its first step outside South Africa, in 1990, and the group now trades from more than 30 Namibian towns, from Windhoek to Katima Mulilo. That spread makes it a textbook example: one head office, many countries, and a web of links between them.

### Its global links, followed one by one

Each link has a direction, and naming it is what the case study asks for. Goods: fresh produce and packaged stock are sourced from farms and factories in several countries and move through the group's large distribution centres in South Africa before reaching Namibian shelves, though stores also buy from Namibian bakers, dairies and vegetable growers. People: Namibian staff work in the stores of a company whose buying, pricing and strategy are decided at the Cape Town head office. Money: the prices Namibian shoppers pay flow back partly as wages and local supplier payments, and partly as profit to the group's shareholders. So a single tin on a Windhoek shelf joins Namibia to farms, factories, workers and investors in several countries at once - which is globalisation, drawn at the scale of one shop.

### Why there, or what there?

A frequent error in questions about transnational corporations is describing what the company does in a place - the jobs it creates, the wages it pays - when the question asks why it operates there. Location reasons (the market, lower labour costs, transport links, government incentives) are not the same as impacts (jobs, wages, pollution, profits leaving the country). The test is whether the answer says why the company is there, or what it does there.
