## Coasts: processes, landforms and management

### Erosion: how the sea attacks the land

Wind blowing over the sea raises waves, and waves do most of the work on a coast. Where they meet the shore they erode it in three ways. In hydraulic action, the water is forced into cracks and joints in the rock, and air is trapped and compressed inside them; each wave is a blow of pressure, and over years the rock is loosened and broken. In abrasion, the waves pick up sand and pebbles and hurl them against the cliff face, scouring it the way sandpaper scours wood. In corrosion, sea water slowly dissolves rock it can chemically attack, most visibly limestone. The three work together: hydraulic action loosens blocks, abrasion grinds them away, and the sea carries off the pieces.

### Transport and deposition

Waves do more than wear the coast away; they move the wreckage along it. Where waves approach a beach at an angle, the swash - the water running up the sand - carries material diagonally up the shore, and the backwash drags it straight back down. Pebble by pebble, the beach material creeps along the coast. This is longshore drift, the coast's conveyor belt. As pebbles travel they grind against one another and wear smaller and rounder, a process called attrition as bouncing, sliding grains. Deposition begins wherever the water loses its energy. In the shelter of a bay, waves arrive weak and drop their load, and a beach builds. Where the coastline turns sharply at a river mouth or a bay, longshore drift carries material on past the corner and drops it in deeper, slower water, building a spit - a long ridge of sand or shingle joined to the land at one end, often curving where the wind changes direction. The wind builds too: at low tide a wide beach dries out, and the wind lifts the fine dry sand and blows it inland, where it piles into coastal sand dunes. So the sea builds beaches and spits, and the wind builds the dunes behind them.

### Three ways the rock breaks

A common error is to swap the erosion processes: writing that abrasion is the force of water and air in the cracks, or that hydraulic action is stones thrown at the cliff. Each name carries its mechanism. Hydraulic action breaks rock by pressure: water and trapped air forced into cracks. Abrasion breaks it by impact: stones hurled against the cliff face. Corrosion breaks it by dissolving: sea water chemically attacking rock such as limestone. The test for any answer is one question: is the rock broken by pressure, by impact, or by dissolving? Name the mechanism with the process, and the confusion has nowhere to live.

### Cliffs and wave-cut platforms

Waves attack a coast at its base, where the breaking water concentrates their energy. Hydraulic action and abrasion cut a notch into the rock at the high-tide line and below it, and the cliff above, undercut, is left hanging. In time the overhanging rock collapses, and the fallen blocks are broken up and carried away by the waves. The cliff line has moved inland, and the flat rocky area left at its foot is a wave-cut platform. The process then starts again on the new cliff face, so a retreating cliff leaves a wider and wider platform behind it.

### From crack to stump

The same attack on a headland passes through a sequence worth learning in order. Waves find a line of weakness - a crack - in the headland's rock and widen it by hydraulic action and abrasion into a cave. The cave is deepened from both sides of the headland until it breaks through, and the tunnel left is an arch. The arch's roof, unsupported, eventually collapses, and the pillar of rock left standing in the sea beside the ruined arch is a stack. The stack, attacked from all sides, is worn down and lowered until at high tide it is barely clear of the water: this is the stump. The order is crack, cave, arch, stack, stump - each stage the previous one taken further.

### Bays, headlands and beaches

Where the coast is made of bands of hard and soft rock meeting the shore at an angle, erosion sorts the landforms. The soft rock wears back fastest, leaving a curved bay; the hard rock resists and stands out as a headland. The headland then concentrates the waves' energy - waves bend round it and break against it on both sides - so it is eroded through the crack-to-stump sequence, while in the bay the weaker water deposits sand and shingle, and a beach builds. Bays and headlands are the large-scale shape of the whole coast; caves, arches and stacks are the smaller features cut into the headland between them. Keeping the two scales apart keeps an answer clear: the bay is a shape of the coastline, the cave a hole in one place on it.

### A spit, worked through

Take a coast running west to east, with a river mouth cutting north into it, and waves arriving from the south-west. The waves strike the shore at an angle, so longshore drift moves beach sand steadily from west to east. At the river mouth the coastline turns, but the drift carries the sand on past the corner and drops it into the deeper, slower water of the estuary mouth. The ridge that builds is a spit: joined to the land at its western end, growing eastwards, and often curving into a hook at its far end where the wind or the tide swings the settling material round. Updrift of the spit the beach may thin, because the sand that would have fed it has been carried onto the spit instead. The worked sequence - angled waves, drifting load, a corner where the load drops - is the same for any spit asked about.

### Coral reefs and mangrove swamps

A coral reef is not a rock but a living structure: a ridge of limestone built from the stony skeletons of coral polyps, animals that live as colonies in shallow tropical seas. Each polyp keeps single-celled algae inside its body, and those algae need sunlight to make food, so the coral depends on light. That is why the conditions for a reef are so tight: warm water, roughly 20 degrees Celsius or warmer; clear water, so the light passes through; shallow water, so the light reaches the seabed; and salty water. A mangrove swamp is the tropical coast's other great living structure: a belt of mangrove trees rooted in the mud of sheltered shores that the tide floods each day. The trees stand on tangled aerial roots that trap the mud and breathe the air, and they tolerate the salt. So the two develop under opposite conditions: coral in clear, sunlit water, mangroves in the muddy water of quiet, sheltered bays.

### What the reef is like, and what it needs

When a question asks for the characteristics of a coral reef, some learners answer with the conditions for its growth instead: warm, clear, shallow, salty water. That is the wrong angle. Characteristics describe what the reef is like - a ridge of limestone, hard and colourful, built by living coral, lying close beneath the surface. Conditions describe what the reef needs before it can grow at all. The test is to ask whether the answer is about the reef or about its requirements. An answer about the wrong one describes something true and answers something else.

### Opportunities on the coast

People gather on coasts because a coast is a place of opportunities. Flat, low land beside the sea is easy to build on, so settlements cluster there. A sheltered bay with deep water makes a harbour, and a harbour makes trade, which brings work in unloading, warehousing and ships' services. The sea's fish are food and a livelihood for a fishing fleet and its processing plants. And the beach and the scenery draw visitors, whose spending supports hotels, restaurants and tour operators. Each opportunity carries its hazard - the flat, low land that is easy to build on is the land the sea can reach - which is why coasts are where hazards and opportunities arrive together.

### Hazards on the coast

The sea that gives a coast its living also threatens it. Storm winds and low pressure raise the sea into a storm surge, a temporary rise in sea level that floods low-lying coastal land. Ordinary weather erodes: waves cut into cliffs and narrow beaches, and a house or road standing too near the edge is undermined as the coastline moves inland. Tropical storms combine the hazards at once - destructive winds, heavy rain on the coast's low ground, and a surge that can push the sea several metres above its usual level. The people most drawn to the coast's opportunities are the people most exposed to its hazards.

### From storm to ruined harvest

Tropical storm Idai, which struck the port of Beira in Mozambique in March 2019, shows the chain in a real place. The storm's low pressure and its winds blowing onshore raised the sea into a storm surge, so the ocean flooded inland across the flat coastal plain. The flood carried salt water onto farmland, which means the soil was left salty and waterlogged after the water drained away, so the season's crops failed and, where water stood for weeks, the fields could not be replanted. The chain runs cause to effect: low pressure and onshore wind, so surge and flood, which means salt on the fields, so harvest lost. The flood, not the wind, is what ruined the harvest - and it can ruin ground far inland of where the storm itself arrived.

### Managing coastal erosion

Coastal management is judged on how each measure works, not on how much concrete it contains. Hard defences face the waves down. A sea wall, usually curved, reflects wave energy back out to sea, and a promenade or road is often built along the top of it. Rock armour - boulders piled at the cliff foot - absorbs the waves' energy in the gaps between them. Groynes are short walls built out from the beach at right angles to the shore; they trap the sand that longshore drift moves along the coast, so the beach between them keeps its width, and it is the wide beach, not the groyne, that takes the day-to-day wave energy. Soft management works with the sand instead. Beach nourishment trucks or pumps sand onto a narrowing beach, widening it again - and because the sea keeps removing sand, nourishment has to be repeated. Managed retreat accepts that some land is lost and moves buildings and uses out of the sea's reach. Whichever measure is named, the answer should say what it does to the waves or the sand.

### Name the defence, then say what it does

A list of defences - sea wall, groynes, rock armour - says little if the answer stops there. Each defence reduces erosion in a way that can be said in one clause: the sea wall reflects wave energy, the groynes trap the sand that longshore drift carries along the beach, the rock armour absorbs the waves' force in the gaps between its boulders. The test of a complete answer is whether it says what the defence does to the waves or the sand. A name on its own is a noun; the explanation is for the verb.

## Case study: the Namib coast (Swakopmund and Walvis Bay)

### Opportunities: Swakopmund and Walvis Bay

Walvis Bay, about 30 kilometres south of Swakopmund on Namibia's Atlantic coast, owns the country's main deep-water port; its new container terminal opened in 2019, and the town is the heart of Namibia's fishing and fish-processing industry, with salt works on the pans to the south. Swakopmund, founded as a colonial port in 1892, lives mainly on tourism: its sea-front hotels, shore angling, dolphin cruises out of Walvis Bay, and trips into the dune belt behind the town.

### The hazards of the Namib coast

The coast is desert - rainfall is usually below about 20 millimetres a year - yet the hazards still arrive. The cold Benguela Current offshore cools the moist air into fog on many days each year, and the fog is a hazard to travel on the coast road. The dune belt between the two towns is mobile: the prevailing south-west wind blows the dry sand inland, over fencing and onto the road that crosses it. And at Swakopmund the low sea front is eroded by high tides and storms.

### Managing the hazards

Management protects where people and trade gather. Swakopmund's sea front is defended by a stone promenade that works as a sea wall, and the town's buildings behind it have stood for more than a century. The road across the dune belt has to be kept clear of drifting sand, and the port's breakwaters shelter Walvis Bay's harbour. Beyond the built-up ground the dunes are left to move as they will, because defending empty desert is not worth the cost - a judgement that shows management is a decision about where to spend, not a wall along the whole coast.
