## Density, distribution and the factors behind them

### Density and distribution are different

Population density is the number of people per unit area, people per square kilometre, worked as total population divided by area: a district of 120 000 people on 300 square kilometres has 120 000 / 300 = 400 per square kilometre. Population distribution is different: it is where people live and where they do not, the pattern of spread. A distribution answer describes the pattern, dense along the river, thin in the north-west, and does it without listing place name after place name.

### The physical factors, each with its direction

Every physical factor must be linked to the way it pushes density. Steep relief makes farming and building hard, so density is low on high mountains; flat, fertile lowland grows crops and carries roads, so density is high. Reliable rain and rivers raise density, because crops and drinking water are secure, while dry climates lower it, because neither crops nor herds can live off the land. The error to avoid is naming a factor without saying which way it pushes: "the relief" is not an answer until it says low density here, high density there, and why.

### The economic and human factors

Jobs, ports and trade routes raise density, because people settle where the work and the markets are, and a city on a good harbour pulls in trade and the people who serve it. Poor access thins a population out, because goods and travellers pass it by, and weak soils and few jobs do the same. Physical factors set the stage and economic factors move the people: a desert oasis can grow if a mine opens, and a fertile valley can empty if its road closes.

### Causes are not consequences

A sparse population is caused by physical and economic factors: relief, climate, water, soils, access and jobs. A shortage of services is not one of the causes, because it comes after the emptiness: the few people cannot support schools and clinics, so the services thin out too. The test is whether the factor came before the sparse population or because of it.

## Case studies: dense and sparse

### Dense: the Ganges delta

The Ganges delta in Bangladesh is one of the densest rural areas on Earth: the country averages around 1125 people per square kilometre (2022) and the delta districts run higher still, with village after village threaded along the embankments between paddy fields. The delta supports its density because annual floods renew the soil for rice, the rivers give water and transport, and three harvests a year are possible, so the same hectare feeds far more people than dryland farming could. That is the pattern the syllabus asks for made concrete: flat, fertile, well-watered land, so people settle everywhere on it, densely and continuously. The density is rural, which is what makes it remarkable: most of the delta's people are farmers, not city dwellers, and the villages sit a few hundred metres apart along the embankments with paddy between them, so the countryside itself carries the density. The same closeness brings the pressures topic 1.1 describes, tiny farms per family and services run past capacity, and it makes land the scarce thing: houses are built on the field edges because farmland is too valuable to build on, and the region's roads thread between fields too narrow to lose.

### Sparse: the Namib coast

The Namib Desert coast of Namibia is sparsely populated: the Erongo and Kunene regions along it hold only a few people per square kilometre, against Windhoek's thousands, because the coast receives under 50 millimetres of rain a year. The sparseness rests on physical limits: with almost no rain, neither crops nor herds can live off the land, and the cold ocean and strong winds make settled farming harder still. The people who do live there cluster at the few places with water or other work, Walvis Bay's port and the uranium mining town of Arandis, so the distribution is one of tiny nodes in a very large emptiness. The nodes survive on engineered water, Walvis Bay's desalination and piped groundwater and Arandis's inland pipeline, and when the engineered water goes, the node goes with it, as the abandoned mine settlements inland of the coast show. The mine at Brandberg West, for instance, closed and its people left, because a settlement cannot outlive its water. This order of events is worth holding on to in an answer: state the rainfall and the soils first, the water engineering second, and the thin services last, and the argument carries itself. The same test works in reverse on a mark-hunting answer, because a cause offered after the consequence has the story running backwards, and an answer that opens with the missing clinics has not yet explained anything.For a learner in Namibia this is a familiar scale of contrast: the whole Namib coast holds fewer people than one Windhoek suburb, though the region is hundreds of kilometres long, and the emptiness between the nodes is grazing for hardy livestock and little else.

### Why the Namib has few services

A sparse population keeps few services, and the order matters: the emptiness comes first, and the thin services follow it. The few people of the Namib coast cannot support schools, clinics and shops within reach of every household, which is a consequence of the low density rather than a cause of it, the same distinction the first half of this topic draws.

### Where this connects

The two case studies answer any 7-mark question that names a dense or a sparse area, and they pair: the delta's floods are the river processes of topic 2.2 at work, and the Namib's aridity is the climate of topic 2.5. Namibia's own population clusters on the central plateau and the north, a distribution this topic's factors explain.
