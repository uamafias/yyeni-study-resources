## Turning effects of forces

### Centre of gravity

The weight of an object is really the pull of the Earth on every part of it, part by part. In force and moment calculations the whole of that weight may be taken to act at a single point, called the centre of gravity of the object. The centre of gravity is the point where the moments of the weights of all the parts balance. For a uniform object of symmetrical shape, such as a metre rule or a solid ball, the centre of gravity sits at the geometric centre of the object. For some objects it lies outside the material altogether: the centre of gravity of a uniform ring is at its centre, in the empty space, and the centre of gravity of a boomerang lies between its arms.

### Why one point is enough

Taking the whole weight as one force acting at the centre of gravity gives the same turning effect as adding up the effect of the weight of each part separately. That is what makes the substitution valid: the centre of gravity is defined as the point where the separate moments of the parts balance, so the single force there reproduces the total turning effect about a pivot. When a uniform plank balances on a fulcrum placed directly under its centre of gravity, the clockwise and anticlockwise moments of its parts match, and it sits level. The same shortcut lets a crane driver treat a loaded container as one force acting at one point.

### Moment of a force

Forces can turn things as well as push them. The moment of a force about a point is the force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force. In symbols, moment = Fd, where F is the force in newtons (N) and d is the perpendicular distance in metres (m) from the point to the line of action of the force. The unit of moment is the newton metre, written N m. The distance must be measured at right angles to the force: imagine dropping a line from the point at 90° to the direction in which the force acts, and taking that line's length. A force whose line of action passes straight through the point has a perpendicular distance of zero, so its moment about that point is zero. A push directed straight through a hinge does not swing the door, however hard it is applied.

### A force at an angle

A force does not need to act at right angles to the thing it turns. When a force acts at an angle to a lever, only its component at right angles to the lever does the turning. The moment about the pivot is then F × d × sin θ, where F is the force in newtons, θ is the angle between the force and the lever, and d is the distance along the lever from the pivot to the point where the force is applied. There are two equivalent routes to the same answer: multiply the force by the component of the distance perpendicular to it, or multiply the perpendicular component of the force by the distance along the lever. A longer lever gives a larger moment for the same force, which is why a long spanner shifts a stubborn wheel nut where a short one does not.

### Worked example: a wheel spanner

A mechanic changes a wheel on a minibus taxi outside Otjiwarongo. A wheel nut needs a moment of 45 N m to loosen it. Her spanner reaches 0.30 m from the nut to her hand, and she pushes at right angles to it with a force of 150 N. The moment is moment = Fd = 150 N × 0.30 m = 45 N m, which matches what the nut needs, so it turns. If she pushes along the spanner instead, at 0° to its length, the component at right angles to the spanner is zero, so the moment is zero however hard she pushes. At 40° to the spanner the same 150 N gives only 150 N × 0.30 m × sin 40°, which is about 29 N m: too little. Distance perpendicular to the force, and nothing else, is what counts.

### Couples

Some arrangements of forces are built to turn things and to do nothing else. A couple is a pair of forces that are equal in size, opposite in direction and parallel, and whose lines of action do not coincide. Because the two forces are equal and opposite, they add to zero as vectors: a couple produces no resultant force at all, and it acts to produce rotation only. Turning a car's steering wheel with two hands makes a couple: one hand pushes one side of the rim up and the other pulls the opposite side down, with forces of equal size. The wheel is not pushed sideways, because the two forces cancel as a push; it is turned, because they do not cancel as a turning effect.

### A couple turns without pushing

The total moment of a couple comes out the same whatever point is chosen as the pivot, which is what makes it a property of the pair of forces rather than of any particular point. Take two forces of size F whose lines of action are a perpendicular distance s apart. About a point on the line of action of one force, that force has zero moment and the other acts at a distance s, giving a total moment Fs. About a point midway between them, each force acts at a distance of s/2 and the two moments point the same way, giving F × s/2 twice, which is Fs again. About any other point the two individual moments change, but their sum stays Fs. A couple is pure turning effect, free of any push.

### Torque of a couple

The torque of a couple is one of the forces multiplied by the perpendicular distance between the two lines of action. In symbols, torque = Fs, where F is one of the forces in newtons and s is the perpendicular distance between the forces in metres. Like a moment, torque is measured in newton metres (N m). The word torque belongs to a pair of forces: for a single force we speak of its moment about a chosen point. Because the total moment of a couple is the same about a point, the torque of a couple can be stated without naming a pivot at all.

### Worked example: a steering wheel

A driver turns a minibus steering wheel of diameter 0.42 m, gripping opposite points of the rim. Each hand applies a force of 11 N tangentially to the rim, one up and one down, so the hands form a couple. The perpendicular distance between the two lines of action is the diameter, 0.42 m. Torque = Fs = 11 N × 0.42 m = 4.62 N m, so the torque is 4.6 N m to two significant figures. Checking the same answer the longer way, about the centre of the wheel: each force has a moment of 11 N × 0.21 m = 2.31 N m in the same sense, and the two add to 4.62 N m. Either route gives the same torque, as it must, because the moment of a couple does not depend on the point chosen.

Torque returns in topic 6, where the twisting of a wire is described the same way. ## Equilibrium of forces

### The principle of moments

The principle of moments states the condition for a body not to start turning: for a body in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about a single point is equal to the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that same point. Three things belong in a complete statement: the two sums, the single point both are taken about, and the equilibrium condition. A see-saw with riders of different weights stays level when the heavier rider sits closer to the pivot, so that the clockwise moments match the anticlockwise moments. The principle is a statement about moments, so it is used alongside the condition that the forces balance.

### Applying the principle

A moments problem is worked in a fixed order. Choose the point to take moments about. Resolve any force that acts at an angle, or measure its perpendicular distance directly. Calculate each moment, and sort them into clockwise and anticlockwise about the chosen point. Then set the sum of the clockwise moments equal to the sum of the anticlockwise moments and solve for the unknown. The point can be chosen freely, and a good choice does half the work: taking moments about a point that an unknown force acts through removes that force from the equation, because its moment about that point is zero. A reaction at a support, for example, vanishes from the equation the moment the support itself is chosen as the point.

### Worked example: a wheelbarrow

A gardener in Windhoek lifts a loaded wheelbarrow. The total weight of barrow and load is 245 N, acting at the centre of gravity, which sits 0.50 m behind the wheel's axle. Her hands on the handles are 1.10 m behind the axle, and she lifts straight upwards with force L. In equilibrium, the moments about the axle balance: clockwise moment of the weight = anticlockwise moment of the lift, so 245 N × 0.50 m = L × 1.10 m. The left side is 245 × 0.50 = 122.5 N m, so L = 122.5 ÷ 1.10 = 111.4 N, which is 110 N to two significant figures. The wheel's upward reaction takes the rest of the weight; it never enters the equation because moments were taken about the axle.

### Equilibrium

A system is in equilibrium when two conditions hold at the same time: the resultant force on it is zero, and the resultant torque on it is zero. Zero resultant force removes any linear acceleration; zero resultant torque removes any starting to rotate. Both conditions must be checked separately, because one can hold while the other fails. Equilibrium is a statement about forces and turning effects, not a description of how fast something happens to be moving.

### At rest is not the whole story

It is tempting to say a body at rest is in equilibrium. Rest by itself shows nothing of the kind. A ball at the top of its flight is momentarily at rest while its weight still acts on it, and a beam supported at one end can have balanced vertical forces yet still be turned by its own weight about the support. The error is concluding equilibrium from rest, or from zero resultant force alone. The test is the two zero resultants, checked separately: the forces must balance, and the moments about a point must balance too. A body at rest in equilibrium stays at rest; a body at rest with a resultant torque on it starts to turn.

### What equilibrium means for the motion

A system in equilibrium does not accelerate and does not begin to rotate. It stays at rest, or keeps moving at a steady velocity in a straight line without starting to turn. The link runs both ways. If both resultants are zero, no push or twist is left over to change the motion. If a system's velocity is steady and its rotation is not changing, the forces on it must balance and the moments about a point must balance, so the system is in equilibrium.

### The triangle of vectors

Three coplanar forces in equilibrium can be drawn as a closed triangle. Draw the first force as an arrow. Draw the second starting head to tail from its tip, parallel to the force and scaled to its size. Draw the third the same way from the tip of the second. Because the arrows return to the starting point, the three forces add to zero: the triangle closes, and a closed triangle of vectors has no resultant. No side of the triangle is a resultant force. Each side is one of the three forces, drawn head to tail; for forces in equilibrium there is no resultant to find.

### Solving with the vector triangle

The angles inside the triangle come from the geometry of the situation, not from the forces themselves. A banner hanging from two ropes is a common case: the ropes make stated angles with the vertical or the horizontal, and those angles set the corners of the triangle once the weight is drawn pointing straight down. With the triangle's angles known, the two unknown force sizes follow by trigonometry: usually the sine rule, or simple cosines if the triangle is right-angled. A rough sketch of the triangle, drawn to scale, doubles as a check on the arithmetic: if the third side does not close the triangle, the two computed forces do not balance the weight.

### Worked example: a banner over a shop

A banner weighing 40 N hangs from a bracket outside a shop in Oshakati. A rope holds it, making an angle of 40° with the vertical, and a horizontal strut pushes out from the wall with force S. The three forces in equilibrium are: the weight 40 N straight down, the tension T along the rope, and the strut's push S horizontal. Drawn head to tail they close as a right-angled triangle: the weight is the side adjacent to the 40° angle, the strut's push is the side opposite it, and the rope's tension is the hypotenuse. So T = 40 N ÷ cos 40° = 40 ÷ 0.766 = 52.2 N, which is 52 N to two significant figures, and S = 40 N × tan 40° = 40 × 0.839 = 33.6 N, which is 34 N to two significant figures. The triangle closes, so there is no resultant: the banner hangs still.

## Density and pressure

### Density

Density is mass per unit volume. In symbols, ρ = m/V, where ρ (rho) is the density in kilograms per cubic metre (kg m⁻³), m is the mass in kilograms (kg) and V is the volume in cubic metres (m³). To find the density of a sample, measure its mass and its volume and divide the mass by the volume. A measuring cylinder gives the volume of a liquid or of a small solid by displacement; a balance gives the mass. Water has a density of about 1000 kg m⁻³, and most common oils are lighter, which is why oil floats on water. A litre of water occupies 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³ and has a mass of 1.0 kg, giving ρ = 1.0 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³.

### Worked example: a brass fitting

A workshop in Walvis Bay casts a small brass fitting. Its mass on a balance is 268 g, and it displaces 31.0 mL of water in a measuring cylinder. Convert first: the mass is 268 g = 0.268 kg, and the volume is 31.0 mL = 31.0 × 10⁻⁶ m³, which is 3.10 × 10⁻⁵ m³. Then ρ = m/V = 0.268 ÷ 3.10 × 10⁻⁵, so ρ = 8645 kg m⁻³, which is 8600 kg m⁻³ to two significant figures. In a buoyancy problem, keep clear about which density is which: 8645 kg m⁻³ is the density of the brass, not of the water it sits in. The fluid's density belongs with the displaced volume when the upthrust is calculated later in this unit.

### Pressure

Pressure is force per unit area, acting at right angles to the area. In symbols, p = F/A, where p is the pressure in pascals (Pa), F is the force in newtons (N) and A is the area in square metres (m²). One pascal is one newton per square metre. A fluid at rest presses at right angles against any surface it touches, whatever the orientation of that surface: the sides of a drum, the bottom of a tank, a diver's suit. Because the same force spread over a smaller area gives a larger pressure, a thin blade cuts and a sharp point enters wood where a blunt one does not. A drawing pin is pushed board-side first: the thumb spread over the wide head feels a small pressure, while the point concentrates the same force into a tiny area.

### Deriving ∆p = ρg∆h

The pressure difference between two depths in a liquid follows from the definitions of pressure and density in three steps. First, picture a vertical column of the liquid with cross-sectional area A, running from the higher depth to the lower depth, a vertical distance ∆h below it. Second, the mass of that column is its volume times the density: m = ρA∆h, so its weight pressing on the base is W = mg = ρgA∆h. Third, this weight acts on an area A of the base, so the extra pressure at the lower depth is ∆p = F/A = ρgA∆h ÷ A, and the area cancels, leaving ∆p = ρg∆h. Here ∆p is the pressure difference in pascals, ρ is the liquid's density in kg m⁻³, g is the acceleration of free fall in N kg⁻¹ and ∆h is the vertical distance between the two depths in metres. The area cancels, so the pressure difference does not depend on the shape or the width of the container, and ∆h is measured vertically.

### Using ∆p = ρg∆h

The equation is used with a single liquid of density ρ, and it gives the difference in pressure between two depths, ∆h apart vertically. For a fixed liquid the pressure difference grows in direct proportion to the depth difference: doubling ∆h doubles ∆p. Where the liquid's surface is open to the air, the total pressure at a depth is the atmospheric pressure on the surface plus the difference ∆p = ρg∆h measured down from the surface. What matters in any container is the vertical depth: a dam wall feels the same pressure at a given depth whether the reservoir behind it is wide or narrow.

### Worked example: a farm dam

A farm dam outside Gobabis holds water to a depth of 6.0 m. The density of water is 1000 kg m⁻³ and g is 9.81 N kg⁻¹. The pressure difference between the surface and the bottom is ∆p = ρg∆h = 1000 × 9.81 × 6.0 = 58860 Pa, which is 5.9 × 10⁴ Pa to two significant figures. Halfway down, at ∆h = 3.0 m, the same working gives ∆p = 1000 × 9.81 × 3.0 = 29430 Pa, half the bottom value, because ∆p is proportional to ∆h. The total pressure at the bottom is this difference plus the atmospheric pressure of about 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa on the surface: a gauge reading only the water's contribution gives 5.9 × 10⁴ Pa, while the total on the wall is about 1.6 × 10⁵ Pa.

### Upthrust from a pressure difference

An object in a fluid feels an upward push, and the cause is a difference in hydrostatic pressure. The pressure in a liquid increases with depth, so the fluid presses harder on the object's lower face than on its upper face. Force is pressure times area, so the upward push on the lower face is larger than the downward push on the upper face, and the difference between them is a resultant upward force: the upthrust. The upthrust acts upwards no matter how deep the object sits, because the pressures on the two faces both grow with depth but their difference stays fixed. Upthrust is a force measured in newtons; pressure is force per unit area measured in pascals. They are not the same quantity, and writing one for the other in a calculation loses the area.

### Archimedes' principle

Archimedes' principle states that the upthrust on an object in a fluid is equal to the weight of the fluid the object displaces. The upthrust is F = ρgV, where ρ is the density of the fluid in kg m⁻³, g is the acceleration of free fall in N kg⁻¹ and V is the volume of fluid displaced in m³. Watch which density and which volume go in: ρ is the fluid's density, and V is the volume the object pushes aside, not the whole volume of anything else. The object's own weight is a different force, and it is not the upthrust. A floating object displaces exactly the volume whose weight equals its own weight, which is why the upthrust on it balances its weight.

### Worked example: a pontoon on the Orange River

A rectangular steel pontoon carrying a pump floats on the Orange River. Its submerged part displaces 2.4 m³ of water. The density of water is 1000 kg m⁻³. The upthrust is F = ρgV = 1000 × 9.81 × 2.4 = 23544 N, which is 2.4 × 10⁴ N to two significant figures. Because the pontoon floats in equilibrium, the upthrust balances its total weight: the weight of the pontoon and the pump together is 2.4 × 10⁴ N, giving a total mass of 23544 ÷ 9.81 = 2400 kg. Notice the division of labour between the two quantities: the fluid's density and the displaced volume set the upthrust, while the object's weight answers for itself.

### Worked example: two liquids in a U-tube

A science club in Rundu pours water into a U-tube, then adds sunflower oil of density 920 kg m⁻³ gently down one side. The liquids do not mix. At rest, the oil column stands 0.120 m taller than the water surface on the other side, and the question is where the two pressures balance. The pressure at the water surface open to the air is atmospheric, about 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. In the oil arm, the pressure at the bottom of the oil column is the same atmospheric pressure plus the oil's contribution: ∆p(oil) = ρg∆h = 920 × 9.81 × 0.120 = 108.3 Pa. In the water arm, the pressure at the same horizontal level is atmospheric pressure plus the water's contribution over a smaller depth ∆h(water). At the level where the two liquids meet in the oil arm, the pressures on the two sides are equal, because a fluid at rest cannot sustain a pressure difference at the same height: the extra 0.120 m of oil, being lighter than water, is balanced by a shorter column of water. Setting the two contributions equal: 920 × 9.81 × 0.120 = 1000 × 9.81 × ∆h(water), so 108.3 ÷ 9810 = 0.01104 m, which is 0.011 m of water to two significant figures. The check is the whole point: pressure at a given depth depends on the liquid's density and the vertical distance down, and the equation is applied once for each liquid with its own ρ and its own ∆h, and the two pressures are then compared at one level.

### Whose density, whose volume

The upthrust equation F = ρgV looks like one multiplication, and that is where the errors creep in. The error is reaching for the object's own numbers: its own density, its own volume read as the displaced volume, or its own weight written down as the upthrust. Each is a different slip with the same cure. The density in the equation is the fluid's, because the upthrust is the push of the surrounding fluid and has nothing to do with what the object is made of: a steel ball and a wooden ball of the same size, held fully underwater, feel the same upthrust. The volume is the volume of fluid pushed aside, which for a fully submerged object is the object's whole volume, but for a floating object is only the submerged part. And the weight of the object is a separate force that belongs on the other side of the equilibrium, not inside the upthrust. The test, used in the worked examples above, is to ask for each symbol in turn: whose mass and whose volume are in the ratio, and which body is doing the pushing. A useful habit is to label the equation in words first - upthrust equals the fluid's density times g times the displaced volume - and only then reach for the numbers.
