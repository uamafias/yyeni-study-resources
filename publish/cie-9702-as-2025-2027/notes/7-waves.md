## General wave properties

### Wave motion

Wave motion is the transfer of energy away from a source through the oscillations of a medium, with no net transfer of the medium itself in the direction of travel. The source — a hand shaking a rope, a loudspeaker cone moving to and fro, a bar dipping into water — disturbs the particles of the medium next to it. Each disturbed particle then drives the particle beside it, and the disturbance is handed along the line while each particle stays close to its own rest position. That is the defining picture of wave motion: energy travels, matter does not. Such a wave is called a progressive wave because the pattern of the disturbance moves forwards through the medium, carrying energy away from the source as it goes.

### Seeing wave motion in ropes, springs and ripple tanks

Three classroom demonstrations show the same physics. Flick the free end of a stretched rope sharply sideways once, and a pulse runs along it: each part of the rope rises and falls as the pulse passes, and the rope as a whole stays where it is. Push one end of a stretched slinky forwards and back along its own length, and a squashed region — a compression — travels the length of the spring while each coil only shuttles to and fro about its rest position.

In a ripple tank, a bar that dips into the water at regular intervals sends out straight wavefronts across the surface. A small cork floating on the water bobs up and down as each wavefront passes, but it stays above the same place on the tank floor: the water itself does not travel with the wavefronts. In each demonstration the oscillating parts stay put while the disturbance — and the energy it carries — moves on. A ripple tank also lets you slow everything down enough to measure: the distance between neighbouring wavefronts is the wavelength, and counting how many times the cork rises in ten seconds gives the frequency.

### Displacement and amplitude

In this topic and the next, displacement carries its wave sense: it is the distance of a point of the medium from its rest position at a given instant. Displacement is counted positive on one side of the rest position and negative on the other, so for a single point it changes all through the oscillation and passes through zero twice in each cycle. Amplitude is different: it is the maximum displacement, a fixed number for a steady wave, reached only at the top of each oscillation.

Take a water wave that lifts a cork 12 mm above its rest position and drops it 12 mm below. The amplitude of the wave is 12 mm. The displacement of the cork is +12 mm at the top of its motion, zero as it passes its rest position, and −12 mm at the bottom. Asking for the displacement means asking where the point is now; asking for the amplitude means asking how far the point can get.

### Period, frequency, wavelength, phase difference and speed

The period T is the time one complete oscillation takes, measured in seconds (s). The frequency f is the number of complete oscillations per unit time, measured in hertz (Hz), where 1 Hz is one oscillation each second. Period and frequency are reciprocals: T = 1 ÷ f. A wave whose period is 0.020 s has a frequency of 1 ÷ 0.020 = 50 Hz, and a wave of frequency 50 Hz has a period of 1 ÷ 50 = 0.020 s.

The wavelength λ is the distance between two adjacent points on the wave that are in phase, such as neighbouring crests or neighbouring compressions, measured in metres (m).

Phase difference states how far one oscillation leads or lags another, as a fraction of a cycle, measured in degrees or radians. Two points exactly one wavelength apart oscillate in step, with a phase difference of 360° or 2π radians. Two points half a wavelength apart oscillate in antiphase, 180° apart: when one is at its crest the other is at its trough. On a rope, the two hands of two learners shaking the same rope can be in phase, in antiphase, or anywhere between, and the phase difference is what decides which it is.

The speed v of the wave, in m s⁻¹, is the rate at which the whole profile — the pattern of crests and troughs — advances through the medium. It is not the speed of any individual particle of the medium, which rises and falls through the cycle and is zero at each extreme of the motion.

### A time, a distance or a rate

Period, frequency and wavelength answer three different questions, and mixing them is the commonest slip in this topic. Is the answer wanted a time? Then it is the period, in seconds. A distance? Then it is the wavelength, in metres. A rate, counted per second? Then it is the frequency, in hertz. Before substituting anything into an equation, ask which of the three the question wants. The wavelength of a sound is a length of air between neighbouring compressions; the period is the time one oscillation of that air takes; the frequency counts the oscillations each second. Confusing the period with the frequency or with the wavelength produces an answer with the wrong kind of unit attached, and that is the tell-tale: a wavelength cannot be measured in milliseconds, and a period cannot be measured in metres.

### The cathode-ray oscilloscope (CRO)

A cathode-ray oscilloscope (CRO) draws a trace of signal voltage against time on a screen ruled into divisions. Two settings turn that trace into measurements.

The time-base sets the horizontal scale: it is the time the spot takes to cross one horizontal division, stated as time per division — for example 5.0 ms per division, or 2.0 µs per division. The y-gain sets the vertical scale in volts per division.

To find the amplitude of a signal, measure the height of the trace from the centre line to a peak, in divisions, and multiply by the y-gain. The peak-to-peak height, crest to trough, is twice the amplitude, so the peak-to-peak voltage divided by two also gives it.

To find the frequency, measure the width of one complete cycle, crest to crest, in divisions, and multiply by the time-base to get the period T; the frequency then follows from f = 1 ÷ T. The time-base setting by itself is not the period — the setting is the time for one division, and one whole cycle spans several of them.

### Worked example: reading a CRO trace

A signal from a small microphone gives a steady trace on a CRO. The crests sit 2.5 divisions above the centre line, the troughs 2.5 divisions below, and one complete cycle spans 6.0 divisions. The y-gain is set to 0.50 V per division and the time-base to 2.0 ms per division.

The amplitude: the peak height is 2.5 divisions, so 2.5 × 0.50 = 1.25, and the amplitude is 1.25 V, which is 1.3 V to two significant figures. The peak-to-peak voltage is twice this: 2.5 V.

The period: one cycle spans 6.0 divisions, and the time per division is 2.0 ms, so T = 6.0 × 2.0 = 12 ms. In seconds that is 0.012 s.

The frequency: f = 1 ÷ T, so 1 ÷ 0.012 = 83.3, and the frequency is 83.3 Hz, that is 83 Hz to two significant figures.

Note the order of the steps — read the divisions, apply the setting, then convert the units — and note that the time-base setting of 2.0 ms is not the period; the cycle spans 6.0 divisions, and the period is the width of the whole cycle times the setting.

### Deriving the wave equation v = fλ

The wave equation is not a new law of physics; it follows in three steps from the definitions of speed, frequency and wavelength.

Step one: speed is defined as distance travelled divided by the time taken, v = d ÷ t.

Step two: watch one crest of the wave. In one period T — the time for one complete oscillation — the wave profile advances by exactly one wavelength λ, because after one full oscillation the pattern repeats itself, shifted along by one wavelength. So the distance travelled in time T is λ, and v = λ ÷ T.

Step three: frequency is defined by f = 1 ÷ T, so T = 1 ÷ f. Substituting this into v = λ ÷ T gives v = λ × f, that is v = fλ.

In words: the wave speed, in m s⁻¹, equals the frequency, in hertz, multiplied by the wavelength, in metres. Because the equation is built from definitions, it applies to any progressive wave, mechanical or electromagnetic.

### The wave equation

The wave equation states v = fλ, where v is the speed of the wave in m s⁻¹, f is the frequency in hertz (Hz) and λ is the wavelength in metres (m). It can be rearranged to suit the question: λ = v ÷ f finds a wavelength from a speed and a frequency, and f = v ÷ λ finds a frequency from a speed and a wavelength. When the wave is electromagnetic, v is the speed in free space, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, taken from the data given; when the wave is mechanical, v is the speed in that medium.

### Worked example: swells on the Zambezi

During the rainy season, swells travel up the Zambezi at 2.4 m s⁻¹. A moored boat rises and falls 15 times in 30 s. The frequency is the count of complete oscillations divided by the time: 15 ÷ 30 = 0.50 Hz. The period is the reciprocal: T = 1 ÷ 0.50 = 2.0 s, so the boat is lifted once every 2.0 s.

The wavelength follows from the wave equation rearranged for λ: λ = v ÷ f, so λ = 2.4 ÷ 0.50 = 4.8. The swells are 4.8 m apart, to two significant figures.

Carry the working to at least three significant figures — here the numbers are exact at two — and round only at the end, stating to how many significant figures the answer is given.

### Worked example: a radio wave, with a prefix to convert

An FM station in Oshakati broadcasts at a frequency of 99.5 MHz, and radio waves travel at c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in free space. To find the wavelength, convert the prefix to base units before substituting: 99.5 MHz = 9.95 × 10⁷ Hz.

Equation: v = fλ, so λ = v ÷ f. Substitution: λ = 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 9.95 × 10⁷ = 3.02 m when carried to three significant figures. The wavelength is 3.0 m to two significant figures, matching the speed, which is the least precise value given.

The same discipline applies to any prefixed quantity: GHz becomes × 10⁹ hertz, ms becomes × 10⁻³ seconds, mm becomes × 10⁻³ metres. Convert first, then substitute; a prefix converted after the substitution is the single commonest source of a wrong power of ten.

### Energy carried by a wave

A progressive wave carries energy, not matter. The oscillating particles of the medium hold energy — kinetic energy while they move, and stored energy while the medium around them is strained — and each particle hands that energy to its neighbour as it drives it. The handing-on runs along the whole line, so energy flows away from the source even though each particle stays near its rest position.

A wave of larger amplitude gives each particle a bigger oscillation: more distance travelled, faster movement, and more energy stored at each extreme. More energy therefore passes any chosen point each second. That is why the sea can fling a boat onto a beach at Henties Bay while no water has travelled there from the storm that raised the swell: the energy arrives, and the water stays where it was, oscillating about its rest position.

### Intensity

Intensity measures how much energy a wave delivers to each unit of area facing it. The definition: intensity is power per unit area at right angles to the direction of travel, I = P ÷ A, with P the power in watts (W), A the area in m² and I the intensity in W m⁻².

For a source radiating equally in all directions, the power crosses the surface of a sphere centred on the source, A = 4πr², so the intensity falls with the square of the distance from the source.

Intensity is also tied to the amplitude of the wave: for a progressive wave, the intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude, I ∝ A². Doubling the amplitude gives four times the intensity; tripling it gives nine times. The reason is the energy each particle holds: doubling the amplitude doubles both how far and how fast each particle oscillates, and four times the energy is then handed along per cycle.

When a question changes the amplitude, square the amplitude ratio before applying it to the intensity. Treating intensity as if it were proportional to amplitude is the standard error: it reports the change as four times too small.

### Worked example: intensity from a loudspeaker

A public-address speaker at a school sports day in Windhoek radiates 0.75 W of sound power equally in all directions. Listeners stand 5.0 m from it. The power crosses the sphere A = 4πr² centred on the speaker.

With r = 5.0 m: 4 × 25 = 100, so A = 3.142 × 100 = 314.2 m², that is 314 m² to three significant figures.

Then I = P ÷ A, so I = 0.75 ÷ 314.2 = 0.00239 W m⁻², which is 2.4 × 10⁻³ W m⁻² to two significant figures.

If the amplifier is turned up until the amplitude of the sound doubles, the intensity rises by a factor of 2² = 4, to 9.6 × 10⁻³ W m⁻²: intensity follows the square of the amplitude.

### Displacement is not amplitude

Displacement and amplitude are two different quantities that share a unit. Displacement is where a point of the medium is now, measured from its rest position; it changes from moment to moment and passes through zero. Amplitude is the largest displacement the point reaches; for a steady wave it stays the same cycle after cycle. The test is to ask whether the quantity changes from moment to moment: if it does, it is a displacement; if it is fixed at the maximum, it is an amplitude. A reading of −4.0 mm is a displacement with a sign; an amplitude of 4.0 mm is a size and carries no sign.

## Transverse and longitudinal waves

### Transverse waves

In a transverse wave, the oscillations are at right angles to the direction of energy transfer. Shake one end of a rope up and down and the pulse runs along the rope while each part of the rope moves across the line of travel. All electromagnetic waves, from radio waves to γ-rays, are transverse. A transverse wave's oscillations could lie in any of the many planes across the line of travel, and that freedom makes polarisation possible, as section 7.5 shows.

### Longitudinal waves

In a longitudinal wave, the oscillations are along the direction of energy transfer. Sound in air is the everyday example: a speaker cone moving outwards squeezes the air next to it and a compression runs outwards; as the cone moves back it leaves that air spread thin, and a rarefaction follows. Each particle shuttles to and fro about its rest position along the line the sound travels, and neighbouring compressions sit one wavelength apart. Pushing a stretched slinky endwise shows the same thing.

### The comparison in one table

Both kinds transfer energy while the medium oscillates about fixed rest positions; they differ in the direction of those oscillations, which decides whether the wave can be polarised.

| Property | Transverse wave | Longitudinal wave |
|---|---|---|
| Direction of oscillations | At right angles to the direction of energy transfer | Along the direction of energy transfer |
| Examples | Rope waves, water surface waves, all electromagnetic waves | Sound in air, compression pulses on a spring |
| Can it be polarised? | Yes | No |
| What passes a point | A crest, then a trough, then a crest | A compression, then a rarefaction, then a compression |

### Displacement–distance and displacement–time graphs

Two graphs carry most of the information about a wave. In both, displacement keeps its wave sense: the distance of a point from its rest position, positive on one side and negative on the other.

A displacement–distance graph is a snapshot of the whole wave at one instant: the horizontal axis is distance from the source, and the curve shows the displacement of every point at that moment. The wavelength is the horizontal distance between adjacent crests.

A displacement–time graph follows one single point of the medium: the horizontal axis is time, and the curve shows that point oscillating. The period is the time between adjacent crests, and the frequency follows from f = 1 ÷ T.

Both graphs show the amplitude as the maximum height of the curve; the axis labels decide what can be read: distance from the source gives wavelengths, time gives periods.

### Reading wave graphs

Read four things off any wave graph. The amplitude: the maximum displacement, from the rest position to a crest — a crest-to-trough reading is twice the amplitude. The wavelength, on a displacement–distance graph: the crest-to-crest distance. The period, on a displacement–time graph: the crest-to-crest time. And the phase of two points: points a whole number of wavelengths apart oscillate in phase, and points an odd number of half-wavelengths apart oscillate in antiphase.

For a longitudinal wave the displacement–distance graph plots displacement along the direction of travel, and the curve crosses the rest position at the compressions, where the particles on either side are displaced towards each other.

| Distance from source / m | Displacement / mm |
|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.0 |
| 0.15 | +2.4 |
| 0.30 | 0.0 |
| 0.45 | −2.4 |
| 0.60 | 0.0 |

The zero crossings at 0.30 m and 0.60 m mark the compressions, one wavelength apart: the wavelength is 0.30 m and the amplitude 2.4 mm.

### Worked example: from a graph to a frequency

The displacement of one point of a string is recorded against time as a wave passes. Trace crests occur at 0.10 s and 0.34 s, and the trace reaches 5.0 mm.

Time is on the horizontal axis, so the crest-to-crest reading is a period: T = 0.34 − 0.10 = 0.24 s. The frequency follows: f = 1 ÷ T, so 1 ÷ 0.24 = 4.167, and f = 4.17 Hz, that is 4.2 Hz to two significant figures. The amplitude is 5.0 mm.

Had the same numbers appeared on a displacement–distance graph, the 0.24 m crest separation would have been a wavelength — the reading is decided by the axis, not by the shape of the curve.

### Sound is not transverse

Sound in air is longitudinal, and treating it as transverse — or expecting it to be polarised — is a known error. The test is the direction of the oscillations: air particles shuttle along the line the sound travels, so there is no plane for a filter to select. Light oscillates across its line of travel, so light can be polarised; sound cannot.

## The Doppler effect for a moving source

### The observation

When a source of sound waves moves relative to a stationary observer, the frequency the observer measures is different from the frequency the source emits: higher than the source frequency while the source approaches, and lower while it recedes. Stand beside a main road and the engine note of a passing car is high on the approach, drops as it passes, and stays lower as the car moves away. The sound still travels through the same air at the same speed; it is the frequency measured at the stationary observer that changes. The source frequency itself does not change — the source oscillates at the same rate throughout.

### Why the pitch changes

The reason lies in the spacing of the wavefronts. A source emits a wavefront at each oscillation, and each wavefront then travels outwards through the medium at the wave speed v, which the medium sets. When the source moves, it emits each wavefront from a new position.

Ahead of the source, each wavefront starts closer to the one before it than it would have from a stationary source, so the wavefronts crowd together: the wavelength in front is shortened. Behind the source, each wavefront starts further from the one before it, so the wavefronts are stretched: the wavelength behind is longer.

The speed of the sound in the air is unchanged, so by the wave equation v = fλ the shorter wavelength in front gives a higher observed frequency and the longer wavelength behind gives a lower one. The pitch heard at the stationary observer rises on the approach and falls on the recede, which is the Doppler effect for a moving source.

### The moving-source expression

For a source moving at speed vs, in m s⁻¹, relative to the air, the observed frequency at a stationary observer is

fo = fs v ÷ (v ± vs)

where fo is the observed frequency and fs the source frequency, both in hertz (Hz), and v is the speed of the sound waves in the medium, in m s⁻¹.

The sign is chosen by the direction of the motion. The minus sign, v − vs, applies when the source moves towards the observer: the denominator is then smaller than v, and the observed frequency comes out above fs. The plus sign, v + vs, applies when the source moves away: the denominator is larger than v, and the observed frequency comes out below fs.

One way to keep the sign straight: the observed frequency must rise as a source approaches, so the denominator must shrink, and the minus sign does the shrinking.

### Worked example: an approaching ambulance

An ambulance travels towards a stationary nurse standing beside the B1 at 30 m s⁻¹, its siren emitting a steady 800 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m s⁻¹.

The source approaches, so the minus sign applies: fo = fs v ÷ (v − vs).

Substitution: fo = 800 × 340 ÷ (340 − 30). The bracket: 340 − 30 = 310. The top line: 800 × 340 = 272 000. The division: 272 000 ÷ 310 = 877.4.

The nurse hears 877 Hz, that is 880 Hz to two significant figures — a rise of 77 Hz above the source frequency.

Once the ambulance has passed and is receding, the plus sign applies: fo = 800 × 340 ÷ (340 + 30), and 272 000 ÷ 370 = 735.1, so the nurse hears 735 Hz, that is 740 Hz to two significant figures — a fall of 65 Hz below the source frequency. The rise on the approach and the fall on the recede are symmetric about the source frequency only when the speeds are equal, as they are here.

### The sign and the frequencies

Two slips account for most of the errors with the moving-source expression. The first is the sign: writing v + vs for an approaching source. The observed pitch rises as a source approaches, and v + vs in the denominator would make the observed frequency fall, so the sign contradicts the physics. The test is whether the source is approaching — the pitch rises, so the minus sign — or receding — the pitch falls, so the plus sign.

The second is swapping the two frequencies: fs is what the source emits and fo is what the observer measures. Keep the physical check with any answer: an approaching source must give fo above fs, and a receding source must give fo below fs.

### Where this section leads

The Doppler effect rests directly on the wave equation v = fλ from section 7.1: the medium fixes v, the motion of the source fixes the wavelength in front of and behind it, and the observed frequency follows. ## The electromagnetic spectrum

### Electromagnetic waves

Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves that need no medium: they cross free space — a vacuum — at the same speed, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, whatever their frequency. The whole family, from the longest radio wave to the shortest γ-ray, shares that speed and that transverse nature.

The regions of the spectrum differ from one another in wavelength, and so in frequency, since the wave equation v = fλ holds with v fixed at c in free space. They do not differ in speed: a γ-ray and a radio wave cross the same vacuum at the same rate, one with a wavelength of a thousandth of a nanometre, the other with a wavelength of a hundred metres. Because v is fixed, the order of the regions by wavelength is the exact reverse of their order by frequency: γ-rays carry the highest frequencies, radio waves the lowest.

In a material such as glass or water the speed sits a little below c, but the value to use in any free-space calculation in this course is c itself, given on the data sheet as 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

### The regions of the spectrum

The principal regions, in order of increasing wavelength, are γ-rays, X-rays, ultraviolet, visible, infrared, microwaves and radio waves. The boundaries are approximate; the ranges below are the ones to recall, and the wavelengths are free-space values.

| Region | Approximate wavelength in free space |
|---|---|
| γ-rays | shorter than 10⁻¹⁰ m |
| X-rays | 10⁻¹⁰ m to 10⁻⁸ m |
| Ultraviolet | 10⁻⁸ m to 4 × 10⁻⁷ m |
| Visible | 4 × 10⁻⁷ m to 7 × 10⁻⁷ m (400 nm to 700 nm) |
| Infrared | 7 × 10⁻⁷ m to 10⁻³ m |
| Microwaves | 10⁻³ m to 10⁻¹ m |
| Radio waves | longer than 10⁻¹ m |

In Namibian terms: the microwaves that carry phone and data links between towers sit near centimetres, the radio waves of a Windhoek FM station sit near metres, and the infrared that Kalahari sand gives out as it cools in the evening sits near 10⁻⁵ m. The sun delivers the whole band at once: the visible glare, the infrared you feel as heat on your skin, and the ultraviolet that burns at the coast even under light cloud.

### Worked example: the frequency of a microwave link

A microwave link between two phone towers near Gobabis uses waves of wavelength 6.0 cm. In free space, v = c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

Convert the prefix first: 6.0 cm = 6.0 × 10⁻² m.

Equation: v = fλ, so f = v ÷ λ. Substitution: f = 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ (6.0 × 10⁻²). The numbers: 3.0 ÷ 6.0 = 0.50, and the powers of ten give 10⁸ ÷ 10⁻² = 10¹⁰, so f = 0.50 × 10¹⁰ = 5.0 × 10⁹.

The frequency is 5.0 × 10⁹ Hz, that is 5.0 GHz, to two significant figures. A microwave and a radio wave of the same wavelength would have the same frequency — the region names sort wavelengths, and the wave equation converts between the two descriptions.

### The visible band

Wavelengths in the range 400 nm to 700 nm in free space are visible to the human eye. In metres that is 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ m to 7.0 × 10⁻⁷ m, a band about 3 × 10⁻⁷ m wide — a narrow slice of the whole spectrum. At 400 nm the light appears violet; at 700 nm it appears red, and the familiar colours sit in order between them, from violet through blue, green and yellow to red.

The two neighbouring regions close in on this band from either side: ultraviolet runs from 10⁻⁸ m up to 4 × 10⁻⁷ m, and infrared starts at 7 × 10⁻⁷ m. So the shortest visible wavelength marks the boundary with ultraviolet, and the longest marks the boundary with infrared — the band the eye responds to sits exactly between the two, and neither ultraviolet nor infrared is visible, however intense: the eye is not a detector for them.

### Which region a wavelength belongs to

Naming the wrong region for a given wavelength is a known error, and the cure is the order of magnitude. A wavelength of 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ m is of order 10⁻⁷ m: visible, near the red end, and not an X-ray, whose range is 10⁻¹⁰ m to 10⁻⁸ m. Write the wavelength as a power of ten in metres, then walk the table from γ-rays upwards until the power falls inside a range.

The same habit answers the reverse question: a wave described as ultraviolet has a wavelength of order 10⁻⁸ m to 4 × 10⁻⁷ m, and a wave described as microwave has a wavelength of order 10⁻³ m to 10⁻¹ m. The region names are shorthand for the powers of ten, nothing more.

The speed c met here is the constant those calculations use, and the intensity of section 7.1 describes the energy the visible band carries.

## Polarisation

### Polarisation

Polarisation is a phenomenon associated with transverse waves. A transverse wave oscillates across its line of travel, and those oscillations could lie in any of the planes through that line. A wave whose oscillations are confined to one plane only is plane-polarised, and polarising a wave means restricting its oscillations to that single plane.

A polarising filter does this for light: it transmits the oscillations along its transmission axis and blocks those at right angles to that axis. Ordinary lamp light oscillates in every plane perpendicular to its direction of travel. Light reflected from a wet road or from the surface of a river, and light scattered by the daytime sky, arrives partly plane-polarised — which is why polarising sunglasses cut the glare off the water of the Okavango in a way an ordinary dark lens cannot: they hold a filter at a fixed orientation that blocks the planes the glare arrives in.

### Why a longitudinal wave cannot be polarised

Polarisation selects one plane of oscillation from the many a transverse wave offers. A rope wave can be made to oscillate vertically, or horizontally, or anywhere between, and a slot that passes one orientation blocks the others — the classic classroom demonstration. A longitudinal wave offers no such choice: its oscillations already lie along the one line of travel, and there is no second direction for a filter to remove.

So sound in air cannot be polarised, and the fact that light can be polarised is direct evidence that light is transverse. The test, applied to any wave, is: are the oscillations along or across the direction the wave travels? Across — polarisation is possible. Along — it is not.

### Malus's law

When a plane-polarised wave of intensity I0, in W m⁻², meets a polarising filter whose transmission axis makes an angle θ with the plane of polarisation, the transmitted intensity is

I = I0 cos²θ.

The cosine is squared, and θ is the angle between the plane of polarisation and the transmission axis of the filter. Two limiting cases are worth carrying. At θ = 0° the axis is parallel to the polarisation plane, cos 0° = 1, and the wave passes with its intensity unchanged. At θ = 90° the axis is at right angles to the polarisation plane, cos 90° = 0, and the transmitted intensity is zero — the filter blocks the wave completely.

For a series of filters, apply the law once per filter: the intensity leaving the first filter becomes I0 for the second, with θ measured between the new pair of directions.

### Worked example: two filters on polarised light

Plane-polarised light of intensity 60 W m⁻² meets a filter whose transmission axis is at 30° to the plane of polarisation. The light that leaves it then meets a second filter at 45° to the first.

First filter: I1 = I0 cos²30°. cos 30° = 0.8660, so cos²30° = 0.8660 × 0.8660 = 0.750, and I1 = 60 × 0.750 = 45.0, that is 45 W m⁻² to two significant figures.

Second filter: I2 = I1 cos²45°, with the 45° measured between the axis of the second filter and the polarisation plane the first filter set. cos 45° = 0.7071, so cos²45° = 0.7071 × 0.7071 = 0.500, and I2 = 45.0 × 0.500 = 22.5.

The final intensity is 22.5 W m⁻², that is 23 W m⁻² to two significant figures. Each application of the law squares its own cosine.

### Square the cosine

The standard slip with Malus's law is to drop the square, using I = I0 cos θ instead of I = I0 cos²θ. At 60°, cos θ = 0.5 but cos²θ = 0.25, so the unsquared law reports twice the true transmitted intensity: an incident 10 W m⁻² would be reported as 5.0 W m⁻² instead of the correct 2.5 W m⁻².

The second slip is the angle. In I = I0 cos²θ, θ is measured between the plane of polarisation and the transmission axis of the filter, not between the axes of two filters and not from any other reference direction. Before substituting, ask two questions: has the cosine been squared, and which two directions does θ separate?
