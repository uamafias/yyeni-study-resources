## Energy conservation

### Work done

Work done by a force is the force multiplied by the displacement in the direction of the force: W = Fs, where W is the work done in joules (J), F the force in newtons (N) and s the displacement in metres (m) measured in the direction of the force. One joule is the work done when a force of one newton moves its point of application one metre in the direction of the force, so 1 J = 1 N m.
Work done and energy transferred are two names for the same quantity: the work a force does is the energy that force transfers to whatever it acts on, and both are measured in joules. That is why the topic of work belongs with the topic of energy.
The direction condition is what the definition turns on. A waiter carrying a tray of drinks at a steady height exerts an upward force on the tray, but the tray moves horizontally: the displacement in the direction of the force is zero, and the work done by that force is zero, however long the shift lasts. Pushing a stalled minibus along the road, with the push along the road, does work on it. When a displacement makes an angle with the force, take the part of the displacement that lies in the direction of the force; a force at right angles to the motion does no work at all.

### The principle of conservation of energy

The principle of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can be converted from one form to another. For a closed system — one where no energy enters or leaves — the total energy, in all its forms, stays constant. When one form decreases, another increases by the same amount.
Every physics calculation in this topic is a piece of that bookkeeping. When a mango falls from a tree, gravitational potential energy becomes kinetic energy. When a car brakes on gravel, kinetic energy becomes thermal energy in the brake pads, the tyres and the stones. When a torch shines, energy stored in its cells becomes light and warmth. In each change the energy has not vanished; it has moved between forms, and the totals still agree.
Where a change loses no energy to friction or to drag, the form that decreases gives up exactly what the form that gains receives. A falling stone turns height into speed: the height it loses sets the energy released, and the energy reappears as movement. Energy accounts are kept in joules, not in speeds or distances on their own, and a complete account is how a physics answer shows its working.

### Worked example: pulling a trailer of firewood

At a settlement outside Okahao, Kavii hauls a trailer of firewood 8.0 m along level ground, pulling with a steady force of 65 N along the direction of travel.
Equation: W = Fs.
Substitution: W = 65 × 8.0 = 520 J, so the work done is 520 J, two significant figures like the data.
The displacement lies along the force, so the whole 8.0 m counts. Two contrasts show the direction rule. Pulling the same trailer up a loading ramp that makes an angle with the ground, with the pull still acting along the ramp, s is the distance measured along the ramp: the force and the displacement still share a direction, and the height of the ramp is not needed for W. And the normal contact force from the ramp, which acts at right angles to the motion, does no work at all, because the displacement in its direction is zero.

### Efficiency

The efficiency of a system is the ratio of the useful energy output from the system to the total energy input: efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input. Both energies are measured in joules, so the ratio has no unit; it is a fraction, often written as a percentage by multiplying by 100.
Because part of the input ends up in forms that are not useful — a motor warms the room it stands in, a pump churns the water it lifts — the ratio is smaller than 1 for any real machine, and a machine reaching exactly 1 would be one with nothing left over.
A borehole pump that lifts water worth 7200 J of energy for 9000 J of electrical input has efficiency = 7200 ÷ 9000 = 0.80, which is 80%. The difference between input and useful output, 1800 J here, is the energy converted to forms that are not useful. Naming where that energy goes — warmth in the motor's windings, sound, churning in the pipe — completes the account and explains the figure.

### Using efficiency in problems

Efficiency problems usually run in one of two directions. Given the input and the useful output, divide: efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input, both in joules, or the same ratio with powers in watts. Given the efficiency and one of the energies, reconstruct the other: useful energy output = efficiency × total energy input, and total energy input = useful energy output ÷ efficiency. The same relations hold for power, provided the input power and the useful output power are measured over the same time.
Keep three checks in mind. Put the useful quantity on top: the input is the larger of the two energies, because it has to cover the useful output and the waste together, so an efficiency above 1 means the ratio is upside down. Carry the unit of each energy, converting kilojoules to joules before substituting. And say where the difference goes: the energy converted to forms that are not useful is the total input minus the useful output, which is what a single efficiency figure hides.

### Keeping the account complete

An energy account earns its keep by leaving nothing out. Take the pump from the efficiency section: 9000 J of electrical energy goes in, 7200 J of energy in the raised water comes out, and 1800 J is the difference. The account says where it went: warmth in the motor's windings and bearings, a little sound, and churning in the pipe. Every form is named, every line carries its unit, and the two sides agree.
Where a stage is missing from an account, the totals disagree, and the missing form shows itself by the size of the gap. A car braking from 20 m s⁻¹ that seems to lose more kinetic energy than the brakes take in as warmth has sent the rest somewhere: to the tyres' grip, to the road's surface, to the air pushed aside. The conservation principle is the check that finds the missing line, and stating the forms, in joules, is how the check is applied. Energy is not created by the account, and none is destroyed by leaving a line out.

### Power

Power is the work done per unit time: P = W/t, where P is the power in watts (W), W the work done in joules (J) and t the time in seconds (s). One watt is one joule per second, written 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹, and a kilowatt is 1000 W.
The definition ties an energy to a time. The same 900 J of work done in 3.0 s is a power of 300 W; the same work spread over an hour of steady transfer is a power of 0.25 W. Nothing about the energy itself has changed — what differs is how quickly it is transferred, and that is what a power rating states. A borehole pump's label states the rate at which it converts electrical energy into work on the water, because a rate is what a pump can be counted on to keep up. Power is a scalar, like the work it is measured from, and it is not the same quantity as energy: dividing by time is the step that turns one into the other.

### Solving problems with P = W/t

The equation P = W/t rearranges into two further tools: W = Pt, the energy transferred by a device running for a time t, and t = W/P, the time a device needs to transfer a stated energy. Each problem starts by asking which of the three quantities is missing.
A borehole pump with a useful output of 240 W that runs for 25 s transfers W = Pt = 240 × 25 = 6000 J to the water. A lift motor that must do 90 000 J of work at 6000 W takes t = 90 000 ÷ 6000 = 15 s. A 12 W car radio playing for 5.0 minutes runs for 300 s and draws 3600 J. Times go in seconds before substituting, kilowatts become watts first, and each answer carries its unit: watts for a power, joules for an energy, seconds for a time.

### Deriving P = Fv

P = Fv is not a new law; it is the definition of power applied to motion at a steady velocity. Start with the definition: power is work done per unit time, P = W/t. Substitute the work relation W = Fs, which holds for a constant force F with the displacement s in the direction of the force: P = Fs/t. The quotient s/t is the velocity v in the direction of the force, so P = F × (s/t) = Fv.
Three conditions ride with the result. The force is constant over the time considered. The velocity is measured in the direction of the force — for a vehicle pushed along its line of travel, that is its speed. And the power in question is the rate of working of that particular force, which for a minibus at a steady speed on level ground is the engine's delivery at the wheels.
Rearranged, F = P/v: at a fixed engine power the driving force falls as the speed rises, which is why a taxi that manages the B1's open stretches labours up a gravel pass in a low gear. The same relation read the other way, v = P/F, says how fast a given power can push against a given force.

### Worked example: the driving force of a minibus taxi

A minibus taxi travels along a level stretch of the B1 at a steady speed of 25 m s⁻¹, with its engine delivering power to the wheels at a rate of 42 kW.
Equation: P = Fv, so F = P/v.
Convert: 42 kW = 42 000 W.
Substitution: F = 42 000 ÷ 25 = 1680 N, so the driving force is 1680 N, three significant figures, matched by the drag and rolling resistance that together act on it at this speed.
At 15 m s⁻¹ in a lower gear, the same engine power gives F = 42 000 ÷ 15 = 2800 N, more than half as much again: the fall in speed buys a rise in force. Working each quotient at full precision and rounding at the end keeps the two figures comparable.

### Work done is not force × any distance

Two slips appear wherever work is calculated. The first is multiplying a force by a displacement that does not lie in its direction — the tension in a tow rope at an angle to the motion, multiplied by the distance travelled. Work done is the force × the displacement in the direction of that force, and the test is: is the displacement along the line of the force?
The second slip is using the resultant force when the question names one force and asks for the work that force does. Each force's work is computed with its own magnitude and its own direction; the resultant's work is a different quantity, the net energy transferred to kinetic energy. Where the two happen to agree, that is a special case, not the rule.

## Gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy

### Deriving ∆EP = mg∆h from W = Fs

Gravitational potential energy is the energy a mass holds because of where it sits in a gravitational field. To raise a mass m slowly through a vertical height ∆h, the lifting force must equal the weight, F = mg, where g is the gravitational field strength in N kg⁻¹. The displacement in the direction of this force is the vertical height ∆h. Substituting into the work relation W = Fs gives W = mg × ∆h, and because the lift is at a steady speed, with nothing left over, the work done is stored: the change in gravitational potential energy is ∆EP = mg∆h.
Two conditions carry the result. The field must be uniform, so that g keeps the same value over the whole lift — true for the heights a truck or a hiker manages near the surface of the Earth, where g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. And the lift must be at a steady speed: lift faster, and the work done also creates kinetic energy, overstating the stored amount.
Gravitational potential energy rises as the mass rises and falls as it falls. What enters the formula is the change in height, in metres, not the level the height is measured from: a 25 kg drum raised 3.6 m gains the same amount from the ground as from a first-floor balcony.

### Using ∆EP = mg∆h

The formula is used in three moves. Write the change: ∆EP = mg∆h, with ∆EP in joules, m in kilograms, g in N kg⁻¹ and ∆h the vertical change in height in metres. Check that ∆h is vertical: on a ramp, the vertical change is the distance along the ramp multiplied by sin θ, where θ is the angle the ramp makes with the horizontal — the distance along the slope on its own is not a height. Watch the sign: a rise is a gain, a fall a loss of the same size.
A 15 kg bale of wool raised 4.0 m into a loft gains mg∆h: the weight is 15 × 9.81 = 147.15 N, and 147.15 × 4.0 = 588.6 J, so the bale gains 588.6 J, about 590 J. A crate sliding down a ramp loses gravitational potential energy set by the vertical drop alone; the distance it travels to get there does not change the loss.

### Worked example: a water tank at Ondangwa

A school in Ondangwa fills its roof tank by pumping 250 kg of water up a pipe to a height of 5.5 m above the pump.
Equation: ∆EP = mg∆h.
Substitution: the water's weight is 250 × 9.81 = 2452.5 N, and 2452.5 × 5.5 = 13 488.75 J.
So the water gains 13 488.75 J, which is 1.3 × 10⁴ J to two significant figures.
Suppose the water instead arrives through a channel that climbs at an angle: the vertical rise of 5.5 m is what enters the formula, whatever the length of the channel. On a delivery ramp inclined at 30° to the horizontal, a cart pushed 6.0 m along the ramp rises 6.0 × sin 30° = 3.0 m, and the energy gained depends on those 3.0 m, not on the 6.0 m.

### Deriving EK = ½mv² from the equations of motion

Kinetic energy is the energy a moving mass holds. Its formula comes from asking how much work a constant force must do to bring a mass m, moving at speed v, to rest.
Start with the equations of motion: with the final speed zero, v² = u² + 2as gives 0 = v² − 2as, where u is the initial speed v and a is the acceleration, so the stopping distance is s = v² ÷ (2a). Next bring in the force: F = ma. Substitute both into the work relation: W = Fs = ma × v² ÷ (2a). The acceleration cancels, leaving W = ½mv². The work done stopping the object is the energy it held while moving, so EK = ½mv².
Every step is a substitution; no new law appears. The result is the work the object can do as it is brought to rest, which is what makes the energy kinetic: a hammer drives in a nail because it arrives with ½mv² of work to give. The derivation needs the braking force constant and acting until the object stops, so that all the energy is spent; the distance the force acts over matters, and the time it takes does not enter at all.

### Using EK = ½mv²

EK = ½mv², with EK in joules, m the mass in kilograms and v the speed in metres per second. The speed is squared before the rest of the substitution, and that square is where the errors live. A 1500 kg minibus at 25 m s⁻¹ carries EK = ½mv²: half of 1500 is 750, and 750 × 625 = 468 750 J, near 4.7 × 10⁵ J — where 625 is 25², and a slip to 25 instead would leave the energy a factor of 25 too small.
Doubling the speed quadruples the energy, because v enters squared; doubling the mass only doubles it. Kinetic energy is a scalar, computed from the speed alone, and no direction enters the formula.
The energy of a falling object ties the two formulas of this sub-topic together: where drag is left out, the gain in kinetic energy equals the loss in gravitational potential energy, mg∆h = ½mv², and the mass cancels to leave v² = 2g∆h.

### Worked example: a barrel rolled down a harbour ramp

At Walvis Bay, a barrel of fish of mass 60 kg is released at the top of a loading ramp and rolls down, losing 2.8 m of height, with rolling losses small enough to leave out.
Equation: gain in kinetic energy = loss in gravitational potential energy, ½mv² = mg∆h.
Substitution: the barrel's weight is 60 × 9.81 = 588.6 N, and 588.6 × 2.8 = 1648.08 J, so the barrel arrives with 1648.08 J of kinetic energy, about 1600 J.
Its speed follows from EK = ½mv². The short route cancels the mass early: v² = 2g∆h. Here g∆h = 9.81 × 2.8 = 27.468, and 2 × 27.468 = 54.936, so v = √54.936 = 7.41 m s⁻¹ to three significant figures, 7.4 m s⁻¹ to two. The long route gives the same: v² = 2 × 1648.08 ÷ 60, and the mass drops out either way.

### ∆h is the vertical change

The commonest error with ∆EP = mg∆h is feeding it a distance that is not a vertical change in height. A crate sliding 3.0 m down a ramp at 25° to the horizontal drops 3.0 × sin 25° = 1.27 m, not 3.0 m; using the full 3.0 m overstates the loss by more than double. The second error is a sign: a fall is a loss of gravitational potential energy and a rise is a gain, so the change in height must be handled with its direction. The test in both cases: is the height vertical, and is it a change?

### Square the speed before you multiply

In EK = ½mv² the square is a step in its own right. A learner who substitutes 20 in place of v² when v = 20 m s⁻¹ writes an energy a factor of 20 too small: v = 20 m s⁻¹ means v² = 400 m² s⁻². Write the square as its own line before multiplying — first v², then ½m, then the product. Doubling a speed, read through the formula, multiplies the kinetic energy by four, which is a quick check that the square is doing its work.
