## Detecting the nuclear atom

### Antiparticles

An antiparticle has the same mass as the corresponding particle but the opposite charge. The positron is the antiparticle of the electron: same mass, about 0.00055 u, and charge +e where the electron's is −e. The antiproton carries −e where the proton carries +e, with the proton's mass; the antineutron carries no charge, like the neutron. The rule is one of pairs: for a charged particle there is an antiparticle of identical mass and opposite charge, and the pair that matters in this topic is the electron and the positron, because β⁺-decay emits the positron. Wherever a particle appears in this topic, ask whether its antiparticle is what the equation really means - the antineutrino of β⁻-decay and the neutrino of β⁺-decay are the pair you will use most.

### Neutrinos in β-decay

β-decay does not emit a β-particle alone. A β⁻-decay emits an electron together with an (electron) antineutrino, written ν̄; a β⁺-decay emits a positron together with an (electron) neutrino, written ν. The neutrino and the antineutrino carry no charge and a very small mass, and in a decay equation each counts as nucleon number 0 and charge 0, so their presence balances the books without changing the nuclei's A or Z totals. The pairing is easy to reverse by accident, and reversing it is the classic slip: β⁻ goes with the antineutrino and β⁺ with the neutrino, and the antineutrino is the one with the bar over its symbol. Both are leptons, fundamental particles like the electron, and neither is made of quarks.

### Why α energies are sharp and β energies are spread

α-particles from a given nuclide emerge with discrete energies - one or a few sharply defined values - because the decay passes between two well-defined nuclear states, and the α-particle carries away a fixed share of the energy every time, like a stair that always drops you the same distance. β-particles behave differently: they emerge with a continuous range of energies up to a maximum, and the reason is the (anti)neutrino. The decay energy available is fixed, but it is shared at random between the emitted β-particle and the (anti)neutrino: when the β-particle takes more of the energy, the (anti)neutrino takes less, and every split between the two is possible. A β-particle that took everything would arrive at the maximum energy, and one that took almost nothing would arrive with almost none; between the two lies every value. That is why no single energy characterises the β-particles from a nuclide. It is also why the neutrino had to exist before anyone had detected it: without a second particle to carry the missing share, the energy books of β-decay would not balance, and a fixed share for the β-particle alone contradicts what the measurements show.

### Conservation in nuclear processes

Two quantities are conserved in every nuclear process, and they are the two books a decay equation must balance. The total nucleon number A is conserved: the sum of the nucleon numbers before equals the sum after. The total charge is conserved: the sum of the proton numbers, with the charges of any emitted particles counted, before equals the sum after. For γ-emission nothing changes in the books at all, because γ-radiation carries nucleon number 0 and charge 0; the nucleus simply settles into a state of lower energy. For α- and β-decay the books are what let you predict the daughter: you know what the emitted particle carries away, so you know what must remain.

### Writing decay equations

An α-decay lowers the nucleon number by 4 and the proton number by 2, written ᴬ₍Z₎X → ᴬ⁻⁴₍Z−2₎Y + ⁴₂α, because the α-particle carries away 4 nucleons and 2 protons. A β⁻-decay raises the proton number by 1 and leaves the nucleon number unchanged, written ᴬ₍Z₎X → ᴬ₍Z+1₎Y + ⁰₋₁β + ν̄, because a neutron becomes a proton and the emitted electron and antineutrino carry away the balancing charge and energy. A β⁺-decay is the mirror image, written ᴬ₍Z₎X → ᴬ₍Z−1₎Y + ⁰₊₁β + ν: the proton number falls by 1, the nucleon number stays, and the emitted positron carries +1e with the neutrino. Test any equation the same way whatever the decay: add the nucleon numbers across the arrow and add the charges across the arrow; each total must be the same before and after, and the daughter element's symbol must match the new proton number. An equation that fails either book is wrong, whatever it looks like.

### Worked example: the uranium-238 decay

Uranium-238 decays by α-emission. Start from the notation: ²³⁸₉₂U → ? + ⁴₂α. The α-particle removes 4 nucleons and 2 protons, so the daughter has A = 238 − 4 = 234 and Z = 92 − 2 = 90. The element with Z = 90 is thorium, symbol Th, so the balanced equation is ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂α. Check both books: nucleon numbers 238 = 234 + 4, and charges 92 = 90 + 2. Both balance, so the equation stands. Now follow the daughter. Thorium-234 decays by β⁻-emission: a β⁻-decay leaves A at 234 and raises Z by 1, to 91, which is protactinium, so ²³⁴₉₀Th → ²³⁴₉₁Pa + ⁰₋₁β + ν̄. Check the books again: nucleon numbers 234 = 234 + 0, and charges 90 = 91 − 1, with the antineutrino carrying no charge and no nucleons. Two decays, two checks, and the daughter each time is read off the conservation rules rather than remembered.

### The unified atomic mass unit

The unified atomic mass unit (u) is defined as one twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12, and 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. A nucleus's mass in u is close to its nucleon number A, not its proton number, because each nucleon has a mass of about 1 u while each proton contributes one unit to both numbers - it is A that counts the heavy constituents, protons and neutrons together. To convert a mass in u to kg, multiply by 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷; to convert a mass in kg to u, divide by 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷. The α-particle's mass of about 4 u becomes 4 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, which shows why u is the convenient unit for nuclear masses: the numbers stay small enough to compare at a glance, and a mass in u tells you roughly how many nucleons are present.

### Nucleon number is not neutron number

A recurring slip is to read A as the neutron count, or to give a nucleus the proton number as its mass in u. The nucleon number counts protons and neutrons together; the neutron number is A − Z. And a nucleus's mass in u is close to A, not Z, because the protons are part of what A counts. The test that tells them apart is one question: does the number include the protons? If it does, it is the nucleon number; if it counts neutrons alone, it is the neutron number, and it never appears in the nuclide symbol.

### The experiment that found the nucleus

Early in the twentieth century a gold foil only a few thousand atoms thick was bathed in a narrow beam of α-particles, and a movable detector counted the α-particles arriving at each angle to the incoming beam. The expectation of the day was wrong, and the results said so plainly. The expectation was that an atom's positive charge spread thinly over its whole volume, the way plum pudding spreads its fruit; spread that thin, the charge could never push a heavy, fast α-particle far off course, and every α-particle should have passed nearly straight through. What the detector found instead was a story in two parts, and each part carries its own inference. Most α-particles passed straight through the foil with no measurable deflection, and that shows most of the atom is empty space. But about one α-particle in several thousand came back through a large angle, and a very small number bounced back almost the way they came. A deflection that large needs a strong repulsive push concentrated in a small place, so the atom must hold a nucleus that is tiny compared with the atom, carries most of the mass, and is positively charged. Match each observation to its inference and the picture draws itself: empty passage proves the atom is mostly empty; large-angle scattering proves a small, dense, positive nucleus.

### Why so few α-particles scatter

The scattering is rare for a reason you can count. An atom is about 10⁻¹⁰ m across, while the nucleus inside it is about 10⁻¹⁴ m across, so the nucleus is about ten thousand times smaller than the atom in each direction - and about one part in 10¹² of its volume. An α-particle is deflected through a large angle only when its path passes close to a nucleus, because only there is the repulsive push strong enough to turn it. Since the nuclei occupy so little of the foil's area, only a small fraction of the α-particles pass close enough to be turned through a large angle; the rest of the beam sees nothing but empty space and passes through undeviated. This is why the experiment needed a thin foil and a beam of many millions of α-particles: large-angle scattering is a rare event, even though it is the observation that proves the nucleus exists. The fraction bouncing back is smaller still - those α-particles met a nucleus almost head-on, which almost never happens when the target is that small.

### The nuclear atom

The model the scattering forced on physics is the nuclear atom. A central nucleus holds all of the atom's protons and neutrons, and so carries all of the positive charge and nearly all of the mass; the electrons orbit the nucleus, and a neutral atom holds as many orbital electrons as there are protons in its nucleus, because the proton's charge and the electron's charge are equal in size and opposite in sign. The nucleus is where nearly all the mass sits because each nucleon is about 1840 times heavier than an electron. Everything in the rest of this topic is bookkeeping on this one picture: counting the protons and neutrons in the nucleus, writing the counts in a standard notation, and tracking what happens to them when a nucleus decays.

### Nucleon number and proton number

The proton number Z is the number of protons in a nucleus. The nucleon number A is the number of protons plus neutrons in it. The two numbers answer different questions: the proton number fixes which element the atom is and equals the number of orbital electrons in a neutral atom, while the nucleon number fixes how massive the nucleus is, which is why it is also called the mass number. The neutron number N is not written in the symbol at all: it is found from N = A − Z. So a nucleus with A = 14 and Z = 7 holds 14 − 7 = 7 neutrons alongside its 7 protons. Say it carefully and the confusion does not arise: the nucleon number counts every nucleon, protons and neutrons together, which is what makes it the mass number; the neutron count and the proton count are the other two quantities, and each is a different number.

### Worked example: counting the constituents

A learner in Otjiwarongo writes a nuclide as ⁵⁶₂₆Fe and asks what the nucleus holds. Read the numbers first: Z = 26 and A = 56. The protons number 26, so a neutral iron atom also holds 26 orbital electrons. The neutrons come from the subtraction N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30. So this iron nucleus holds 26 protons and 30 neutrons, 56 nucleons in all, and the atom as a whole holds 26 electrons in orbit. The same two steps - read Z for the protons and the electrons, subtract for the neutrons - work for any nuclide written in the standard notation, whatever the element.

### Isotopes

Isotopes are nuclei of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, so they share a proton number and differ in nucleon number. Because the proton number is the same, a neutral atom of each isotope holds the same number of orbital electrons, and the atoms take part in the same chemical reactions: carbon-12 and carbon-14 behave identically in a chemistry laboratory, even though carbon-14 holds two more neutrons. Take the uranium pair as the standard example. Uranium-238 (A = 238, Z = 92) holds 238 − 92 = 146 neutrons, and uranium-235 (A = 235, Z = 92) holds 235 − 92 = 143 neutrons; the two isotopes differ by three neutrons in the nucleus and by a small difference in mass, and not at all in their chemistry. Every element has isotopes; most have several, and a few of those are stable while the rest decay, which is what the rest of this topic is about.

### The ᴬ₍Z₎X notation

A nuclide is written with the nucleon number A before and above the chemical symbol and the proton number Z before and below it: ᴬ₍Z₎X, where X is the element's symbol. The notation ²³⁸₉₂U reads: uranium, 92 protons, 238 nucleons. Two reading rules keep it honest. First, the lower number is the proton number, never the neutron number; the neutron number is A − Z and never appears in the symbol. Second, the upper number is the count of nucleons - protons and neutrons together - which is what makes the notation useful for decay equations: the books balance when the totals above the arrow and below the arrow are equal on both sides. The notation carries exactly what you need to know and nothing you do not: which element, how many protons, how many nucleons.

### The three radiations

Nuclei that decay emit one or more of three radiations, and each is a different thing. An α-particle is a helium nucleus: two protons and two neutrons, charge +2e (about +3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C), mass about 4 u. A β⁻-particle is an electron: charge −e, mass about 0.00055 u, which is about 1/1840 of a nucleon's mass. A β⁺-particle is a positron, the antiparticle of the electron: charge +e, the same mass as an electron. γ-radiation is an electromagnetic wave, not a particle: it carries no charge and no rest mass of its own, and in equations it is written with nucleon number 0 and charge 0. All three ionise the matter they pass through, but not equally. α-radiation ionises most strongly and is stopped by a few centimetres of air or a sheet of paper; γ-radiation ionises least and penetrates furthest, needing several centimetres of lead to attenuate it seriously; β-radiation sits between the two, stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. The pattern is not an accident: the heavier and more highly charged the particle, the more strongly it interacts, and the shorter the distance it lasts.

### What the scattering experiment could not do

The experiment's inferences are strong, but the measurement has limits worth naming. Large-angle scatters are rare - a few in a thousand - so the counting takes a long run and the statistical uncertainty in a small count is large, which affects how precisely the fraction deflected is known. The foil had to be thin enough that an α-particle meets at most a few nuclei on the way through, because a thick foil would let one α-particle scatter more than once and smear the angles. The detector had to be moved angle by angle and each angle's count read and recorded, and small errors of alignment affect the angle more at large deflection than at small. None of this weakens the two inferences; it sets how precisely the size and the charge concentration of the nucleus can be pinned down from them.

### Reading the ν̄ and ν symbols correctly

The bar over the ν is the marker of the antiparticle, in symbols as in words: ν̄ is the antineutrino and ν the neutrino. When a decay equation is checked, both symbols count as nucleon number 0 and charge 0, so they never disturb the balance; what they balance is the energy and the lepton count, which is why the pairing cannot be swapped. A β⁻-decay emits the β⁻-particle with the antineutrino, and a β⁺-decay emits the positron with the neutrino; the mirror pairing is the error, not a different way of writing the same decay.

### How the three radiations appear in equations

Each radiation has its standard entry in a decay equation. The α-particle is written ⁴₂α: nucleon number 4, charge +2. The β⁻-particle is written ⁰₋₁β (or ⁰₋₁e), nucleon number 0 and charge −1, and the β⁺-particle is written ⁰₊₁β, charge +1. The γ-ray is written γ, contributing nothing to either book. These entries are what get added across the arrow when the two conservation totals are checked, and writing them from memory is part of writing the equation itself.

### Worked example: comparing the masses in u

Compare the masses of the three radiations in u and the comparison explains their behaviour. The α-particle, 4 nucleons, has a mass of about 4 u, which is 4 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. The β-particle, an electron or positron, has a mass of about 0.00055 u, which is 0.00055 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 9.13 × 10⁻³¹ kg - about 1/1840 of one nucleon, so about 1/7360 of the α-particle. The γ-ray has no rest mass at all. The ratio of the two particle masses is 4 ÷ 0.00055 = 7272.7..., so the α-particle is over seven thousand times heavier than a β-particle, which is part of why α-radiation dumps its energy in a short distance and β-radiation travels further before it is stopped.

### The α-particle is not singly charged

A second recurring slip gives the α-particle a charge of +e. It is a helium nucleus: two protons and two neutrons, so its charge is +2e, about +3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, and its mass is about 4 u. The test is to count the protons it contains: two protons means +2e. The same count gives its nucleon number: two protons plus two neutrons makes 4, which is the ⁴₂α of the decay equation. Nothing about the α-particle needs remembering beyond its composition - everything else follows by counting.

### Section 11.1 in one place

The atom is mostly empty, with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus that carries nearly all the mass; electrons in orbit balance the proton count in a neutral atom. Nuclei are counted with A (nucleons) and Z (protons), written ᴬ₍Z₎X; the neutrons are A − Z, and isotopes share Z and differ in neutrons. Decay emits α (a helium nucleus, +2e, about 4 u), β⁻ (an electron, −e), β⁺ (a positron, +e) or γ (an electromagnetic wave, no charge, no rest mass), with the antineutrino in β⁻-decay and the neutrino in β⁺-decay. Every decay equation balances nucleon number and charge across the arrow; α lowers A by 4 and Z by 2, β⁻ raises Z by 1 with A fixed, β⁺ lowers Z by 1 with A fixed. Masses are measured in u, with 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and a nucleus's mass in u close to its nucleon number.

### Reading the scattering result as evidence

It is worth being exact about what the scattering experiment does and does not establish, because the inference is the skill being examined. The experiment does not show the nucleus directly; nothing does. It shows a pattern of counts against angle, and the pattern forces the model: an atom that is mostly empty explains the undeflected majority, and a nucleus that is tiny, massive and positively charged explains the rare large deflections and the rarer still rebounds. Working the inference forwards and backwards is the whole of the objective. Given the observations, state the model; given the model, state what each observation requires. A learner who recites the model without tying each part to its observation has memorised the answer without the evidence, and the evidence is what the question asks for.

### Working decay questions

In the exam, decay questions reward a fixed routine, so use the same one every time. Write the parent and the emitted particle with their numbers in the standard notation, leaving the daughter as a blank. Balance the nucleon numbers across the arrow to fill the daughter's A. Balance the charges across the arrow to fill the daughter's Z. Write the element symbol that matches the new Z, and finish with a check of both books before moving on. The routine takes half a minute, works for α, β⁻ and β⁺ alike, and never fails on the equation-writing part of a question. Where the question asks how you know, quote the two conservation totals, because that is the justification the totals exist for. Where it asks for the daughter's neutrons, subtract at the end: N = A − Z with the daughter's own numbers. And where it asks which radiation, match the change to the books: A down by 4 with Z down by 2 is α, Z up by 1 with A fixed is β⁻, Z down by 1 with A fixed is β⁺. Every one of these is the same two checks read in a different direction, which is why the routine is worth drilling until it is automatic.

### Where this connects

The nucleon and proton numbers are pure counting, which is why this topic builds on the units and quantity language of topic 1. The charge carried by each radiation connects back to the charge carriers of topic 9: an α-particle is positive like a hole in the story of conduction, and a β⁻-particle is an electron, the same particle that drifts through a wire carrying current. γ-radiation belongs to the electromagnetic family of topic 7, the same wave physics with a much shorter wavelength than light. The conservation of nucleon number and charge is a bookkeeping rule in the mould of the conservation of momentum in topic 3: totals that do not change while everything else does. And the scattering experiment's rare-event counting is trustworthy only because of the reading discipline of topic 12, where counts, repeats and uncertainties are handled.

## Quarks, leptons and the classification of particles

### Fundamental particles and the six quark flavours

A fundamental particle is one with no smaller constituents: there is nothing inside it to take apart, no experiment that splits it into something else. Quarks are fundamental, and there are six flavours (types) of them: up, down, strange, charm, top and bottom. The word flavour means nothing more exotic than type - it is the physicist's word for which of the six a quark is, the way a shop might speak of six flavours of the same kind of sweet. The up and down quarks are the pair that matter every day, because they make the proton and the neutron, and so every atom of every substance; the other four flavours appear in heavier, short-lived particles produced in high-energy collisions, and at this level they are met and named rather than used. The idea of a fundamental particle is the classification that drives this whole section, so it is worth being exact about it. A particle either has smaller constituents or it does not, and that single question sorts everything that follows. Protons and neutrons do have constituents - quarks - so they are not fundamental, whatever their everyday fame. Quarks and leptons do not, so they are, as far as any experiment has ever shown.

### The charge of each quark flavour

The charge of a quark is a fraction of the elementary charge e. The up, charm and top quarks each carry +⅔e. The down, strange and bottom quarks each carry −⅓e. A handy grouping keeps them apart: the quarks whose names point up (up, charm, top) carry +⅔e, and the rest (down, strange, bottom) carry −⅓e. Each antiquark carries the opposite charge to its quark: anti-up is −⅔e, anti-down is +⅓e, anti-charm is −⅔e, anti-strange is +⅓e, anti-top is −⅔e and anti-bottom is +⅓e. No knowledge of any other property of quarks is needed at this level: charge is the one number the syllabus asks you to recall and use, and the two fractions above are all of it. Notice what the fractions make possible. Two thirds and one third add to one: ⅔e + ⅓e = +1e, and ⅔e − ⅓e − ⅓e = 0. Whole charges come from combining the fractions, which is exactly how the proton gets its +1e and the neutron its zero, as the next block shows.

### Protons and neutrons are made of quarks

Protons and neutrons are not fundamental particles, because each is made of quarks. A proton holds two up quarks and one down quark, written uud. A neutron holds one up quark and two down quarks, written udd. Check the compositions against the charges and they confirm themselves. A proton's quarks carry +⅔e + ⅔e − ⅓e, which adds to +1e, the charge a proton is measured with. A neutron's carry +⅔e − ⅓e − ⅓e, which adds to 0, the charge a neutron is measured with. The charge arithmetic is the reason these compositions and not others: three up quarks would give +2e and three down quarks −1e, and neither is a particle you will meet as a proton or a neutron. So the names of the quarks inside each nucleon can be recovered from the charge alone, if you remember that each is three quarks: a total of +1e from three thirds needs two ups and a down, and a total of 0 needs one up and two downs.

### Hadrons: baryons and mesons

A hadron is a particle made of quarks, and every hadron is either a baryon or a meson. A baryon consists of three quarks: the proton (uud) and the neutron (udd) are the two baryons you meet every day, and every baryon you are asked about is three quarks, no more and no fewer. A meson consists of one quark and one antiquark, so it holds two constituents, not three, and its quark and antiquark need not share a flavour: an up quark paired with an anti-down antiquark is a meson, and so is a strange quark paired with an anti-up antiquark - the classification asks how many and which kind of constituents, not whether they match. The classification is a two-way sort with no third box: three quarks makes a baryon, a quark and an antiquark makes a meson, and anything made of quarks is one or the other. The proton and the neutron, being made of quarks, are hadrons; being made of three quarks, they are baryons. Learn the chain in one line: made of quarks means hadron; three quarks means baryon; one quark and one antiquark means meson.

### Worked example: a meson's charge

A meson is built from an up quark and an anti-down antiquark. What charge does it carry? Add the constituents' charges: the up quark carries +⅔e and the anti-down antiquark carries +⅓e, so the meson carries ⅔e + ⅓e = +1e. The two fractions add to a whole elementary charge, which is typical of quark combinations: quark charges come in thirds, and combinations of quarks and antiquarks add to whole multiples of e. Test the rule the other way before trusting it. Had the pairing been an up quark with a plain down quark, the total would be ⅔e − ⅓e = +⅓e, which is not a whole charge - and that pairing is not a meson anyway, because a meson needs one quark and one antiquark, not two quarks. The charge arithmetic and the classification rule work together: one tells you what the particle is worth, the other tells you what it is, and a candidate answer that breaks either is wrong.

### Quark changes in β-decay

β-decay, described in section 11.1 as the emission of an electron or a positron together with an (anti)neutrino, is at quark level a change inside one nucleon of the nucleus. In β⁻-decay a down quark becomes an up quark, so a neutron (udd) becomes a proton (uud); in β⁺-decay an up quark becomes a down quark, so a proton (uud) becomes a neutron (udd). The direction follows the decay: β⁻-decay leaves the nucleus with one more proton and the same nucleon number, which means a neutron must have become a proton, which means a down quark became an up quark; β⁺-decay is the exact reverse. The charges confirm the direction, so the rule can be re-derived from the charge books rather than remembered. In β⁻-decay the quark change is −⅓e → +⅔e, a rise of 1e, and the emitted β⁻-particle's charge of −1e balances it, keeping the total charge the same before and after. In β⁺-decay the change is +⅔e → −⅓e, a fall of 1e, and the emitted positron's +1e balances that. Reverse the quark change by accident and the emitted particle comes out wrong too: the conservation books of section 11.1 and the quark charges agree, or the equation is not β-decay.

### Leptons

Electrons and neutrinos are fundamental particles called leptons: they are not made of quarks, and nothing smaller has ever been found inside them. The lepton family in this topic holds the electron, the positron, the neutrino and the antineutrino - the positron being the electron's antiparticle and the antineutrino the neutrino's. The β-particles and (anti)neutrinos a nucleus emits in β-decay are leptons, not hadrons, whatever their origin in a nuclear process: where a particle comes from does not change what it is. The split between hadrons and leptons is the top-level sort of the standard picture: hadrons are made of quarks, leptons are fundamental and not made of quarks, and a particle is one or the other, never both and never neither. A proton is a hadron and a baryon; an electron is a lepton; a neutrino is a lepton; a neutron is a hadron and a baryon. Nothing that decays in this topic crosses the line, which is what makes the classification stable enough to test.

### The errors the classification catches

Three errors account for most lost marks here. Giving a meson three quarks: a meson is one quark and one antiquark, two constituents, and assuming the pair shares a flavour is a second error on the same card. Calling the proton or the neutron fundamental: both are baryons made of three quarks, and only quarks and leptons are fundamental in this topic. Giving a quark the wrong charge: up, charm and top carry +⅔e; down, strange and bottom carry −⅓e; and the antiquarks carry the opposites. Each error is caught by one test: count the constituents (two or three?), ask whether it has smaller parts, and check the fraction of e against the flavour.

### Why the standard picture is a classification, not a zoo

The particle names in this topic are few, and the classification does the work of remembering them. Start from the top question: is the particle made of quarks? If yes, it is a hadron, and the next question is how many constituents: three quarks makes it a baryon, and one quark plus one antiquark makes it a meson. If no, it is a lepton, and the leptons in this topic are the electron, the positron, the neutrino and the antineutrino, all fundamental. Under the baryons sit the proton (uud) and the neutron (udd), the two you already use; every other name in the classification is a box, and the boxes are what the syllabus asks about. The sort runs in one direction only, so every particle lands in exactly one box, and no particle is both fundamental and made of quarks - which is what makes the classification safe to test.

### Worked example: checking a stated composition

A revision card claims that a baryon with quark composition uds... take a cleaner case. A learner writes that the neutron is uud and the proton udd, the wrong way round. Check each against its charge. The learner's neutron (uud) would carry ⅔e + ⅔e − ⅓e = +1e, but a neutron is measured with zero charge, so the composition fails; the learner's proton (udd) would carry ⅔e − ⅓e − ⅓e = 0, but a proton is measured with +1e. Both fail, and swapping them back fixes both: neutron udd, proton uud. The check is mechanical - add the thirds, compare with the measured charge - and it works for any three-quark composition you are asked to test.

### Where this connects

The quark picture completes the account of β-decay from section 11.1: the emitted electron and antineutrino of a β⁻-decay are leptons, and the change that released them was a down quark becoming an up quark inside one nucleon. The charges of the up and down quarks are the arithmetic behind the conservation of charge: adding ⅔e, ⅔e and −⅓e is the same bookkeeping as balancing the proton numbers across a decay equation. The elementary charge e itself was met in topic 9 as the size of the charge on an electron, and it is the same e every quark charge is a fraction of. The classification habit - fundamental or not, hadron or lepton, baryon or meson - is the sort of sorting that topic 1's quantity language makes precise, and the same habit of asking what is inside what organises the whole of the physical world this syllabus walks through.
