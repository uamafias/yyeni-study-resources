## Stationary waves

### The principle of superposition

When two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement at that point is the sum of the displacements that each wave alone would produce there at that instant. Displacement here means the distance of a point of the medium from its rest position — the wave sense of the word, used throughout this topic — with a sign to show which side of the rest position the point sits. The displacements may be of any size and either sign: two displacements of the same sign add to a larger one, and an upward and an equal downward displacement sum to zero.

### What the meeting does, and does not do

Superposition is momentary. Where the two displacements have the same sign the resultant is larger than either alone (constructive); where they have opposite signs it is smaller (destructive), and equal opposite displacements cancel exactly. The instant passes and each wave carries on: after two waves have met and superposed, each continues onwards with its original amplitude, speed and frequency unchanged, as though the other were not there. The resultant intensity at a point follows from the resultant displacement, and intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude — so overlapping waves do not add their intensities.

### Worked example: two pulses on a skipping rope

Leena and Sipho flick opposite ends of a stretched skipping rope. At the moment the two pulses pass the same point, Leena's pulse alone would hold the rope 4.0 cm above its rest line and Sipho's alone would hold it 1.5 cm below. The resultant displacement is the sum: 4.0 cm upwards together with 1.5 cm downwards leaves 2.5 cm upwards, because 4.0 − 1.5 = 2.5. A moment later the pulses have passed through each other and travel on unchanged, Leena's still 4.0 cm tall and Sipho's still 1.5 cm deep.

### Add displacements, not amplitudes or intensities

A common error is to add the amplitudes, or the intensities, of two overlapping waves. Superposition adds displacements at each point at each instant. The amplitude of the resultant at a point is the largest displacement the sum reaches there, and the intensity follows from that amplitude squared. The test: is it displacements that are added? If a learner has added two amplitudes or two intensities, the principle has been applied to the wrong quantities.

### Microwaves reflected from a metal plate

A microwave transmitter is aimed straight at a flat metal plate. The plate reflects the wave, so two waves of the same frequency travel in opposite directions along the same line and superpose: a stationary wave is set up between transmitter and plate. A probe receiver connected to a meter is moved slowly along the line, and the reading rises and falls — a maximum at an antinode, a minimum (zero or close to it) at a node. The distance across several node-to-node gaps is measured with a ruler; each gap is half a wavelength, so the wavelength follows by doubling the per-gap spacing. With the wave speed known — microwaves travel at 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ — the frequency then follows from the wave equation v = fλ met in topic 7.

### Stretched strings and air columns

A long string is fixed at both ends and driven by a vibration generator. At particular frequencies the string shows points that stay still while the segments between them swing: a stationary wave, formed because the wave from the generator and its reflection from the fixed end travel in opposite directions. For an air column, a tuning fork is held at the mouth of a tube whose length is changed by sliding it in a deeper or shallower pan of water. At some lengths the note grows loud: the column then carries a stationary wave with a node at the water surface and an antinode at the open mouth, and the fork's driving matches a frequency at which the column naturally sounds — resonance.

### Stationary waves, nodes and antinodes

A stationary wave is the pattern formed where two waves of the same frequency travelling in opposite directions superpose. A node is a point where the amplitude is zero; an antinode is a point where the amplitude is greatest. The nodes and antinodes stay in fixed places — the pattern does not travel along — and the wave transfers no energy along the medium, unlike the progressive waves of topic 7. A node forms at a fixed plate or a fixed end; the open mouth of an air column sits at an antinode.

### Explaining the formation graphically

To see why the nodes exist, draw the two waves separately at several instants. Take two identical waves of amplitude A travelling in opposite directions, and draw them at times separated by a quarter of a period. At each chosen instant, add the two displacements point by point along the length to draw the resultant. At certain points the two curves are equal and opposite at each instant drawn: the resultant there is zero at every instant — a node. Halfway between neighbouring nodes the two displacements are equal at every instant, adding to 2A when both are largest — an antinode. The resultant, drawn at several instants, keeps the same nodes while its antinodes oscillate: that envelope is the signature of a stationary wave, and a sketch graph of it with the nodes marked is the standard way the pattern is shown.

### Finding the wavelength from the node pattern

Neighbouring nodes are half a wavelength apart; neighbouring antinodes are half a wavelength apart too; a node and the next antinode are a quarter of a wavelength apart. So one node-to-node measurement gives λ = 2 × spacing. A better method measures across many gaps: with 6 node-to-node gaps spanning 14.4 cm, each gap is 14.4 ÷ 6 = 2.40 cm and λ = 2 × 2.40 = 4.80 cm. The longer the span measured, the smaller the percentage uncertainty a single ruler reading contributes, because the reading's uncertainty is spread across a larger distance. The same numbers come from antinode positions.

### A stationary wave is not two-source interference

A stationary wave is sometimes described as if it were the two-source interference pattern of sub-topic 8.3. It is not. The two waves that form a stationary wave travel in opposite directions — most often a wave and its own reflection — and the pattern is fixed in place. Two-source interference is formed by waves from two separate side-by-side sources travelling onward the same way, and its pattern spreads out from between the sources. The test: are the two waves travelling towards each other? If they are, a stationary wave is forming; if they travel onward side by side, it is a two-source pattern.

### A node is a quarter of a wavelength from the next antinode

The distance from a node to the next antinode is a quarter of a wavelength, not half. A learner who doubles a node-to-antinode distance has halved the wavelength. The safe rule is to work from node to node: that spacing is λ/2. The test: node to node, or node to antinode? Node to node is λ/2, safe to double; node to antinode is λ/4, to be multiplied by four; doubling is right for the first and wrong for the second.

### Connections

This section leans on topic 7: progressive waves, the wave equation v = fλ, and phase and phase difference. Interference (sub-topics 8.2 and 8.3) is the other face of superposition. The measuring skills used here — spacing readings across many gaps, and their uncertainties — are practised fully in topic 12.

## Diffraction

### What diffraction is

Diffraction is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or past the edge of an obstacle. The wave stays in the same medium: its speed, frequency and wavelength are unchanged; what changes is the set of directions in which the wave energy travels. Each point of a wavefront that survives at the gap sends wave energy onward into new directions, which is why the wavefront beyond a narrow gap curves outward. (A point's displacement — its distance from its rest position — carries on oscillating at the same frequency throughout.) The spreading is not refraction: refraction is the change of direction at a boundary where the wave's speed changes.

### The gap relative to the wavelength

How much a wave spreads depends on the ratio of the gap width to the wavelength. The spreading is greatest when the gap is close to one wavelength wide. A gap several wavelengths wide lets the middle of the wavefront through almost straight, with slight curving at the two edges. A gap much narrower than a wavelength passes little energy, though what passes spreads widely. The same rule applies at an obstacle's edge: a wave spreads around it appreciably when the obstacle's size is comparable to the wavelength.

### A ripple tank shows diffraction

A ripple tank has a vibrating bar that makes straight wavefronts. A barrier with a gap is placed in their path, and the wavefronts beyond the gap are viewed against the tank's shadow grid. With the gap set about one wavelength wide, the wavefronts beyond it spread into wide, nearly semicircular arcs. The gap is then widened to several wavelengths, and the wavefronts beyond stay nearly straight apart from curving at the edges. The wavelength is read against the grid so the gap width can be set as a stated multiple of it, and the comparison of the two patterns shows the effect of the ratio.

### Behind the wall, and behind the hill

Diffraction explains why waves carry round obstacles. A minibus taxi's horn in a Windhoek street is heard from behind a concrete wall: the horn's low notes have wavelengths of a metre or so, comparable to the wall, so the sound spreads around its edge and reaches the pavement beyond. The same physics separates radio from light: a radio beacon of wavelength 1.2 km serves fishing boats behind a rocky headland about a kilometre across, because the radio wave spreads appreciably around an obstacle of comparable size; the harbour lantern on the same headland, with a wavelength of order 5 × 10⁻⁷ m, spreads far too little on that scale to be seen around it and travels on in straight lines.

### Diffraction is not refraction

A learner who says a wave 'bends because it enters shallower water' has described refraction. Diffraction happens in one medium with no change of speed; refraction happens at a boundary between two media of different speeds. The test: does the wave change medium? If it does not, any spreading at a gap or an edge is diffraction; if it does, the bending is refraction.

### Connections

Diffraction is one of the two behaviours that follow from superposition (8.1); the two-source interference patterns of 8.3 are seen at all because each slit's waves diffract before they overlap. Topic 7 supplies the wavelength of each region of the electromagnetic spectrum.

## Interference

### Interference

Where waves from two sources overlap, the displacements — each point's distance from its rest position — superpose point by point (sub-topic 8.1). Where the two waves arrive in step, with a path difference of a whole number of wavelengths, the resultant displacement is large: constructive interference, a maximum. Where they arrive in antiphase, with a path difference of an odd number of half wavelengths, they cancel: destructive interference, a minimum. Interference is this steady pattern of maxima and minima in the overlap region.

### Coherence

Two sources are coherent when they have a constant phase difference, and so the same frequency. With a constant phase difference the maxima and minima stay in fixed places long enough to be seen; sources that drift in and out of step average the pattern away.

### Coherent does not mean in phase

A learner who says coherent sources must be in phase has asked too much. In phase is one special case: phase difference zero, held constant. Two sources holding a steady phase difference of any size — a quarter of a cycle, half a cycle — are coherent and interfere steadily. The test: must the phase difference be zero, or only unchanging? Only unchanging is required.

### Four demonstrations, one pattern

The same pattern is shown by every kind of wave. In a ripple tank, straight wavefronts reach a barrier with two gaps: beyond them the water carries fixed bands of strong and weak oscillation. With sound, two loudspeakers about a metre apart are fed from one signal generator, and a listener walking a line in front of them passes loud and quiet places. With microwaves, a transmitter faces a metal plate bearing two gaps, and a receiver moved along an arc behind them reads alternating strong and weak signals at fixed angles. With light, a double slit is lit by a single lamp and a screen beyond shows bright and dark fringes. In each of these four demonstrations the two sources are coherent, because both are driven by one parent wave.

### Why each pair of sources is coherent

In each demonstration the two sources share one origin. The two gaps in the ripple tank are cut in the path of one set of wavefronts; the two loudspeakers are fed by one signal generator; the two microwave gaps are lit by one transmitter; the two slits of the double slit are lit by one lamp. Each gap or speaker passes on the phase of the single wave that reaches it, so the two sets of waves leave their sources with a phase difference that stays constant. This is the practical reason two-source interference is demonstrated this way: the coherence is arranged at the source, and the pattern in the overlap region then holds steady enough to measure.

### Where the maxima and minima sit

The position of each band follows from the path difference, the difference between the two distances travelled to the point. On the centre line, equidistant from the two sources, the path difference is zero: every wave arrives in step, and the central maximum sits there. One wavelength of path difference to either side gives the next maximum; half a wavelength gives the first minimum. Counting outwards from the centre, the maxima sit at whole numbers of wavelengths and the minima at odd numbers of half wavelengths, which is why the pattern is a set of fixed bands: each band is the set of points that share one path difference.

### The conditions for observable fringes

Two-source fringes are seen when three conditions hold. The sources must be coherent: the same frequency with a constant phase difference. The two waves must arrive with comparable amplitudes — a strong wave and a weak one barely dent each other, so both must come from similar sources and overlap where neither has died away. And the fringe spacing must be wide enough for the eye or detector to resolve, which sets a lower limit on the source spacing for light. Two separate lamps fail the first condition: the phase of the light from each changes abruptly many times each microsecond, so the phase difference between the lamps — and with it every maximum and minimum — changes faster than the eye can register, and the pattern averages away. A double slit lit by a single lamp works because both slits are lit by the same wave train: whatever phase leaves the lamp enters both slits together, so the phase difference at each point, set by the two path lengths, stays constant.

### The double-slit equation

For light, the fringe spacing obeys λ = ax / D, where λ is the wavelength of the light in m, a the separation of the two slits in m, x the distance between the centres of neighbouring bright fringes in m, and D the distance from the slits to the screen in m. Rearranged, x = λD / a: the fringes are wider apart for a longer wavelength, for a screen further away, and for slits closer together. The equation holds for the small angles of a double-slit pattern, where the screen is far from the slits compared with their separation.

### Worked example: red light through a double slit

Red light passes through two slits 0.45 mm apart onto a screen 2.5 m beyond them. Nine gaps between bright fringes span 24.3 mm. Fringe spacing first: x = 24.3 mm ÷ 9 = 2.70 mm. Convert to metres: a = 0.45 mm = 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ m and x = 2.70 mm = 2.70 × 10⁻³ m. Equation: λ = ax / D. Substitution: λ = (4.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.70 × 10⁻³) ÷ 2.5. Now 4.5 × 2.70 = 12.15, so the numerator is 1.215 × 10⁻⁶, and 1.215 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2.5 = 4.86 × 10⁻⁷ m. So the wavelength is 4.86 × 10⁻⁷ m, given as 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ m to two significant figures — red light, about 490 nm.

### Slit separation, fringe spacing, gaps not fringes

Two lengths in λ = ax / D get confused. The slit separation a is the distance between the two slits — fixed by the slide; the fringe spacing x is the distance between the centres of neighbouring bright fringes on the screen — set by the experiment. And when a span of fringes is measured, the span is divided by the number of gaps between them: twelve bright fringes have eleven gaps between them, so the spacing is the span ÷ 11. The test: count the gaps, not the fringes; and check which length is a and which is x before substituting.

### Connections

Everything here rests on superposition (8.1) and on the diffraction that lets each slit's wave spread (8.2). The double slit returns as the many-slit grating in 8.4, where a large number of slits sharpens the maxima; the loudspeaker and microwave patterns are the same physics as the light fringes.

## The diffraction grating

### The grating equation

A diffraction grating is a slide carrying many equally spaced parallel slits — several hundred to a few thousand per millimetre. Its maxima sit at angles given by d sin θ = nλ, where d is the spacing between the centres of neighbouring slits in m, θ the angle between the straight-through direction and the nth-order maximum, n the order of the maximum, and λ the wavelength in m. The order counts the maxima out from the centre: n = 0 is the straight-through maximum, n = 1 the first on either side, and at the nth-order maximum the path difference between waves from neighbouring slits is n whole wavelengths — so the displacements arriving from neighbouring slits, each point's distance from its rest position, are in step and add. The spacing is the reciprocal of the line density: for N lines per millimetre, convert to lines per metre first, then d = 1 ÷ N. Because sin θ cannot exceed 1, the largest order visible is the greatest whole number n with n ≤ d ÷ λ.

### Worked example: the first order of green light

Green light of wavelength 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ m falls normally on a grating with 400 lines per mm. Line density: N = 400 per mm = 4.00 × 10⁵ per m, so d = 1 ÷ N = 2.50 × 10⁻⁶ m. Equation for the first order: sin θ = nλ ÷ d. Substitution: sin θ = (1 × 5.0 × 10⁻⁷) ÷ (2.50 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.20. So θ = 11.5° to three significant figures. Because the angle is modest, the maxima of the first order sit near the centre of the screen; a grating with more lines per mm gives a larger angle and a more precise measurement of θ.

### Finding a wavelength with a grating

To determine a wavelength, shine the light normally onto the grating and let the pattern fall on a screen a measured distance away. Measure the distance from the central maximum to the chosen order's bright line, together with the grating-to-screen distance; the tangent of the angle is their ratio, giving θ. The line density printed on the grating mount gives d = 1 ÷ N with N converted to lines per metre. Then the equation λ = (d sin θ) ÷ n gives the wavelength. A grating is preferred to a double slit because its maxima are sharper and brighter, and its larger angles carry a smaller percentage uncertainty, so the wavelength comes out more precise. The structure of any mounting apparatus is a laboratory matter; the physics needed at this level is the geometry of the angles and the one equation.

### White light through a grating

White light is a mixture of wavelengths, and d sin θ = nλ sends each wavelength to its own angle. At the centre all wavelengths share θ = 0, so the central maximum stays white. In each outer order the colours separate: violet, the shortest visible wavelength of about 4 × 10⁻⁷ m, is deviated least and sits nearest the centre; red, about 7 × 10⁻⁷ m, is deviated most and sits outermost. Each order is therefore a band of colours, and the bands repeat at larger angles for the higher orders.

### d is a spacing in metres; θ is measured from the centre

Two slips recur with the grating equation. First, d is the slit spacing in metres, not the number of lines: for a 500-lines-per-mm grating, N = 5.00 × 10⁵ per m and d = 1 ÷ N = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ m; substituting 500 puts the equation out by many powers of ten. Second, θ is measured from the straight-through direction to the maximum concerned: the angle between the two first-order maxima, one on each side, is 2θ. The test: is d a spacing in metres, and is θ measured from the centre?

### Connections

The grating is the many-slit version of the double slit of 8.3: its maxima are interference maxima, sharpened by the many slits. The geometry of the angles continues the trigonometry of topic 1, and the wavelengths it separates come from the regions of the spectrum met in topic 7.
