## Kinematics

### Distance and displacement

Distance is the total length of the path a body moves along, measured in metres (m); it is a scalar, so it adds up along the journey whatever the direction of each leg. Displacement is the distance moved in a stated direction from the starting point, also in metres; it is a vector, so it carries a magnitude and a direction. A taxi that drives 3.0 km east and then 1.0 km west travels a distance of 4.0 km but finishes with a displacement of 2.0 km east. The question that keeps the two apart is whether the direction of the motion changed at any point. When it did, the distance is greater than the magnitude of the displacement; when a body ends where it began, the distance can be large while the displacement is zero. Both are measured with the same instrument, a tape or a rule, but they answer different questions: how much ground was covered, and how far, and which way, from the start.

### Speed and velocity

Speed is distance travelled per unit time, in m s⁻¹; it is a scalar. Velocity is the rate of change of displacement, also in m s⁻¹; it is a vector whose direction is the direction of motion at that instant. For a journey in one direction without turning, the average speed and the magnitude of the average velocity are the same. The moment a journey turns - a return leg, a loop back - the two part company: distance keeps growing along the path while displacement can shrink or reverse. A courier who rides 300 m east in 60 s and 300 m west in 60 s covers 600 m in 120 s, an average speed of 5.0 m s⁻¹, but finishes where she started, so her displacement is zero and her average velocity is zero. An average value is the total divided by the total time; the instantaneous velocity is the value at one moment of the journey.

### Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = (v − u) ÷ t, where u is the initial velocity, v is the velocity after time t, and the unit is m s⁻². It is a vector, with a direction and a sign. A body accelerates when it speeds up, when it slows down, and when it changes direction while keeping the same speed, because in each of these the velocity - speed together with direction - is changing. In everyday speech slowing down is not called acceleration; in physics it is, with the acceleration directed against the motion. A car pulling away from a traffic light might gain 4.0 m s⁻¹ of velocity each second: its acceleration is 4.0 m s⁻² in the direction of travel. A negative acceleration in the positive direction means the velocity is decreasing; whether that means slowing down depends on which way the body is moving.

### Worked example: a taxi on the B1

A minibus taxi leaves Otjiwarongo northbound on the B1. It covers 12 km in 14 min, stops, then returns 4.0 km south in 6.0 min. Work each quantity from its definition. The distance is the whole path: 12 + 4.0 = 16 km, which is 16000 m. The time is 14 + 6.0 = 20 min, which is 1200 s. So the average speed is 16000 ÷ 1200 = 13.3 m s⁻¹. The displacement is the straight-line change: 12 − 4.0 = 8.0 km north, which is 8000 m, so the average velocity is 8000 ÷ 1200 = 6.67 m s⁻¹ north, or 6.7 m s⁻¹ to two significant figures. The speed exceeds the velocity because the taxi changed direction; had it driven 16 km straight on, the two would have matched.

### Representing motion on graphs

Motion is represented on graphs plotted against time, and the axis labels carry the meaning: a distance–time graph, a displacement–time graph, a speed–time graph or a velocity–time graph. On a displacement–time graph the gradient of the line gives the velocity; on a velocity–time graph the gradient gives the acceleration and the area between the line and the time axis gives the displacement. A distance–time graph differs from a displacement–time graph for the same journey: distance adds along the path, so its graph cannot fall, while displacement is measured from the start with direction, so its graph can fall and can pass below the axis. A graph described in words needs three things: the quantity and unit on each axis, the shape of the line - horizontal, straight and sloping, or curved - and the key values where the line starts, ends or changes. On a velocity–time graph a horizontal line means constant velocity, a straight sloping line means uniform acceleration, and a curve means an acceleration that is itself changing.

### Worked example: describing a graph in words

Describing a graph in words is itself part of using graphical methods. Take a cyclist who rides at 4.0 m s⁻¹ for 10 s, then slows uniformly to rest over the next 5.0 s. Her velocity–time graph has velocity in m s⁻¹ on the vertical axis and time in s on the horizontal axis. It starts at a height of 4.0, stays horizontal until t = 10 s, then falls as a straight line to zero at t = 15 s. The acceleration in the second phase is the gradient: the velocity drops by 4.0 m s⁻¹ in 5.0 s, which is 0.80 m s⁻². The total displacement is the whole area: 40 m for the rectangle and 10 m for the triangle, 50 m in all. Anyone who reads the description can sketch the graph from the axes, the shape and the key values alone.

### Displacement from the area under a velocity–time graph

For any velocity–time graph, the displacement in a time interval is the whole area between the line and the time axis over that interval, because velocity multiplied by time is a distance-type quantity and the area is that product collected slice by slice. Split the area into rectangles and triangles. A stretch of constant velocity v for a time t contributes the rectangle v × t. A stretch of uniform acceleration from rest contributes a triangle, so s = ½vt; and since v = at, this is s = ½at². A stretch that starts at a non-zero velocity u contributes a rectangle ut beneath the starting velocity and a triangle above it for the increase; the pair together is a trapezium of parallel sides u and v, which is where s = ½(u + v)t comes from. Areas below the time axis are negative velocities: they subtract from the displacement, because the body is moving back towards the start.

### The whole area counts

The commonest area error is taking the region under a velocity–time line as one triangle when the motion does not start from rest. A line from (0, 3.0) to (12, 9.0) on a velocity–time graph does not enclose a single triangle with the axes: the area is a rectangle of 3.0 × 12 = 36 m plus a triangle of 36 m on top, 72 m in all, while the triangle-only answer of 54 m silently drops the rectangle under the starting velocity. The test is one glance: does the line start from the origin? If it does, one triangle reaches the axis. If it does not, count the rectangle as well - the displacement is the whole area between the line and the time axis.

### Worked example: a bakkie joining the B1

A bakkie joins the B1 at 2.0 m s⁻¹, accelerates uniformly to 6.0 m s⁻¹ over 8.0 s, then holds 6.0 m s⁻¹ for a further 12 s. Its velocity–time graph starts at a height of 2.0, rises as a straight line to 6.0 at t = 8.0 s, and continues horizontally to t = 20 s. The displacement is the area, taken piece by piece. The acceleration phase is a rectangle plus a triangle: the rectangle is 2.0 × 8.0 = 16 m, and the triangle, of height 6.0 − 2.0 = 4.0 m s⁻¹ and base 8.0 s, adds half of 32 m, which is 16 m; the phase total is 32 m. The constant phase is one rectangle: 6.0 × 12 = 72 m. So after 20 s the bakkie has advanced 32 + 72 = 104 m, and the graph's own gradient, (6.0 − 2.0) ÷ 8.0 = 0.50 m s⁻², is its acceleration.

### Velocity from the gradient of a displacement–time graph

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement, and on a displacement–time graph a rate of change is a gradient. For a straight line the velocity is v = ∆s ÷ ∆t, using any two points on the line. For a curve there is no single gradient: the average velocity over an interval is the gradient of the chord joining the two ends of the interval, and the velocity at one instant is the gradient of the tangent drawn at that point. The shape of the graph carries the motion: a horizontal line is a body at rest, a straight sloping line is constant velocity, a curve is a changing velocity, and a steeper line is a faster body because the same time interval carries a larger change of displacement. Where the graph slopes downwards the velocity is negative: the body is returning towards its starting point. A straight line rising from 0 to 90 m in 12 s gives v = 90 ÷ 12 = 7.5 m s⁻¹, and because the line is straight, that value holds along the whole stretch.

### Acceleration from the gradient of a velocity–time graph

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, and on a velocity–time graph it is the gradient of the line: a = ∆v ÷ ∆t, in m s⁻². Take ∆v and ∆t from two points far apart on the line, so that the small uncertainty in reading each axis is a small fraction of each difference. A straight line has one acceleration along its whole length; a curve has a changing one, read tangent by tangent. The three quantities the graph carries must be kept apart: the height of the line is the velocity, the gradient is the acceleration, the area is the displacement. A negative gradient means the velocity is decreasing in the positive direction - the body slows down if it moves positively and speeds up if it moves negatively, because the acceleration acts along the negative direction in both cases.

### The height is not the gradient

Asked for an acceleration, a learner who reads the height of a velocity–time line has answered a different question. At t = 4.0 s a line may stand at 10 m s⁻¹: that is the velocity, and its unit says so. If the line is straight with a gradient of 2.0 m s⁻², then 2.0 m s⁻² is the acceleration at that moment and at every other. The one-glance test: is the question about the slope of the line or about its height? Slope questions take the gradient; height questions take the reading; anything about the region under the line is a displacement.

### Deriving the equations of uniformly accelerated motion

The equations of uniformly accelerated motion are derived, not memorised: each comes from the definitions of velocity and acceleration, for motion in a straight line with uniform acceleration. First, the definition of acceleration, a = (v − u) ÷ t, rearranges to v = u + at. Then the displacement is written as the area under the velocity–time line. Over a time t that area is the rectangle ut beneath the starting velocity plus the triangle ½(v − u)t above it; collecting the terms gives s = ut + ½at². The same area, taken as a trapezium with parallel sides u and v and width t, is s = ½(u + v)t - the average velocity times the time. Finally t can be eliminated: substituting t = (v − u) ÷ a into s = ½(u + v)t and simplifying gives v² = u² + 2as, the equation to reach for when the time is neither given nor asked. Each equation links four of the five quantities s, u, v, a and t, and that is how one is chosen: list what the problem gives and wants, and pick the equation whose missing quantity is the one neither given nor wanted.

### Falling freely

A body moving freely under gravity, with air resistance ignored, has a constant downward acceleration g = 9.81 m s⁻², the acceleration of free fall. The value does not depend on the body's mass, on its speed, or on whether it is rising or falling: a stone released from rest, a stone thrown down at 4.0 m s⁻¹ and a stone thrown up at 15 m s⁻¹ all carry a = 9.81 m s⁻² downward at every instant of their flight. A rising ball slows down because the acceleration opposes its motion; the same ball, once past the top, speeds up for the mirror reason. Free-fall problems are solved with the equations of uniformly accelerated motion exactly as they stand, with a = g or a = −g according to the sign convention chosen.

### Worked example: a stone dropped into a borehole

A stone is dropped from rest down a borehole and strikes the water 45 m below. Take down as positive, so u = 0 and a = g = 9.81 m s⁻². The time comes from s = ut + ½at²: with u = 0, 45 = ½ × 9.81 × t², so t² = 90 ÷ 9.81 = 9.17 and t = 3.03 s, which is 3.0 s to two significant figures. The impact speed comes from v² = u² + 2as: v² = 2 × 9.81 × 45 = 882.9, and the square root of 882.9 gives v = 29.7 m s⁻¹ downward. The working carries at least three significant figures until the final line, and each answer states to how many significant figures it is rounded.

### Vertical motion: signs and the turning point

Vertical problems go wrong on signs more often than on algebra. The cure is one rule, applied once: choose which way is positive, then give s, u, v and a their signs from that choice, and keep the choice for the whole flight. With up positive, a ball thrown up at 8.0 m s⁻¹ has u = +8.0 m s⁻¹, a = −9.81 m s⁻² while it rises and while it falls, a negative v on the way down, and a negative s once it passes below the release point. The acceleration does not flip sign part-way through the flight, because gravity has not changed: what changes is the velocity. At the highest point the velocity is zero for an instant while the acceleration stays 9.81 m s⁻² downward - zero velocity is not zero acceleration. And distance divided by time does not give the final velocity: it gives the average, which for uniform acceleration is ½(u + v).

### An experiment to determine the acceleration of free fall

To determine g with a falling object, drop a dense ball from rest and time the fall. Set up a release mechanism above a trapdoor or light gate, both wired to an electronic timer: the release starts the clock and the arrival stops it. Measure the height of fall h with a metre rule held vertical, from the ball's release point to the detector, reading the rule at eye level to avoid a parallax error. Take the fall time t from the timer. Repeat the timing at each height and average the repeats to reduce the effect of random error; the scatter of the repeats shows the size of that error. Vary the height across at least six values spread from about 0.30 m to the greatest height the apparatus allows, so the graph spans a wide range. The readings give g two ways. For a single fall, h = ½gt², so g = 2h ÷ t². Better, plot h against t²: the points lie on a straight line through the origin whose gradient is g ÷ 2, so g is twice the gradient, in m s⁻². The graph is better than one pair of readings because a line drawn through six points averages them and exposes any reading that is out of line.

### Worked example: the class's falling-ball readings

Here are our readings from one run of the experiment, each time the average of several drops at that height.

| h / m | t / s | t² / s² |
|---|---|---|
| 0.300 | 0.247 | 0.0610 |
| 0.500 | 0.319 | 0.102 |
| 0.700 | 0.378 | 0.143 |
| 0.900 | 0.428 | 0.183 |
| 1.10 | 0.474 | 0.224 |

Plotted as h against t², the points lie close to one straight line. The gradient, taken from the first point to the last, is (1.10 − 0.300) ÷ (0.224 − 0.0610) = 0.800 ÷ 0.163 = 4.91 m s⁻², and g is twice that: 2 × 4.91 = 9.82 m s⁻². The value sits close to 9.81 m s⁻²; the small gap is the effect of the remaining timing uncertainty, a plausible outcome for this apparatus.

### Velocity in one direction, acceleration at right angles

When a body keeps a uniform velocity in one direction while a uniform acceleration pushes at right angles to it, the path is a smooth curve - a parabola. The reason is that the two directions do not mix. Along the direction of the velocity nothing changes: equal distances are covered in equal times, and the component of velocity along that direction keeps its value. Along the direction of the acceleration the component of velocity changes at a constant rate, so the uniformly accelerated equations apply there. The two separate descriptions are joined by one shared quantity: the time. The time of the flight is set entirely by the perpendicular motion, and every distance worked out along the first direction uses that same time. A ball rolled horizontally off a table top keeps its forward speed while gaining downward speed at 9.81 m s⁻¹ each second: the table's height fixes how long it falls, and that time fixes how far forward it lands. Faster roll, same height: the same time in the air and a longer horizontal carry.

### Components of a velocity

A velocity at an angle is handled by splitting it. A launch velocity u at θ above the horizontal has a horizontal component u cos θ and a vertical component u sin θ; the horizontal one is unchanged by gravity and the vertical one gains 9.81 m s⁻¹ downward each second. Every question about the height or the time in the air uses the vertical component alone; every question about the horizontal reach uses the horizontal component with the time found vertically. When the components need combining again - for the speed at some point of the flight - they combine by Pythagoras, the square root of the sum of their squares, because they are perpendicular: adding them as ordinary numbers is a vector error dressed as arithmetic. At 12 m s⁻¹ at 30°, the components are 12 cos 30° = 10.4 m s⁻¹ and 12 sin 30° = 6.0 m s⁻¹, and the speed is the square root of (10.4² + 6.0²) = 12 m s⁻¹, as it must be.

### Worked example: an orange off a shelf

An orange rolls off a shelf 1.20 m above the floor, moving horizontally at 0.60 m s⁻¹. The vertical fall sets the time, because the horizontal motion keeps its steady rate while gravity acts only downward. Vertically: h = ½gt², so t² = 2h ÷ g; 2 × 1.20 = 2.40, and 2.40 ÷ 9.81 = 0.245, so t = 0.495 s. Horizontally: the distance is the constant velocity times that time, x = 0.60 × 0.495 = 0.297 m, which is 0.30 m to two significant figures. On landing the orange has a downward component of 9.81 × 0.495 = 4.86 m s⁻¹ while its horizontal component is still 0.60 m s⁻¹, so its speed is the square root of (0.60² + 4.86²) = 4.9 m s⁻¹. Both answers are quoted to two significant figures, the precision of the data.

### Checking the topic

Distance and speed are scalars; displacement, velocity and acceleration are vectors. A displacement–time graph gives velocity from its gradient; a velocity–time graph gives acceleration from its gradient and displacement from its area. The equations of uniformly accelerated motion follow from those two facts, and free fall is those equations with a = 9.81 m s⁻². A velocity in one direction with an acceleration at right angles to it is two straight-line motions sharing one time.
