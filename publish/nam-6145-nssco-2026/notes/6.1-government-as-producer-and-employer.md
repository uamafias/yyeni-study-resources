## Government as producer and employer

### Separate production, provision and employment

Government participates in an economy by organising output and hiring people to help provide it. As a producer it creates goods or services; as an employer it purchases workers’ labour under employment arrangements. These roles can occur together. A government workshop making furniture may hire carpenters, while a government clinic may hire nurses to provide care. The furniture is a good and the care is a service, but each activity uses resources to satisfy wants.

Ownership and output type answer different questions. Public sector identifies government control. Goods and services identify what is provided. A government-controlled organisation can produce tangible goods, and a private organisation can provide services. Likewise, a payment from government does not establish that government itself performed the production. It may purchase output from a private supplier. The supplied ownership, production and employment facts determine the description.

Use an invented Namibian district throughout this topic. Its government workshop makes desks and storage cabinets, its clinic provides consultations, and its public library offers reading and lending services. The workshop employs carpenters; the clinic employs nurses; the library employs librarians. A separate privately owned courier delivers purchased supplies under a contract. These examples are hypothetical arrangements for identifying economic roles, rather than claims about the staffing of an actual institution.

### Government as a producer of goods

Goods are tangible outputs. In the invented district workshop, wood, tools, premises and skilled labour are organised to make desks and cabinets. Government controls the workshop’s production, so the case describes government production of goods. Identifying the desks alone describes the output; explaining the role also identifies the organisation producing them and the resources it uses.

Producing these goods has an opportunity cost. Wood used for desks cannot simultaneously be used for another product, and carpenters’ time has alternative uses. Public ownership does not make the materials or labour unlimited. Even if a school receives a desk without paying a separate price, resources were needed to make it. Distinguish no direct payment by the recipient from no economic cost.

If government instead buys ready-made desks from the private Reed Corner Workshop, the private workshop produces those desks and government purchases them. Government funding and government production are related but different roles. A question must state who actually makes the goods before the producer can be identified. The purchase can still support public provision of education, but that does not turn the independent workshop into a government organisation.

The case may also state a production target rather than a sales target. A district workshop could be instructed to provide desks for public schools. This describes the purpose and destination of the output without proving that resources have been used efficiently. In this topic the immediate task is to recognise the production role, not assume a financial or social result absent from the evidence.

### Government as a provider of services

Services provide useful activities rather than a separately stored physical product. A clinic consultation, a lesson or a library lending service can satisfy wants while being delivered. Such output is production even though no factory makes an object. Government can organise these services through the organisations and workers it controls.

The invented library uses librarians’ time, a building, records and equipment to help users access materials. The books are goods used in providing a service; the lending and assistance are service activities. This distinction prevents an explanation from calling the whole activity goods production merely because tangible objects are involved. A clinic similarly uses equipment and supplies while providing care.

A service can be supplied without a direct user charge under a stated arrangement. That affects how access is financed, not whether scarce resources are consumed. Suppose an invented weekly library budget is N$4,000, including staff and operating resources, while users pay no lending fee. It is free at the point of use in that arrangement but still has a resource cost. The N$4,000 could have alternative uses.

Government may fund a service delivered by a private provider or provide it through its own staff. Read the case before identifying the producer and employer. Topic 6.2 considers government policy choices and financing, while this topic establishes the distinct roles. No assumption about the current legal entitlement to a particular service is needed to describe the invented provision accurately.

### Government as an employer

Government acts as an employer when it hires workers for duties in its organisations. The invented district workshop’s carpenters and library’s librarians are direct government employees because the case states who employs them. Their work helps create goods or provide services. Wages are payments for labour, while the produced output is a separate part of the activity.

An employee’s occupation alone does not identify the sector. A carpenter can work for a government workshop, a private firm or independently. A nurse can work for different kinds of provider. Establish the employment relationship rather than inferring government employment from the job title. The person or organisation making employment decisions and paying wages under the stated contract is relevant.

The private courier’s drivers remain employees of the courier when delivering supplies to the district. Government buys the delivery service from the contractor; it does not directly employ those drivers in the supplied arrangement. This boundary matters when describing the size or role of public employment. Counting everyone whose work eventually serves a public organisation would overstate direct government employment.

### Public provision, public goods and free goods

A public good in the economic sense is non-rival and non-excludable: one person’s use does not reduce availability to another, and preventing non-payers from benefiting is difficult. A free good has no opportunity cost in the relevant circumstances. Neither definition means simply something government supplies without charging the user.

The invented workshop’s desks use scarce materials and labour, so they are economic goods even if supplied without a school purchase charge. A library service also uses scarce staff time and premises. Public provision describes the institutional arrangement; classifying a public good requires the non-rivalry and non-excludability tests. Keep these tests separate from whether an organisation is public sector. Topic 1.1 owns the underlying scarcity distinction, which continues to apply when government provides the output.
