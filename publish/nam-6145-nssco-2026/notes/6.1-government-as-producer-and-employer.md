## Government as producer and employer — 6.1

### Public operations and scarce resources

The public sector comprises activities owned or controlled by government. Government can act as a producer of goods and services and as an employer of labour. These roles are connected but not identical. Producing output means combining resources to provide something useful; employing people means directly hiring them to perform work. Public employees can provide the labour needed for a service, while buildings, equipment and organisation also matter. A government paying an independent contractor for a task does not thereby directly employ the contractor’s whole workforce.

A publicly operated community facility may hire staff to maintain grounds and provide an information service. The facility provides output and employs workers. Its contribution should be assessed through the useful service and resources used, rather than merely the number hired. A service offered without a user fee still requires labour, equipment or other scarce inputs. Its opportunity cost is the alternative use of those resources. Funding it collectively changes how payment is arranged, rather than making production costless.

A public good is a specific economic category: non-rival and non-excludable. One person’s use does not reduce availability to others, and excluding non-payers is difficult. Not every government-provided service has these properties. A crowded classroom or an individual treatment can use scarce capacity. Nor does zero price make a service a free good; a free good has no opportunity cost in the relevant circumstances. This distinction links to scarcity in 1.1, government policy in 6.2 and the reasons for government intervention in 6.3. Classify by rivalry, exclusion and resource cost rather than by who pays or owns the provider alone. The funding arrangement and the economic properties of the output answer different questions and should be explained separately.

## Government as producer and employer — 6.1.1

### Government as producer

Government acts as producer when public operations combine resources to provide goods or services. The output may serve access, safety or other social objectives that are not captured by sales receipts alone. Producing a service still means using productive inputs: staff, equipment, premises and management must be available and organised. A financial loss does not by itself show a socially valuable service should cease, but a social aim does not remove the need to examine cost and effectiveness.

Consider a public information centre serving a remote community. It provides assistance that residents might otherwise have difficulty obtaining. The benefit concerns the actual service and access, not the centre’s government label. If funding is limited, spending on the centre has an opportunity cost in other possible services. Free access also does not prove the output is a public good: access and capacity characteristics must be considered separately.

Provision, funding and production can differ. Government may fund a service produced by an independent supplier, or produce it through its own operations. Read the case before assigning the producer role. A public purchase shows demand for an output, whereas direct public production means the government operation itself combines the relevant inputs.

Government produces goods and services for several reasons. Public goods, such as national defence, street lighting and police protection, would be under-provided by private firms because non-payers cannot be excluded. Merit goods, such as education and health care, are goods whose benefits to the person and to society are greater than people realise, so they would be under-consumed if left to the market; government provides them free or at a low price. Government may also run essential services, such as electricity, water, rail transport and postal services, through state-owned enterprises (parastatals). It does this to make sure the services reach every region, including areas a private firm would find unprofitable, to stop a private monopoly exploiting consumers, or to keep control of strategic industries.

Privatisation is the transfer of a state-owned enterprise, or one of its activities, to private ownership. Arguments for privatisation: the profit motive may improve efficiency, the sale raises revenue for government, competition may lower prices and improve quality, and taxpayers no longer cover losses. Arguments against: a private owner may cut unprofitable services to remote areas, a private monopoly may raise prices, workers may be made redundant, and government loses control of strategic services. Nationalisation is the reverse: the state takes an industry into public ownership. Government may subsidise a loss-making state enterprise so that it keeps an essential service or jobs, but the subsidy has an opportunity cost in other spending.

### Government as employer

Government acts as employer when it directly hires people for paid work in public operations. The employees provide labour services and receive remuneration under the relevant arrangements. Public work can involve many occupations, including administration, maintenance and specialised services. The ownership sector does not determine whether the job is manual, professional or clerical; those are separate occupational classifications.

Hiring staff can expand the ability to deliver a useful service when the right skills, equipment and organisation are available. If a public facility hires technicians but has no working equipment, employment numbers alone do not establish greater service output. Training, support and effective scheduling can affect how much satisfactory work is completed. Equally, an employee’s contribution cannot be judged from a government label without examining the task and resources. The economic comparison concerns the service benefit and the scarce resources needed.

A contractor’s staff and a public employee have different direct employers even when both work towards the same public project. This matters when describing the government as an employer rather than a purchaser. It also connects to earnings comparisons in 4.2: varied roles and conditions need a fair basis before comparing public and private rewards. Government employment can provide household income and support public output, while budget commitments and opportunity costs remain.

Government is one of the largest employers in the economy, employing teachers, nurses, police officers, soldiers, administrators and workers in state-owned enterprises. As an employer it provides public services, creates jobs in regions where private firms are few, and can reduce the number of people without work. The wages it pays become household income that is spent on goods and services, which raises demand, encourages firms to produce more and can contribute to economic growth. However, a large public wage bill must be financed by taxes or borrowing, and the money has an opportunity cost in other spending.
