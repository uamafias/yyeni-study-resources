## Market forces

### Specify the market and the period

Demand and supply describe two sides of a market. Buyers’ demand concerns the quantities they are willing and able to buy at alternative prices. Producers’ supply concerns the quantities they are willing and able to offer at those prices. State the product, the units and the period. Demand for bread rolls per week is not the same quantity as demand for bread loaves per month, so comparisons need a consistent basis.

A schedule is a set of hypothetical price-and-quantity combinations. It does not mean that every listed price applies simultaneously. It asks what quantity would be demanded or supplied at each possible price, holding the other relevant conditions unchanged. The resulting demand and supply curves summarise those relationships. Put price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, with clear labels and units. A point records a combination; a curve describes the relationship across combinations. Keeping those ideas distinct prepares the analysis of both movements along curves and shifts of them.

### Demand includes willingness and ability

Demand is the quantities consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices over a specified period, other things equal. Wanting a product without the ability to pay does not establish the same market demand as a willingness backed by purchasing power. The period and the other conditions matter because income, preferences or the availability of related products can change what buyers want at a given price.

On a conventional downward-sloping demand curve, a lower own price is associated with a larger quantity demanded, with those other conditions unchanged. This is a movement along the existing curve. It is not an increase in demand in the precise sense of the curve shifting right. For example, reading 100 rolls at N$6 and 80 rolls at N$8 from the same schedule compares two points on one relationship.

An increase in demand means buyers are willing and able to buy more at each stated price, not simply that a larger quantity is bought after a price reduction. That distinction is essential when identifying the cause of a change. Ask whether the product’s own price changed or whether some other condition changed the relationship between price and quantity. Use the curve’s position for demand and an individual point for quantity demanded.

An extension of demand is a movement to a larger quantity demanded after an own-price fall; a contraction is the reverse movement after an own-price rise. For supply, extension accompanies a higher own price and contraction a lower own price, other conditions unchanged. Use increase or decrease for a shift of the whole relationship.

### Supply refers to output offered for sale

Supply is the quantities producers are willing and able to offer at different prices over a specified period, other things equal. It concerns offers under given conditions, rather than the producer’s wishes alone. Available inputs, capacity and costs affect what can be produced and supplied. A stock of unsold products is not a complete supply schedule; the schedule describes quantities at alternative prices.

A conventional upward-sloping supply curve associates a higher price with more quantity supplied, other conditions unchanged. A higher selling price can make additional production worthwhile, but the curve assumes the relevant production conditions rather than changing them at the same time. Moving from one price-and-quantity point to another on that curve is a movement along supply.

An increase in supply instead means producers can offer more at each stated price, for example after a reduction in input costs or a suitable improvement in technology. That is a shift of the whole relationship. Do not explain an increase in supply merely by saying the product’s own price rose: that explains a larger quantity supplied along the original curve. State the changed production condition and trace how it affects output offered at each price.

### Construct an equilibrium from a schedule

Invented weekly market for bread rolls at the fictional Erongo Oven Stall in Namibia. Prices in N$ per roll: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Quantities demanded in rolls per week: 120, 100, 80, 60, 40. Quantities supplied: 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, respectively.

Plot the original demand points and supply points with price vertically and rolls per week horizontally. Label the curves D and S. At N$8, both quantities are 80, so the curves intersect at an equilibrium of N$8 and 80 rolls per week. This is the price at which the stated plans to buy and sell match, rather than the highest or lowest listed price.

At N$6, buyers demand 100 rolls while producers supply 60, giving excess demand of 40 rolls. If prices can adjust competitively, the shortage can create upward price pressure. At N$10, producers offer 100 while buyers demand 60, giving excess supply of 40. Unsold offers can create downward pressure. These are adjustment mechanisms under the model’s conditions, not a guarantee that every real market instantly clears. Equilibrium does not establish that the resulting distribution is fair or that every need is satisfied; it states the balance of market quantities at the specified price.

### Analyse a shift while holding the other curve fixed

Use the original bread-roll schedule and suppose that buyers’ preferences change. New quantities demanded at prices N$4, N$6, N$8, N$10 and N$12 are 160, 140, 120, 100 and 80. Supply stays at its original quantities. Plot a new demand curve D1 to the right of D. At the original N$8 price, buyers now want 120 rolls while only 80 are supplied. The excess demand creates upward pressure; the new intersection is N$10 and 100 rolls. The result is a shift of demand accompanied by movement along unchanged supply.

Alternatively, let demand remain original and increase supply to 80, 100, 120, 140 and 160 at those prices. Plot S1 to the right of S. At the original price there is excess supply. The new intersection is N$6 and 100 rolls: price falls and quantity increases. These two cases illustrate different causes even though both raise equilibrium quantity.

When both curves change, do not assume a price result from one curve alone. If the stated demand increase and supply increase occur together, their schedules meet at N$8 and 120 rolls. Different relative shifts can give a different price. Identify the causes, specify the curves and compare the resulting intersection rather than guessing from the word increase.

### A determinant changes a relationship

Demand determinants concern buyers: income, preferences, the prices of related goods and the number of buyers. Supply determinants concern producers: input costs, production technology, taxes or subsidies affecting production, and the number of suppliers. The distinction is about the mechanism. A rise in the price of flour raises bread-production costs and can decrease bread supply; a change in buyers’ preferences for bread concerns demand instead.

Keep a demand determinant separate from a determinant of price responsiveness. Income or preferences can shift the demand curve, while matters such as the availability of close substitutes help explain how strongly quantity demanded responds to a change in the bread roll’s own price. Saying that demand rises because it is elastic confuses position with responsiveness. Topic 3.3 develops elasticity; here the task is to identify the non-price cause that changes demand or supply at each price.

An imposed price is another distinct case. Setting a maximum price below equilibrium does not itself move D or S. It selects a price at which their quantities differ. A shortage or surplus should be shown by comparing quantities on the existing curves unless the example also specifies a separate change in a determinant.

### Trace the cause of a demand shift

For a normal good, higher household income can make buyers willing and able to purchase more at each price. That shifts demand right, other demand conditions unchanged. The normal-good condition matters: a rise in income does not increase demand for every product. Some products can become less attractive as people can afford alternatives. State the relevant assumption before drawing the curve change.

Related goods provide another mechanism. Substitutes can meet similar wants. If the price of a specified substitute rises, buyers may switch towards the product being analysed, raising its demand at each price. Complements are used together. If the price of a specified complement rises, use of the combined activity can decline and demand for the product may fall. The example must establish the relationship rather than assume any two goods are substitutes.

Preferences, population and the number of buyers can also change demand. More interested buyers can increase market demand even when each individual’s behaviour stays similar. In each explanation identify the starting cause, show its effect on willingness and ability at the same price, and then state the shift. A change in the product’s own price alone remains a movement along the curve, not one of these non-price determinants.

### Trace the cause of a supply shift

Production conditions determine the output producers can offer at each price. If flour becomes cheaper, bread costs less to make, other input and production conditions unchanged. Additional output can become worthwhile at a given selling price, so supply shifts right. A rise in input costs can work in the opposite direction, decreasing supply. The starting change is the input cost, not a claim that a higher own price shifts supply.

A suitable technology improvement can reduce waste or allow more output from the available inputs. Its effect depends on installation, skills and capacity, so do not assume that every new machine improves supply immediately. A production tax can raise the cost relevant to an additional unit, while a suitable subsidy can reduce the producer’s effective cost. Explain the actual incentive or cost change rather than treating the word government as sufficient.

The number of suppliers matters for market supply, while weather can affect the supply of an agricultural input. Again, the chain needs to match the product. A poor crop can raise flour costs and then affect bread supply; it is not directly a statement about households’ taste for bread. Link the cause to producers’ willingness or ability to offer output at each price and hold the other curve fixed unless the case says otherwise.

### Compare price, quantity and receipts

Now increase bread-production input costs. New supplied quantities at the original prices are 0, 20, 40, 60 and 80, while demand remains 120, 100, 80, 60 and 40. Plot the decreased supply curve S2 left of the original S. Its intersection with D is N$10 and 60 rolls. Compared with the original N$8 and 80 rolls, price is higher but fewer rolls are traded. This differs from the demand-increase case, which also raised price but increased quantity.

At the original equilibrium, seller receipts were 8 × 80 = N$640 per week. Under the decrease in supply they are 10 × 60 = N$600. The higher price has not established higher receipts because quantity fell. Receipts also differ from profit, which requires costs to be considered. The input-cost change therefore prevents a reliable profit judgement from price alone.

An evaluation should distinguish affected groups. Buyers face a higher price and lower available quantity. Sellers receive more per roll but sell fewer rolls and face changed costs. A demand increase instead reflects greater willingness and ability to buy and can create different opportunities for sellers. Use the actual curve change, evidence and cost conditions to judge effects rather than claiming that every price rise benefits all producers.

### Translate schedules into a clear construction

Choose scales that can show the full supplied range. On the horizontal axis use rolls per week; on the vertical axis use Namibia dollars per roll. For each row, the quantity is the horizontal coordinate and price the vertical coordinate. Thus original demand at N$4 is plotted at quantity 120 and price 4, while original supply at that price is quantity 40 and price 4. Confusing the order of these coordinates can reverse what the picture appears to show.

Plot enough supplied points to establish each relationship and label the demand and supply curves separately. Mark the intersection, then read its price across to the vertical axis and its quantity down to the horizontal axis. For a changed schedule, keep the original curves visible, use a distinct label such as D1 or S1, and identify what remains unchanged. The construction is evidence for an explanation: naming a new curve alone does not explain the cause or the adjustment. The supplied schedules are the stimulus; the original and new equilibria belong in the completed response. Show a clear arrow from the original curve towards the new curve to identify the direction of the shift.

### Planned quantities differ from actual transactions

Demand and supply at a stated price are plans to buy and offer, not proof that both quantities are actually traded. At the N$6 controlled price in the original schedule, 100 rolls are demanded but 60 offered. Without an additional source of supply, buyers cannot collectively purchase 100 rolls from those offers. The shortage describes the gap between their plans. How the available rolls are distributed is a separate question not answered by the schedule alone.

Likewise, 100 offered at N$10 and 60 demanded do not establish 100 completed sales. Excess supply identifies offers exceeding buyers’ plans at that price. Do not assume a government purchase, guaranteed sale or particular rationing arrangement unless the problem supplies it. Equilibrium is useful because the stated plans match, but it should not be confused with a general guarantee about fairness or the absence of unexpressed need. The distinction helps keep price-control interpretation grounded in the quantities actually provided.

### Do not infer the cause from price alone

A higher observed price can be consistent with several different causes. In the demand-increase example, quantity also rises; in the supply-decrease example, it falls. If both the stated demand increase and decrease in supply happen together, their schedules meet at N$12 and 80 rolls. Price rises while quantity returns to the original level, even though both underlying relationships have changed. This demonstrates why a statement such as the price rose so demand must have increased is insufficient. Establish the demand and production conditions, identify the changed schedules and only then interpret their joint result.

### Price controls and further analysis

In the original schedule, a maximum price of N$6 below equilibrium gives excess demand of 40 rolls, while a minimum price of N$10 above equilibrium gives excess supply of 40. Mark the imposed horizontal price separately from D and S; do not redraw a curve to represent the control alone.
