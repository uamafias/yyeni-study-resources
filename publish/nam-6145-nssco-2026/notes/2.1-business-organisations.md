## Business organisations — 2.1

### Ownership and the activity performed

The private sector comprises activity owned or controlled by private individuals or organisations. The public sector comprises activity owned or controlled by government. These terms classify ownership or control, not the stage of production. The sectors also differ in aim, finance and control. Private-sector firms usually aim to make a profit; public-sector organisations aim to provide services for the welfare of the public, such as water, electricity, education and health. Private firms are financed by owners’ capital, shares, loans and retained profit; public-sector organisations are financed mainly by government through taxes and borrowing, plus charges to users. Private firms are controlled by their owners or a board of directors; public-sector bodies are controlled by government, usually through a minister and an appointed board. A privately owned mine operates in the private sector and carries out primary activity. A state-owned manufacturer operates in the public sector and carries out secondary activity. A business providing services can likewise have private or public ownership. The words public company require particular care: shares being available to the public does not itself mean that government owns the business. Identify the owners or controlling organisation from the case. Do not infer ownership simply from the business being large, providing an important service or using the word public in a legal-form description.

Businesses can also be formal or informal. Formal businesses are registered, pay taxes and follow labour and licensing rules. Informal businesses, such as many street vendors and home-based traders, are not registered, usually operate on a small scale with little capital, and do not normally pay business taxes or keep formal records.

### The dimensions that distinguish forms

Compare business forms through several separate questions. Who owns the business and supplies its finance? Who controls daily decisions? Does the business have legal identity separate from its owners? What exposure do owners have to its debts? Can ownership interests be transferred, and what happens if an owner leaves? These dimensions can vary independently. A business may retain concentrated control while using an incorporated form, or have many shareholders who elect directors rather than manage daily work themselves. Limited liability concerns owners' legal exposure under the relevant rules; it does not mean that the business has no debts or that a creditor cannot lose money. A personal guarantee or a separate personal obligation can also matter.

### A form should match the decision

A repair business has one owner, modest equipment needs and customers who value the owner's personal work. Direct control and simplicity may favour sole ownership at that stage. If the owner wants a colleague's expertise and resources, joint ownership may become attractive, but the partners need an agreement about decisions and returns. If the activity requires larger commitments and a distinct legal entity, incorporation may be worth considering. The argument should use the needs in the case rather than assume that growth makes a particular form automatically best. Compare the finance obtained with the control shared, obligations accepted and risk exposure changed. A change of legal form does not by itself create customer demand or ensure that the business will earn enough to meet its commitments.

### Finance, control and risk can conflict

A wider group of owners may contribute more resources, while the original founder may prefer to retain decisions. Investors may expect information or influence in return for their funds. Formal governance and reporting can support accountability but use time and money. A suitable judgement identifies the strongest objective and the constraint that matters most. If the business cannot finance the necessary equipment from its present resources, retaining complete control may come at the cost of an expansion it cannot undertake. Alternatively, expansion may be unnecessary for a small service meeting its owners' goals. Weigh feasible alternatives and distinguish an owner benefit from a benefit to customers or workers. A recommendation must remain conditional on the actual finance, risk and management circumstances.

### Co-operation, state ownership and international scale

Not every organisation has the same purpose as a founder seeking personal profit. Members may establish a co-operative to purchase inputs or market output together. A public corporation may provide a service or pursue a strategic objective. Both still require resources and workable management. Multinational, meanwhile, identifies operations in more than one country rather than a separate universal legal form. A firm can operate across countries and also be described by its share-ownership arrangements. Exporting a product to another country does not by itself establish that the firm has productive operations there. Ask which feature the classification is describing. Keeping purpose, ownership, legal form and geographical operations separate makes comparisons more precise and avoids treating one label as a complete account of how the organisation works.

### A disciplined comparison

For a new case, identify the present arrangement, the proposed alternative and the business problem being addressed. Compare the forms using that problem rather than producing an unrelated list. Distinguish ownership from control and separate the business's obligations from owners' personal positions. Topic 2.3 develops financial institutions and raising funds, while topic 5.4 develops growth and integration. This topic concerns how ownership and organisation can serve the needs of the venture and its stakeholders. The most defensible conclusion explains why the selected form fits those needs and what change would make another option preferable. Control therefore concerns who makes decisions, while liability concerns whose assets may be used to meet debts.

## Business organisations — 2.1.2

### Sole proprietorship

A sole proprietor is an individual owning an unincorporated business and bearing its obligations personally. The business may employ workers; sole ownership does not mean that the owner works alone. An alterations service owned by one person can employ an assistant while remaining a sole proprietorship. The owner can make decisions directly and receives the residual return after costs, but also bears the consequences if the venture performs poorly. Distinguish sales receipts from what remains for the owner after expenses. A sole proprietor has unlimited liability: if the business cannot pay its debts, the owner’s personal possessions, such as a house or car, can be used to pay them. This differs from a separate incorporated entity. The nature of the activity and the obligations undertaken affect the practical risk, so a simple ownership structure should not be described as risk-free merely because its initial costs are low.

Advantages of a sole proprietorship: it is easy and cheap to set up, decisions are quick, the owner keeps all the profit, the owner has close contact with customers, and the accounts can be kept private. Disadvantages: unlimited liability, limited capital (mainly the owner’s savings and loans), long working hours, and lack of continuity because the business may close if the owner dies or falls ill.

### When one owner may suit the activity

Sole ownership can suit a small activity where the owner's skill, customer relationships and desire for control are central. Decisions can be quick because agreement with other owners is not needed. However, finance may be limited by the owner's resources and ability to obtain it. The business can also depend heavily on that person's availability and expertise. If the owner becomes unavailable, providing the same service may be difficult. Additional workers can reduce some operational dependence without changing ownership. A judgement should compare these features with the activity's needs. An owner seeking a manageable personal service may value direct control more than large-scale expansion, while one facing a major investment requirement may prefer a form that brings other contributions. The preferred form is therefore connected to objectives rather than size alone.

### Partnership

A partnership is a business owned by two or more partners (usually no more than 20), who share the capital, decisions and profits, normally under a written partnership agreement (deed). Partners can combine funds, knowledge, contacts and labour to operate the venture. Their agreement should address contributions, returns, decision-making and changes in participation. A design practice may bring together one partner with technical expertise and another with customer-management skills. The gain arises from complementary contributions rather than simply from adding names to the business. Do not assume that every partner contributes equal amounts or performs the same work unless the case says so. Legal responsibility depends on the partnership arrangement and applicable rules; a basic partnership should not be described as automatically providing the separate identity and limited liability of a company. Ordinary partners have unlimited liability, and the partnership may end when a partner dies or leaves.

Advantages of a partnership: more capital than one owner can supply, shared skills, workload and losses, and partners can cover for one another. Disadvantages: unlimited liability for ordinary partners, disagreements between partners, profits must be shared, each partner can be bound by the decisions of the others, and lack of continuity.

### Shared resources require workable agreement

Partnership can spread responsibilities and provide a wider resource base than one person could supply. A colleague's relevant skill can improve a weak part of the operation, while sharing tasks may help the business continue when one partner is absent. These benefits depend on cooperation. Disagreement about spending, workload or the use of profit can slow decisions and damage relationships. A clear agreement helps but cannot remove every possibility of conflict. Compare the resources obtained with the control shared and obligations accepted. If a repairer needs a partner's specialised skill, a partnership argument should explain that connection. Saying that partnership is better because there are more people omits both the quality of their contributions and the difficulty of coordinating them. An exit or dispute arrangement also matters when judging continuity.

### Private company

A private company is incorporated and has restricted share ownership rather than inviting unrestricted public subscription. Its separate legal identity distinguishes its rights and obligations from those of shareholders. Shares represent ownership interests, while directors manage the company within the relevant governance arrangements. A founder can remain closely involved in management, so incorporation does not automatically require distant or anonymous ownership. The restriction on public share availability distinguishes the private-company form from a public company; private here does not mean that the company must keep every activity secret. Official registration and ongoing obligations apply. A private company’s name ends with (Pty) Ltd. Its shares cannot be offered to the general public and are subject to transfer restrictions in the company’s articles. Advantages: limited liability, more capital than a partnership, continuity if a shareholder leaves, and the founders can keep control. Disadvantages: legal registration costs and formalities, capital is limited because shares cannot be sold to the public, and shares are not easy to sell.

### Separate identity and limited liability

Separate legal identity allows the company to hold rights and incur obligations as an entity distinct from its shareholders. Limited liability generally restricts shareholders' exposure for company debts to the obligations associated with their investment, subject to applicable rules and any separate commitments. It does not prevent a shareholder losing the money invested. Nor does it ensure that the company can pay its creditors. An owner who makes a separate personal guarantee may have an additional obligation. In an equipment business, incorporation may therefore matter to how obligations are organised, while lenders still assess the company's ability to repay. Compare legal exposure with commercial performance rather than claiming that the form supplies an automatic financial return. Reporting and administration can also impose costs that need to be considered against the benefits of incorporation.

### Public company

A public company can offer shares to the public, subject to the applicable rules. This can widen access to equity finance compared with relying on a small invited ownership group. Shareholders own the company and receive part of the profit as dividends. They elect a board of directors, which controls and manages the company on their behalf. Wider share availability does not identify the government as owner. A public company can be part of the private sector. Nor does the word public alone establish that the company's shares are currently listed on a particular exchange. Distinguish the legal ability to offer shares from the separate arrangements for trading them. A manufacturer planning a substantial expansion might consider a wider ownership base, but the prospect of raising funds depends on investors' willingness to invest and the information available to them.

A public company’s name ends with Ltd, and its shares can be sold to the public, often through the Namibian Stock Exchange (NSX). Advantages: limited liability attracts investors, very large amounts of capital can be raised, shares are easy to transfer, and the company continues when shareholders change. Disadvantages: it is expensive and complex to set up, accounts must be published, ownership is separated from control, and there is a risk of takeover because anyone can buy shares.

### Ownership, control and wider finance

Wider share ownership can provide resources for an activity and make it easier for some owners to transfer their interests. It can also separate ownership from daily control because a large group of shareholders cannot each run the business. Directors then make decisions on the company's behalf and shareholders need suitable accountability arrangements. Additional reporting and governance can be costly but can provide information to investors. The original owners may have to share influence, and a change in the ownership pattern can affect control. When evaluating a public-company option, compare the capital requirement with the reporting burden and the owners' priorities. Do not assume that investors will buy shares merely because they are offered. The quality of the venture, expected return and perceived risk affect the finance actually obtainable.

### Multinational company

A multinational company has productive operations in more than one country. The label describes geographical operations rather than a universal legal form. A company can be a multinational and also have particular private or public share arrangements. A food processor with factories in two countries illustrates cross-border production. Selling exports from a single domestic factory, by contrast, does not by itself establish productive operations abroad. A business may expand internationally to reach customers, obtain inputs or organise stages of production in different locations. Those motives need to be connected to its activity. Different markets, transport arrangements, workforce capabilities and production conditions can influence the decision. International scale does not make the business immune to cost problems or prove that it benefits every country where it operates.

### Cross-border benefits and trade-offs

A host economy may gain employment, training, purchases from local suppliers or access to production knowledge from an international business. The size of those gains depends on how the operation uses local resources and the terms on which it operates. Some returns may leave the host economy, and local firms may face stronger competition. Decisions taken elsewhere may also affect local employment. For the company, operating in several countries can widen markets but creates coordination, information and management challenges. A comparison should identify whose interests are being considered: the owners, workers, customers or the host community. Avoid concluding that foreign operations are necessarily harmful or beneficial from the label alone. Use the actual activity, local links and alternative opportunities to judge the likely effects, and keep geographical scale separate from legal ownership classification.

### Co-operative

A co-operative is owned by members to pursue common economic interests through joint activity. Producers may cooperate to buy inputs, share facilities or market output, while consumers may organise to obtain goods or services for members. A group of growers could jointly use a packing facility that each would struggle to finance alone. Members' interests and participation are central to the purpose, rather than an assumption that one outside owner seeks the residual profit. Membership is voluntary, and each member usually has one vote whatever the size of the contribution (democratic control). Profits (surpluses) are shared among members, usually according to how much each bought from or supplied to the co-operative. Common types are producer co-operatives (for example farmers marketing crops together), consumer or retail co-operatives, and savings and credit co-operatives. For an economic comparison, explain how ownership by members connects to the shared service. A co-operative still needs resources, effective decisions and enough receipts or contributions to cover its activity. Mutual purpose does not remove scarcity or the need for financial discipline.

### Joint activity can help and constrain members

Combining members' purchases can improve their ability to obtain inputs, while shared equipment may spread costs over a larger volume of activity. Joint marketing can also make it easier to meet an order that would be too large for one member. These benefits depend on reliable contributions and suitable management. Members may disagree about prices, investment or the distribution of benefits, and reaching collective decisions can take time. Raising additional finance can also be difficult if available contributions are limited. A growers' co-operative should therefore be evaluated through its members' needs, the shared facility's use and the arrangements for paying and managing it. A useful judgement recognises the value of cooperation while asking whether the organisation can maintain quality, timely supply and fair participation under the stated constraints.

### Close corporation

In Namibia, a close corporation is a legal entity separate from its members and generally provides limited liability under the applicable law. Membership ownership distinguishes it from a sole proprietor's unincorporated activity. The entity's legal position also means that its obligations should not simply be treated as the personal obligations of its members because they belong to it. The protection is subject to the law and does not remove separate personal commitments or consequences of conduct for which members can be liable. A close corporation (CC) is registered under the Close Corporations Act with a founding statement. It has between one and ten members, who are normally natural persons (individuals), subject to the Act’s limited representative exceptions, and its name ends with CC. It suits small businesses that want limited liability without the formalities of a company. A small maintenance business may consider the form because the owners want to organise the venture separately from their personal affairs.

### A form is not a substitute for sound operation

A close corporation can be attractive where a small ownership group wants a distinct legal entity, but the business still needs customers, finance and workable management. Incorporation brings obligations rather than making administration disappear. Limited liability concerns how legal exposure is organised; the members can still lose invested resources if the venture performs poorly. A lender may also require information or additional security. Compare the form with the owners' objectives and circumstances rather than treating it as an automatic next step for every sole proprietor. If an existing business changes form, consider whether the change addresses a real need, such as organising several owners' participation or separating the entity's affairs.

### Public corporation

A public corporation is owned or controlled by the state and established for public purposes. Its objectives may include supplying an essential service, extending access or carrying out a strategic activity. It can charge for output and employ productive resources, so public purpose does not imply that its activity is costless. Distinguish the public corporation from a public company whose shares are available to private investors. The ownership and purpose need to be identified from the case. In an example, a state-controlled regional transport organisation may be required to maintain routes that are important for access but would not cover their costs at affordable fares. The arrangement can make wider objectives explicit, while creating a need to decide how the required resources are financed and accounted for.

### Public purpose and accountability

A public corporation can coordinate a service across areas or pursue an access goal not fully reflected in paying demand. However, its resources have alternative uses, and weak cost control or poor service can impose a burden on users and public finances. Clear objectives, reliable information and accountability matter. A judgement should compare performance against the stated purpose, not assume that profit is the sole criterion or that losses prove the service has no benefit. Equally, a public objective does not justify avoidable waste. For a transport service, examine the access gained, cost of delivery, feasible alternatives and quality achieved. Topic 6.1 develops government as producer and employer. The ownership form identifies who controls the organisation; explaining whether it serves its purpose requires evidence about what it actually does.
