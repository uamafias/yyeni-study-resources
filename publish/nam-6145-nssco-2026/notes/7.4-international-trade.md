## International trade — 7.4

### Trade patterns and external accounts

Exports are sales of goods or services to buyers abroad, while imports are purchases from abroad. Patterns can change in product composition, trading partners or values over time. A rise in the money value of exports may reflect quantity, price or exchange effects, so it should not automatically be described as more physical output. State the measure and period before explaining the change. Product demand, production capacity, costs and international conditions can influence those patterns.

The goods balance is goods export value minus goods import value. In an invented period, exports N$90 million and imports N$110 million give a goods deficit of N$20 million. This is not the entire balance of payments. Services, relevant income and transfers as well as financial flows concern other international transactions. Investment funds entering an economy are not a service export simply because money arrives from abroad. A foreign investor financing a new workshop and a foreign customer paying for a repair service are different transactions. This distinction preserves the financial-flow meaning sometimes described with legacy capital-account language in older material. A wider external assessment needs the relevant accounts and financing evidence rather than treating a goods deficit as the whole result. An export pattern can change even if the overall goods balance stays the same. For instance, a decline in one product’s receipts may be offset by another’s rise. The composition matters to exposure and capacity even when the net number is unchanged. A description should therefore use the disaggregated information supplied, while a causal explanation links product demand or supply to the relevant change.

### Changing exchange rates

An exchange rate expresses one currency’s value in another. Under market determination, a higher demand for a currency relative to its supply can raise its value, while a larger relative supply can lower it. Buyers of exports may need the exporter’s currency, and residents purchasing imports may supply domestic currency to obtain foreign money. Financial investment, interest incentives and expectations can also affect currency demand and supply. These mechanisms operate together, so one trade change alone does not prove the final rate movement.

For an invented market-determined currency, greater foreign demand for local products may increase currency demand if transactions require conversion. The resulting appreciation is conditional on the other influences, not a guaranteed response of every rate. A rise in relative interest returns may attract funds where risk and expectations support it, but investors make their own decisions. Namibia’s N$/rand parity is an institutional arrangement described in topic 2.3, so these market examples should not be read as a freely floating bilateral N$/rand forecast. A depreciation can raise domestic costs of unchanged foreign-priced imports. Whether it improves the current account depends on sufficiently responsive trade demand and whether supply can respond, as well as time and input costs. Keep the rate quote and response conditions visible. A quotation should be read before interpreting appreciation or depreciation. More domestic currency per foreign unit means the domestic unit is weaker against that foreign currency, not stronger because the printed number is larger. Foreign units per domestic unit use the opposite quotation direction. Topic 2.3 develops conversion; here the reason for a fluctuation must be kept separate from the arithmetic description of it.

### Two advantage tests

Absolute advantage compares production efficiency using a common resource basis. Comparative advantage compares the alternative output forgone per unit. A side can be better at producing both goods in absolute terms yet still have a higher opportunity cost in one. The distinction explains why looking only at who makes more of everything does not settle the opportunity-cost pattern.

Use two invented producers with the same resource allocation. Producer A can make either 30 cloth units or 15 grain units; B can make either 12 cloth or 12 grain. These are alternative maxima, not simultaneous output. A has absolute advantage in both because 30 exceeds 12 and 15 exceeds 12. But A’s cloth costs 15/30=0.5 grain per cloth, compared with B’s 12/12=1. A has comparative advantage in cloth. A’s grain costs 30/15=2 cloth per grain, while B’s costs 1, so B has comparative advantage in grain. The ratios identify the good forgone and the good produced; a number without these units can be inverted accidentally.

### Gains and limits

With different opportunity costs, specialisation and exchange can create potential gains under suitable terms. In the example, exchanging one cloth for 0.75 grain lies between the cloth opportunity costs 0.5 and 1 grain. A receives more grain for its cloth than its own forgone grain, while B obtains cloth for less grain than producing it itself would require. The calculation illustrates a possible mutually beneficial exchange, not a prediction of actual national trade terms.

Transport, quality, adjustment and other resource costs can reduce or offset gains. Resources may not move immediately between uses, and dependence on a narrow activity can create risks. A supported judgement states the feasible production and trade assumptions. This links to opportunity cost in 1.1 and specialisation in 1.3. Absolute output differences, comparative ratios, transaction flows and a country’s wider external position are related questions, but they are not interchangeable measures.

## International trade — 7.4.3

### Absolute advantage from equal resources

To use output data for absolute advantage, first establish a common input basis. When the same resources can produce more of a good at A than B, A has the absolute advantage in that good. If resource amounts differ, greater output alone may simply reflect greater input. Alternatively, data about resources needed for the same output identify advantage through lower requirements. Read which form the question supplies before comparing numbers.

For the invented alternative maxima, A produces 30 cloth against B’s 12 using equal resources, an output difference of 18 cloth. It produces 15 grain against B’s 12, a difference of 3 grain. A therefore has absolute advantage in both. The difference has physical units; it is not a comparative-advantage coefficient. The question might award one result mark for a difference even though the learning explanation shows the condition and classification. Do not infer that the alternatives can be produced together at those maxima. Each is what the resources could make if directed to that good. This common-resource assumption permits a fair output comparison and supports the opportunity-cost calculation in the next block. Without it, an explanation should state the missing basis rather than assign an advantage from raw totals. If the data instead give hours required per unit, the lower input requirement establishes absolute advantage. A larger number is then worse rather than better. This contrast shows why reading the table heading is part of the economic method, not a detail to skip before comparing figures. Output maxima and input requirements are different data forms.

### Comparative advantage from opportunity cost

Comparative advantage belongs to the producer with the lower opportunity cost of the good. For alternative maximum outputs, divide the other good forgone by output of the target good. A can produce 30 cloth or 15 grain, so one cloth costs 0.5 grain. B can produce 12 of either, so one cloth costs 1 grain. A sacrifices less grain per cloth and has comparative advantage in cloth. Reverse the target for grain: A sacrifices 2 cloth per grain, B1, giving B comparative advantage in grain.

The numerator and denominator must travel with the units. Dividing cloth by grain answers cost of grain in cloth, not cost of cloth in grain. Both ratios are useful but answer different questions. Comparing absolute output alone would miss B’s relative advantage despite its lower maxima in both goods. To apply the result, explain which opportunity is forgone and why the lower sacrifice supports specialisation. Potential trade gains require feasible exchange terms and relevant costs, not merely a low ratio on paper. If opportunity costs were equal, this simple two-good model would not show the same comparative-cost basis for gains. The numerical distinction links back to the parent example: efficient output, relative sacrifice and the actual terms of trade should be stated separately so a learner can test each rather than use the labels as synonyms. Check the reciprocal interpretation with both goods. A’s 0.5 grain per cloth corresponds to 2 cloth per grain, while B’s one-for-one opportunity costs are reciprocal ones. This confirms what each ratio measures without substituting a check for the explanation of comparative advantage. The conclusion remains lower sacrifice of the other product for the chosen good. A clear answer names the target good, the alternative forgone and the producer with the lower ratio.
