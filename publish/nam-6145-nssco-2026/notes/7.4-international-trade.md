## International trade

### Trade patterns and the external accounts

Exports sell output to other countries; imports buy output from them. A trade pattern describes the products, trading partners and values or quantities involved. A rise in export value can reflect higher prices, more units or both. It does not necessarily establish greater real production or improved profitability. Similarly, more imports can include productive equipment as well as consumer goods, so interpretation needs their composition.

Use invented annual goods values in N$ million. Exports rise from 80 to 100 while imports rise from 90 to 120. Goods trade moves from a deficit of 10 to a deficit of 20 despite export growth. Comparing exports alone misses the faster import increase. The overall balance of payments also includes services, income, transfers and financial transactions, so this goods deficit is not a complete external account.

An overseas investor acquiring a lasting ownership interest in an invented domestic workshop undertakes foreign direct investment. Its funding is a financial-account transaction, not simply a current-account export. The workshop’s later sale of goods abroad is a separate export transaction. Keeping flows distinct prevents external financing being mistaken for income from selling current output.

Trade protection can influence patterns. A tariff taxes imports, while a quota limits their quantity under the stated rule. Both can reduce competitive pressure on some domestic producers but may raise buyer costs or affect input access. Topic 9.1 evaluates the conflicting interests and longer-term consequences; identify the mechanism here without assuming protection guarantees jobs or external stability.

Changes in trading partners can also alter exposure. A producer selling most output to one foreign market may be vulnerable to that buyer’s demand conditions. Selling to several markets can spread some risk, although transport, standards and marketing costs may rise. Diversification is therefore a change in pattern with a possible benefit, not proof that every additional partner improves the external balance.

The trade values must use the same currency and period. An export quantity can fall while its money value rises if prices increase enough. For example, 100 units at N$20 yield N$2,000, while 80 at N$30 yield N$2,400. Higher receipts here coexist with fewer units sold. The value table alone cannot establish the associated domestic employment or resource use.

### Exchange-rate causes and conditional effects

State the quotation first. If US$1 costs N$18 and later N$20, more Namibia dollars are needed for the same US dollar, so the Namibia dollar has depreciated against that third currency. Under the maintained one-to-one rand peg, this is not an independent N$/rand depreciation. Actual Namibia examples must preserve that distinction, as documented by the [Bank of Namibia](https://www.bon.com.na/Informations/FAQ/Roles-of-the-Bank.aspx).

Currency demand can rise when foreign buyers purchase a country’s exports or investors seek its assets. Currency supply can rise when residents need foreign money for imports or overseas assets. In a hypothetical floating system, stronger demand for a currency with other conditions unchanged can raise its price; increased supply can lower it. Relative interest returns, expected inflation and confidence can influence these flows, but no factor determines the rate in isolation. The actual rand-peg framework constrains the Namibia case.

A third-currency depreciation can make an unchanged foreign-currency import price more expensive domestically. An invented US$100 input costs N$1,800 at the first quotation and N$2,000 at the second. Exporters receiving dollars may receive more Namibia dollars for the same dollar sale, but imported inputs may also become dearer. A lower foreign-currency price can stimulate export quantities only if buyers respond and firms can supply.

The trade balance is therefore not guaranteed to improve. Existing contracts may fix quantities or prices for a time, essential imports may be hard to reduce and export capacity may be limited. A firm with spare capacity and local inputs can respond differently from an importer with no substitute. Identify whose receipts and costs change, then assess the quantity response and period rather than drawing a conclusion from the currency direction alone.

Appreciation against a third currency reverses the direct conversion effect: fewer domestic currency units buy the same foreign amount. This can ease an importer’s cost while reducing converted receipts from a fixed foreign-currency export sale. The competitive effect still depends on prices offered, inputs used and demand. A common currency movement can therefore benefit one business while creating a challenge for another, even within the same economy and maintained rand-peg framework.

### Calculate the sacrifice before recommending specialisation

Use two invented producers, A and B, each with its own fixed resource bundle for one period. A can make either 12 mats or 6 baskets; B can make either 4 mats or 4 baskets. Assume constant opportunity costs, comparable quality, divisible resource use and no transport costs. A produces more of either good from its bundle, so it has absolute advantage in both. That does not prevent mutually useful specialisation.

For A, one mat sacrifices 6/12, or half a basket. For B, one mat sacrifices 4/4, or one basket. A has comparative advantage in mats because its sacrifice is lower. Reverse the ratios for baskets: A sacrifices 2 mats per basket; B sacrifices 1. B has comparative advantage in baskets despite lower absolute output. The relevant comparison changes with the concept being tested.

At full specialisation, A makes 12 mats and B 4 baskets. Suppose they exchange 3 mats for 2 baskets, equivalent to 1.5 mats per basket. A obtains each imported basket for fewer mats than the 2 it would sacrifice producing it. B receives more mats per basket than the 1 it could make by sacrificing a basket itself. Both gain access to more favourable exchange than their own opportunity costs.

After that trade A has 9 mats and 2 baskets; B has 3 mats and 2 baskets. These combinations lie beyond what each could produce alone under its schedule. This is not a claim that full specialisation raises every good’s aggregate output from any chosen starting allocation. The gain is demonstrated through feasible consumption and the trade terms. Transport costs, imperfect mobility, different quality or changing opportunity costs can reduce the simple model’s benefit.

Check the individual opportunity explicitly. To produce 2 baskets alone, A would sacrifice 4 mats and have only 8 mats left; trade leaves it 9 mats with the same 2 baskets. B producing 3 mats alone would sacrifice 3 baskets and have only 1 basket left; trade leaves it 2 baskets with those 3 mats. Each obtains a combination unavailable from its own resources alone. This comparison supplies evidence of a potential gain without assuming both would prefer an identical consumption mix.

### Absolute advantage: compare resource productivity

Absolute advantage means producing more with the same resources or requiring fewer resources for the same output. In the invented maximum-output schedule, A’s 12 mats exceed B’s 4 and A’s 6 baskets exceed B’s 4. The comparison uses matched resource bundles and periods. Comparing different-sized countries’ raw totals without considering resources would not establish productive efficiency in this sense.

The word absolute concerns the output or resource requirement, not the sacrifice of the alternative good. It answers a useful question but cannot replace the comparative calculation. A can be absolutely better in both activities while B sacrifices less of one good to make the other. Choosing trade specialisation from the bigger output number alone would miss that relative difference.

### Comparative advantage: compare opportunity costs

Comparative advantage means producing at a lower opportunity cost. Express the sacrifice in units of the other good, not as a bare ratio. In the invented schedule, A’s cost of one mat is half a basket, while B’s is one basket. For one basket the costs are 2 mats and 1 mat respectively, so B has the basket advantage. Label both the good being produced and the alternative sacrificed to avoid reversing the result.

Trade terms need to fall between the relevant opportunity costs for both to gain in this simple case. At 1.5 mats per basket, A pays less than its own 2 and B receives more than its own 1. Terms at a boundary leave one party with no strict improvement from that unit exchange; terms outside can disadvantage one side. Comparative advantage supports a conditional opportunity for trade, not an obligation to accept any offered price.
