## Conflicts

### A judgement needs the people, periods and alternatives

Economic choices can create conflicts because resources have competing uses and costs and benefits fall on different people. A business may gain profit while neighbours bear pollution. A consumer may gain from cheaper imports while a domestic producer loses sales. A government project may provide a useful shared service while taxes reduce private purchasing power. Identifying the conflict means naming the groups and the mechanism, not merely saying there are advantages and disadvantages.

Use an invented Reedplain community in Namibia. A basket workshop harvests reeds, employs workers and sells output. Nearby residents use the wetland’s services, while future producers may also need the resource. The community is considering a public access path and a temporary import tariff on competing baskets. Each choice has more than one dimension: current livelihoods, future resource use, buyer prices and the alternative uses of public funds.

Start with the relevant alternative. Stopping harvest entirely is different from limiting it to a sustainable amount; spending on a path is different from leaving the same funds for private purchases or using them for water infrastructure. A recommendation should compare feasible options and explain the condition changing the choice. Acknowledging a cost does not require abandoning every benefit, but a claim of net benefit needs the costs within the chosen boundary.

### Private, external and social: keep the accounting boundary clear

Private costs are borne by the decision-maker, such as the workshop’s labour and material expenses. Private benefits accrue to it, such as receipts from its product. External costs fall on third parties outside the transaction and are not fully reflected in the private decision. Damage to neighbouring gardens from the workshop’s waste is an external cost in the stated example. Social cost combines the private and external components rather than replacing one with the other.

Use invented comparable one-period values in N$. Private benefit is 12,000 and private cost 8,000, so the private net benefit is 4,000. External damage costs 5,000 and no external benefit is stated. Social cost is 13,000 and social net benefit is negative 1,000. The profitable-looking private decision does not show a positive social result. Adding the external damage to the private benefit instead would reverse its meaning.

Suppose a feasible cleaner method raises private cost to 9,000 but reduces external damage to 1,000, with benefit still 12,000. Private net benefit falls to 3,000, but social net benefit rises to 2,000. This illustrates a conflict between the workshop’s preferred cost and the wider outcome. The calculation assumes the stated figures include the relevant effects on a comparable basis; unvalued effects remain a limitation rather than something to invent.

A justified conclusion can favour the cleaner option if the figures and feasibility hold. It should still consider who pays for the change and whether the affected households actually receive protection. A positive total does not establish that every group gains. Compensation or another implementation arrangement can change the distribution, but the possibility of compensation is different from evidence that it occurs.

### A present benefit can carry a future cost

Short-term effects occur relatively soon; long-term effects arise over a more extended period relevant to the decision. There is no single number of months defining the distinction for every choice. A harvest can provide income immediately, while depletion affects future production. A training project can cost time and funds now but support later skills. The judgement should state the effect and period rather than attach short or long labels without explanation.

At Reedplain, a larger harvest can raise this season’s basket income. If it damages regeneration, later harvests may be smaller and households may lose both work and wetland services. The short-term gain is real but not sufficient to establish a favourable lasting outcome. Conversely, a temporary harvest limit can reduce earnings now while preserving future opportunities, depending on the ecological and livelihood facts supplied.

The public path has a different time pattern. Construction can disrupt access and require immediate tax funding, while later reliable transport can reduce delivery costs. If the path is poorly maintained or rarely used, the anticipated later gain may not materialise. Future benefits are uncertain, so a proposal needs a credible use and maintenance plan rather than an optimistic promise.

Evaluate feasible adjustment. Phasing a change can reduce immediate hardship, while training can help workers move into alternative tasks. These measures use resources and may not be available instantly. The condition supporting a recommendation could be successful regeneration, affordable transition support or reliable future use. A balanced judgement identifies the decisive condition and distinguishes it from an outcome already demonstrated.

### Conserve functions and options while assessing current use

Exploiting a resource means using it. Overexploitation means using it beyond sustainable limits under the relevant conditions. The words are not synonyms. A renewable reedbed may support repeated harvest if extraction, regeneration and damage are managed appropriately. A non-renewable mineral stock is reduced by extraction even when operations are carefully managed; conserving it can preserve future options but forgo current income.

Conservation can protect a future supply, biodiversity, water-related services or opportunities for later uses. Exploitation can provide present materials, jobs and receipts. The conflict concerns the timing and distribution of these benefits, not a choice between an activity with benefits and one with none. Households relying on current harvesting may bear substantial hardship from an immediate complete closure.

In an invented case, monitoring shows that the proposed intensive reed harvest would exceed regeneration and damage the wetland. A lower managed harvest is feasible and supports some livelihoods. The evidence supports the managed alternative more strongly than unrestricted extraction, provided monitoring and compliance work. A complete ban could preserve more of the resource but sacrifices the livelihood output that the sustainable option retains.

If the case instead establishes irreversible damage even at the proposed lower harvest, the judgement can change. A temporary limit is not enough merely because it is called managed. Likewise, a claim that jobs will be replaced elsewhere requires actual opportunities and a feasible transition. Compare the resource’s future functions, the current users’ needs and the practical alternatives before recommending conservation or use.

### Trade creates different gains and adjustment costs

Free trade can widen choice, allow lower-cost supply and increase competitive pressure. Comparative advantage can support mutually useful specialisation under the conditions in topic 7.4. Consumers and firms using imported inputs may benefit from lower prices or access. Some domestic producers can lose sales, however, and their workers may face adjustment costs before alternative work is found.

Protection through a tariff or quota can give some domestic producers room to develop or adjust. The case is stronger if there is a credible temporary need, achievable improvement and a way to review results. Protection that persists without productive progress can keep buyer costs high and weaken the incentive to become more efficient. Other countries may respond, affecting exporters as well as the protected industry.

At Reedplain, an invented temporary basket tariff may support local producers’ sales, but buyers pay more and a downstream seller may face higher input costs. The producer gain does not establish a gain for the whole community. Nor does a cheaper imported basket erase the hardship of a displaced worker. Both groups’ effects need consideration.

The conditional judgement should compare the value of adjustment support with the cost to buyers and others. A time-limited measure with credible training and efficiency targets may be defensible under supplied conditions. Where domestic capacity cannot improve and buyers bear large costs, open trade with targeted adjustment assistance can be stronger. This topic owns the conflict and time judgement; topic 7.4 explains the trade and exchange mechanisms.

### Public and private spending compete for resources

Public spending can provide infrastructure or services benefiting several users. Private spending allows households and firms to pursue their own consumption and investment priorities. Neither category is inherently costless or efficient. Tax funding can reduce funds available privately; public borrowing creates financing commitments. The opportunity cost is the useful alternative forgone, not simply the money changing hands.

Consider an invented N$100,000 budget for the Reedplain path. If it is tax-funded, the comparison includes the private purchases reduced by that tax and any other feasible public project displaced. A path used regularly by several producers can have wider access benefits. If use is slight and maintenance is unfunded, keeping funds for higher-valued alternatives may be preferable.

Do not count the whole tax payment as an additional resource cost on top of the same construction resources without checking the accounting. Taxation finances the project and changes purchasing power; labour and materials are real inputs. Distribution still matters: some households may pay while others use the path most. A net community benefit can coexist with individual losses.

A recommendation should therefore explain what the spending produces, who benefits and pays, and what is forgone. Funded maintenance, reliable use and access for the intended users strengthen the case. A private alternative might serve only paying customers, while a public project could offer broader access; the particular arrangements decide the comparison. The choice should follow evidence about value and feasibility rather than ownership labels alone.
