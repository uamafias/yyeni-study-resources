## Conflicts — 9.1

### Whose gains, which costs and when?

Economic choices can create conflicts of interest because gains and costs fall on different people or arise at different times. A firm may benefit from output while neighbours experience noise; residents may gain current jobs while future users face resource depletion. A supported judgement identifies these effects rather than assume the decision-maker’s profit represents the whole community’s net outcome. Social costs include private plus external costs, and the same broad distinction applies to benefits.

Time and resources complicate the comparison. Conservation can preserve future options but may reduce current income opportunities. Exploitation means using a resource, not necessarily using it excessively. Evaluate the rate, method and consequences of use. Trade protection can assist particular producers while raising buyer costs; a tariff taxes imports, whereas a quota limits quantity. Public and private expenditure involve different decisions and financing, but both commit scarce resources. The appropriate choice depends on the specific need, available alternatives and effective delivery.

A council considering a processing site should identify jobs and output, the firm’s resource costs, effects on nearby residents and future environmental consequences. It should compare feasible ways to reduce harm rather than treat the project as either costless progress or harm without benefit. State which effects are evidenced and which depend on assumptions. This links to opportunity cost in 1.1, government intervention in 6.3 and living standards in 8.2. The task is not to force identical preferences onto all groups, but to make a reasoned decision that recognises the relevant interests, distribution, period and limits of the evidence.

## Conflicts — 9.1.1

### Private, external and social effects

Private costs and benefits accrue directly to the decision-maker. A firm pays for materials and labour and receives relevant gains from its activity. External effects fall on third parties outside the transaction, such as residents affected by a production process without choosing to buy or supply that output. Social cost includes the private cost and external cost; social benefit includes private and external benefit. Social does not mean government spending, and external does not mean a cost physically outside the country.

In a simple project, private cost N$80,000 and external cost N$20,000 give social cost N$100,000. Calling the N$20,000 social cost would omit resources already counted privately. If a feasible change reduces external harm but adds a private cost, compare both before judging the net effect. A third-party benefit can also occur, such as an activity helping others without receiving the whole reward. External costs include air and water pollution, noise, traffic congestion and damage to land. External benefits include the gains to society when a person is educated or vaccinated, when a firm trains workers who later move to other firms, or when a new road built for a mine also serves nearby residents. The classification depends on the relationship to the decision and transaction, not whether the effect is desirable. Numerical estimates need a stated scope and basis, since not all effects are easily priced. If values are absent, explain the direction and the affected groups rather than invent a total net benefit. The framework helps reveal a conflict; the practical judgement also considers evidence, feasible alternatives and distribution. A cost can be real even when no bill is sent to the decision-maker. Lost quiet enjoyment or harm requiring resources can matter outside the firm’s accounts. Conversely, a payment already included privately should not be added again as though it were a separate external cost. Clear scope prevents both omission and duplication.

### Effects over different periods

Short-term and long-term costs and benefits distinguish when effects arise. They do not indicate that immediate effects matter and later effects can be ignored, or that long-term outcomes are automatically favourable. A maintenance programme may disrupt production now but prevent later breakdowns. Conversely, avoiding maintenance can save cash immediately while increasing later risk. State the mechanism connecting the present choice with the future outcome and the assumptions supporting it.

A training programme uses worker time and resources this month. It may later improve satisfactory output or access to skilled work if the training is appropriate and used. That possible future benefit is not already achieved merely because the programme starts. The comparison needs a suitable horizon and evidence about implementation. Different groups can also experience effects at different times: current workers might bear adjustment while future users gain a better service. A judgement should make that distribution visible. Short-run and long-run in this conflict discussion refer to the timing of effects; do not silently substitute the separate production-theory distinction about which inputs can adjust. The meaning follows the question. A supported decision can acknowledge uncertain future gains while identifying the evidence that would make them credible. It should not use distant promises to erase a known current cost or use a current saving to ignore a plausible later burden. Evidence about future effects should identify the causal link and uncertainty. A possible later benefit can support a conditional case, while a measured present effect can be stated more directly. Distinguishing those confidence levels makes the evaluation more useful than treating all claimed numbers or promises as equally established.

### Conservation and economic use

Conservation protects or manages resources to preserve useful capacity or future availability. Exploitation means using resources for economic purposes. The terms can conflict but are not automatic opposites in the sense that any use destroys a resource. The resource type, rate, method and effects matter. A renewable resource used within a sustainable management arrangement differs from use exceeding regeneration. A non-renewable resource also involves depletion choices and alternative future uses.

A community may obtain current income from a resource-based activity while valuing future access and environmental services. Restricting use can preserve options but reduce immediate output or work; excessive or damaging use can impose future costs or harm other people now. The comparison should identify who gains, who bears adjustment and which alternatives are feasible. A management approach might reduce harm while permitting useful output, but its effectiveness requires evidence rather than a label such as sustainable. Governments conserve resources through fishing and hunting quotas, licences, closed seasons, protected areas, taxes or fines on overuse and pollution, and support for recycling and renewable energy. Arguments for exploiting a resource now: jobs, incomes, export earnings and tax revenue. Arguments for conserving it: income and access for future generations, tourism, biodiversity and avoiding the collapse of a stock such as a fishery. This links resource choice to opportunity cost and external effects. Evaluate the specific use and time horizon instead of assuming exploiting means abusing or conserving means producing nothing. A supported judgement states the condition under which continued use is acceptable and what evidence is needed to monitor it. A management judgement also needs the relevant boundary. Protecting one site while shifting the same harm elsewhere may not produce the intended wider gain. State the affected resource and people rather than assume a local improvement proves that total harm fell.

### Free trade and protection

Free trade permits international exchange without protectionist barriers, while protection uses measures to restrict imports or shield domestic producers. A tariff is a tax on imports. A quota is a quantity restriction rather than a tax, so it is a non-tariff measure. These instruments can affect prices, quantities, government receipts and who obtains access differently. Naming a barrier is not enough to explain its consequences; identify how it changes a decision.

Protection may give selected domestic firms time or opportunity to develop and can support some jobs where import competition is strong. Buyers may face higher prices or fewer choices, and domestic users of imported inputs may face higher costs. Other countries’ responses or reduced competitive pressure can create additional disadvantages. Benefits are not necessarily shared across all workers or firms. A claim for temporary support also needs a credible reason that the activity can become viable rather than remain dependent indefinitely. Free trade can support specialisation and choice, but adjustment costs can fall on particular groups. This links to comparative advantage in 7.4: potential aggregate gains do not imply painless or equal outcomes. Evaluate the stated objective, affected groups, duration and alternatives without assuming one instrument solves every problem. A tax and a quantity limit should remain distinct even if both aim to reduce imports.

Methods of protection include tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (limits on the quantity imported), embargoes (bans on specified imports), subsidies to domestic producers, exchange controls and strict administrative standards. Reasons for protection include helping infant industries grow until they can compete, protecting domestic jobs (a tariff makes imports dearer, so buyers switch to local goods, raising demand for local labour), preventing dumping of goods below cost, raising government revenue from tariffs, reducing a trade deficit, and protecting strategic industries such as food or energy. Free trade gives consumers lower prices and wider choice, lets countries specialise according to comparative advantage, increases competition and efficiency, and opens export markets. Protection risks retaliation by other countries, higher prices and less efficient domestic firms.

A domestic producer subsidy can reduce effective cost per unit. If the firm passes the saving to buyers, it can offer a lower price against imports. This uses government resources and does not guarantee efficiency or extra sales.

### Public and private expenditure

Public expenditure is spending by government; private expenditure is spending by households or privately controlled organisations. The distinction concerns the decision-maker, not whether an item is socially useful. A private household can buy something beneficial, while a public programme can fail to meet its aim. Conversely, collective provision may support access or outputs that private decisions alone do not adequately provide. Evidence of need, incentives, financing and delivery determines the comparison.

A public transport proposal may improve access for residents who would struggle to purchase individual alternatives. It also commits resources that could fund other services and may require revenue or financing. A private option can reflect individual preferences and flexibility but may leave some residents excluded or external effects unaddressed. These are mechanisms to examine, not a universal ranking. A supported judgement compares feasible outputs and costs, access and accountability on the same period and quality basis. Spending more is not itself proof of better service, and cutting public spending does not automatically create an equal private replacement. This brings together opportunity cost, policy and living-standard analysis. Identify what people need, which arrangement can deliver it effectively and who bears the resources and risks. The conclusion should state the decisive condition rather than merely repeat that each side has advantages and disadvantages. Financing and delivery should remain separate questions. Paying collectively can change access and the source of resources, while useful output still depends on implementation. A private fee can also support a service without proving every resident can obtain it. The comparison needs both dimensions.

Public expenditure is paid for by taxes or government borrowing. Higher taxes reduce households’ disposable income and firms’ profits, so private consumption and investment may fall. Heavy government borrowing can leave less finance available for private firms. The government spends on public goods (street lighting, defence) and merit goods (education, health) that the private sector would under-provide because they are not profitable or because people undervalue them. Private spending reflects individual choice, and private firms driven by profit may produce more efficiently. The conflict is therefore over how much of the economy’s resources should be allocated by government and how much left to households and firms.
