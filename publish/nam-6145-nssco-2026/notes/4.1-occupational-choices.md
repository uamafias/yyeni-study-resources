## Occupational choices — 4.1

### Choosing work

Occupational choice is an individual’s selection of a type of work, subject to preferences, opportunities and requirements. Earnings matter because they support spending and saving, but work also occupies time, affects safety and can provide interest, skills or satisfaction. Wage factors concern monetary earnings; non-wage factors concern other job characteristics. A person comparing jobs therefore needs more than the headline monthly pay. Required qualifications, available vacancies and the ability to reach a workplace also affect which choices are feasible.

An invented applicant may choose a nearby N$5,200 job over a N$5,700 job requiring unpredictable night shifts. The extra N$500 is a wage advantage, while the working pattern and location are non-wage considerations. The choice is not irrational merely because it does not maximise stated earnings. Its quality depends on the person’s needs, alternatives and costs. Another applicant may reasonably prefer the higher-paying role. No single ranking follows from these figures alone.

Changing occupation differs from changing location. Training that lets a clerk become a technician supports occupational mobility. Moving towns while continuing as a clerk concerns geographical mobility. A person can experience both changes, but they remain separate dimensions. This links to earnings in topic 4.2 and employment in topic 7.2: a wage difference does not prove workers can immediately change jobs, since skills and movement can constrain access. When advising an individual, compare monetary rewards, relevant conditions and the practical requirements of each option before making a condition-based judgement. The individual’s priorities should be stated explicitly.

## Occupational choices — 4.1.1

### Wage factors and a fair comparison

Wage factors concern monetary earnings from work, including the pay rate, possible bonuses and future earnings prospects. A person may prefer higher income because it expands the goods and services the household can obtain. The comparison must use the same time basis. A monthly figure for a job with many hours should not be treated as proof of a higher hourly reward. Expected overtime or a conditional bonus also differs from a stated regular payment. The likelihood of receiving extra earnings matters to the individual’s assessment.

Suppose an invented employer offers N$36 an hour for 40 paid hours a week, while another offers N$32 for 50 hours. Weekly earnings are N$1,440 and N$1,600 respectively. The second pays more per week because more hours are worked, despite the lower hourly rate. This does not make the second role automatically preferable: the additional ten hours use time that could serve other purposes. It also does not establish take-home income, because no deductions or other expenses are supplied. State what has been calculated and avoid adding missing terms. Earnings prospects concern the future as well as the initial rate, but a possible promotion should not be treated as certain. A realistic comparison asks what conditions must be met for the higher earnings to arise and how much confidence the applicant can place in that prospect.

### Non-wage factors and personal priorities

Non-wage factors are job characteristics other than direct monetary earnings. They include safety, interest in the work, working hours, location, employment security, training opportunities and the social environment. A safer workplace may reduce exposure to injury. Predictable hours can help someone coordinate study or caring responsibilities. A location close to home can reduce travel time. An interesting role may provide satisfaction independent of its wage, while training can make future occupational change more feasible. Explain the relevant consequence rather than simply list good conditions.

Different people may value these features differently. An applicant supporting dependants may put weight on stable earnings and predictable shifts. Someone seeking a new trade may favour training despite a lower starting rate. These examples are possibilities, not fixed rules about particular groups. A non-wage factor can also have monetary consequences: location is a characteristic of the role, while the resulting transport bill affects the household budget. Keep the characteristic distinct from its consequence rather than assuming non-wage means irrelevant to money. This links back to the parent comparison: compare direct earnings, time and conditions, and then state which matters most for the individual described. The best-supported recommendation identifies a priority and the condition under which another choice would become preferable. A choice also depends on feasibility. A desirable occupation may require qualifications the person does not yet have, while a job with an attractive location may not currently offer a vacancy. Preferences explain what the person would like; skills, opportunities and practical access constrain what can be chosen now. Training can change that feasible set over time, but the availability of a course does not itself establish successful completion or a job offer.
