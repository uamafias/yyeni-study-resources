## Productivity

### Output per input is the key comparison

Productivity is output per unit of input. Labour productivity can describe how much output each worker produces in a period or how much is produced per worker-hour. Production is the amount of output itself. The distinction matters because a firm can produce more by using more inputs without becoming more productive. State both the output and the input before drawing a conclusion.

At the invented Swakop Reed Packing Workshop, 10 workers pack 800 acceptable boxes in a week. If 20 workers pack 1,600 comparable boxes during an otherwise equivalent week, output doubles but output per worker remains 80 boxes. Production rises; productivity on this measure is unchanged. If the original 10 workers instead pack 1,000 acceptable boxes with the same hours, output per worker rises to 100. That is a productivity improvement.

Productivity is a ratio, so a larger total does not establish a larger ratio. The same output with fewer worker-hours can also raise measured productivity. However, calling a method better requires attention to quality and other inputs: reducing paid labour hours while shifting work elsewhere does not necessarily mean fewer total resources are used. Keep the chosen measure visible rather than treating every efficiency claim as interchangeable.

### Measure consistently and interpret the result

To measure labour productivity per worker-hour, divide usable output in the chosen period by the worker-hours used in that period. Worker-hours combine the number of workers and hours worked: 10 workers each working 40 hours give 400 worker-hours. If they pack 800 acceptable boxes, productivity is 800 divided by 400, or 2 boxes per worker-hour. The unit belongs in the answer; a bare number does not reveal whether the calculation is per person, hour or week.

Use invented before-and-after figures. In Week 1 the workshop packs 800 acceptable boxes using 400 worker-hours. In Week 2 it packs 1,000 using 400 worker-hours. Productivity rises from 2 to 2.5 boxes per worker-hour. The increase of 0.5 is 0.25 of the original 2, equivalent to 25%. Input hours are unchanged, so the 25% increase in output matches the productivity increase in this case.

Now suppose Week 2 output is 1,000 but input is 500 worker-hours. Productivity remains 2 boxes per worker-hour. Output rises 25%, but hours also rise 25%. Counting only output would give the wrong interpretation. Similarly, comparing per-worker figures can mislead when workers have different hours. Per-hour measurement controls for the amount of labour time, though equipment and product mix still affect the comparison.

Check quality. A rise in boxes counted is less useful if many are damaged or incorrectly packed. Use acceptable comparable boxes where the case supplies them, not a mixture of finished work and rejected items. Finally, a before-and-after increase does not isolate its cause. If software, training and product mix all change together, the ratio shows what happened but cannot assign the whole improvement to software alone.

The percentage base matters as well. The increase from 2 to 2.5 boxes per hour is compared with the original 2, not the final 2.5. Dividing the 0.5 increase by 2 gives a proportion of 0.25, equivalent to 25%. Reversing the comparison is a different calculation: a fall from 2.5 to 2 is a 20% decline relative to 2.5. State the initial value and direction before interpreting a percentage.

### Match the improvement to the constraint

Training can help workers complete tasks accurately with less wasted time. Suitable equipment can remove a bottleneck, while maintenance can reduce interruptions. Improved organisation may coordinate deliveries, prevent queues between stages and clarify responsibilities. Safer, more workable conditions and appropriate rest can help sustain effective performance. Each mechanism should explain how usable output increases relative to the chosen input, rather than merely listing a desirable action.

At Swakop Reed Packing Workshop, incorrect labels require boxes to be unpacked and redone. Training in the labelling procedure can reduce errors, releasing worker-hours for acceptable output. If the initial problem is instead repeated equipment failure, training alone may not solve it; reliable equipment and maintenance are relevant. A useful proposal identifies the constraint and the reason the intervention addresses it.

Costs and adjustment time matter. Training takes time away from immediate output, and unfamiliar methods can initially slow work. These costs do not rule out later benefits, but they weaken a promise of instant improvement. A firm should compare results over an appropriate period with consistent quality and hours. Higher productivity can help reduce labour cost per acceptable unit if the relevant hourly cost is unchanged, but it is not identical to higher profit: equipment costs and saleability of extra output must also be considered.

### Information technology changes the work process

Information technology can automate routine processing and improve the movement of information. A stock system can tell staff where materials are available, reducing search time. A digital order record can reduce repeated entry and mismatches between orders and labels. Better scheduling can coordinate stages so workers spend less time waiting. In each case the potential productivity gain is more acceptable output per worker-hour or the same output using fewer hours.

Suppose the workshop introduces a suitable label-and-order system. Trained workers can retrieve the correct order and print its matching label without retyping the details. Fewer entry errors mean less rework, allowing more acceptable boxes to be completed during the same labour time. This describes a mechanism, not a guarantee that installing a computer produces a particular percentage gain.

Training, reliable power and maintenance affect the result. If staff cannot use the system, duplicated manual and digital work can waste time. An outage can interrupt a process that depends on online records. Poor information entered quickly can still produce incorrect labels. The strongest conclusion is conditional: suitable IT used effectively can improve productivity, while implementation problems can limit or temporarily reverse the gain. Topic 8.2 considers the different issue of household access and living standards; the present focus is output per input at work.

The relationship between IT and employment is also conditional. If output is fixed, fewer worker-hours might be required. If lower delays enable a business to serve more customers, it may expand output instead of reducing hours. The observed productivity measure does not establish the firm’s staffing decision. Likewise, staff time freed from repeated entry can be reassigned to checking or customer assistance. Evaluate the change in the whole process, including acceptable quality, rather than counting computer use as productive output in itself.

An input measure should cover the work actually counted in the output measure, including relevant checking time rather than omitting it selectively.

### Health, continuity and productivity

The economic effects of HIV and AIDS should be explained through health-related circumstances, not assumptions about a person’s status. Illness can lead to time away from work or reduced capacity while unwell. Prolonged disruption can require replacement training or interrupt the transfer of experience. These effects may reduce production and, where rework or disruption lowers acceptable output per hour, measured productivity. Their size depends on the particular health and workplace conditions.

HIV status alone does not establish reduced ability or productivity. Effective antiretroviral treatment can support healthy lives for people living with HIV, as explained in the [WHO HIV and AIDS fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hiv-aids). An economic case should state actual illness or disruption if its explanation relies on those facts. It should not infer that a worker is ill, absent or less productive merely from a diagnosis. These notes discuss economic mechanisms rather than individual medical treatment.

Separate lost hours from output per hour. If illness-related absence reduces labour hours from 400 to 300 and output from 800 to 600 comparable boxes, productivity remains 2 boxes per worker-hour even though production falls. If disruption also creates errors or delays during the hours worked, output per hour may fall, but that further condition needs evidence. An absence figure alone is not a productivity ratio.

Support for health and continuity can reduce disruption. Appropriate access to care, workable scheduling and cross-training can help retain experience and maintain processes when someone is absent. These actions do not justify assuming a guaranteed numerical result. In an invented case with illness-related interruptions, evaluate training and suitable IT alongside health support and operational reliability. Workplace protection and non-discrimination are considered in topic 6.3; the productivity question here concerns actual inputs, usable output and the conditions affecting them.
