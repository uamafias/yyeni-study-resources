## What is a market?

### A market is an arrangement, not just a place

A market is an arrangement through which buyers and sellers interact to exchange a product or resource. A physical stall is one possible setting, but a market does not require everyone to gather in the same square or building. Buyers and sellers can communicate by telephone, through messages or through an online ordering system. What matters economically is that they can identify what is being offered, communicate requirements and reach terms for an exchange.

Use the invented Namibian business Tsumeb Threadworks. A household sends a message asking to buy a bag, the seller quotes a price, and the parties arrange payment and collection. Their interaction forms part of a market even though the order was agreed remotely. An advertisement without any willing buyer does not by itself prove that an exchange has occurred; communication creates an opportunity to trade, while an acceptable agreement is still needed.

### Product markets and factor markets

A product market exchanges goods or services for use by customers. The bag bought from Threadworks is a product. A repair service sold to a household also belongs in a product market even though the service is not a physical object. Identify what is being exchanged rather than assuming that a market must sell a tangible good.

A factor market exchanges productive inputs. If Threadworks hires a worker to stitch bags, it demands labour services, while the worker supplies those services in return for pay. The worker’s search for a job does not make the worker the demander of labour in this economic classification. The relevant input is the work supplied to production. The firm can be a seller in the bag market and a buyer in the labour market at the same time.

Classification depends on the transaction. Buying a finished bag and hiring someone’s working time are different exchanges, even when they involve the same business. State the product or input, name the buyer and seller, and explain what each provides. This method avoids deciding the market type merely from the identity of one participant.

### Other ways to describe a market

Geographic descriptions concern the reach of trading. A local market connects a more limited area; a national market reaches buyers and sellers across a country; an international market involves cross-border trade. The label needs evidence about the reach of the activity. A small seller can use remote communication to obtain customers outside its immediate town, so the size of its premises alone does not establish its market’s boundary.

Physical and remote arrangements describe how participants interact. They can exist alongside geographic and product classifications. An online sale can be a national product-market transaction; a locally agreed job can be a factor-market transaction. These descriptions answer different questions, so one does not cancel another.
