## Price elasticity — 3.3

### Responsiveness rather than size

Price elasticity measures how strongly quantity demanded or supplied responds to a change in the product’s own price. Comparing percentage changes makes the measure independent of whether quantities are counted in individual items or hundreds. A fall of ten units could be large for a seller initially selling twenty but small for one initially selling a thousand. The percentage change supplies the needed reference. Divide the percentage quantity change by the percentage price change; do not reverse the ratio.

Classify responsiveness by the coefficient’s magnitude. Elastic means above one: the percentage quantity response is larger than the percentage price change. Inelastic means below one: it is smaller. Unit elastic means equal percentage changes, giving one. Perfectly inelastic means zero quantity response, giving zero. Merely inelastic demand can still change quantity; it is not necessarily perfectly inelastic. These categories describe responsiveness over the relevant change and under the stated conditions. They do not mean that a whole product category has one unchanging coefficient in every market and time period. Use evidence about price and quantity rather than guessing the classification from whether the price looks expensive.

### Applying demand responsiveness

Total revenue is price multiplied by quantity sold. It is the money received from sales before costs are subtracted, so it is not profit. When demand is price inelastic for the relevant change, raising price increases total revenue because the quantity sold falls proportionately less than price rises. For price elastic demand the loss of quantity can instead outweigh the higher price, reducing revenue. Unit elasticity means equal proportional responsiveness. For finite initial-value examples, calculate the actual revenue from both price-quantity pairs rather than assuming an exact offset. Perfectly inelastic demand has no quantity response over the assumed change, but that is a special case rather than a description of most ordinary products.

Consider an invented stall increasing price from N$20 to N$22 while weekly sales fall from 100 to 95. Revenue moves from N$2,000 to N$2,090. Using initial values, price rises 10 percent and quantity falls 5 percent, giving demand elasticity magnitude 0.5, inelastic. The revenue increase is N$90; costs have not been supplied, so a profit conclusion would be unsupported. A different sales response could reverse the conclusion. The example demonstrates why a price rise alone is not a complete pricing argument. A firm needs evidence about the relevant buyers and should consider whether their response changes as they find alternatives over time.

### Factors behind responsiveness

Close alternatives can make demand more price responsive because buyers can switch when one product becomes dearer. A necessity with few substitutes may have less responsive demand. Budget share can matter: a large expense creates more incentive to search for alternatives than a very small one. Adjustment takes time, so people may have greater ability to change habits or equipment in the longer term. These factors support a reasoned tendency rather than a guaranteed coefficient. A named product without case evidence is insufficient to establish a precise elasticity value.

Supply responsiveness depends on how easily producers can alter the amount offered. Spare capacity, available inputs and inventories can support a faster response. Production requiring long growing or construction periods may be difficult to expand immediately. A workshop with unused machines may offer substantially more after a price rise; a harvest already determined by the season may change little in the immediate period. The distinction is quantity response to own price, not a supply shift caused by new technology. Price changes move along the relevant relationship; changes in production conditions can alter that relationship. Keep the comparison period clear when applying either concept.

### Interpreting a calculated ratio

An elasticity coefficient is a ratio, not a percentage. If quantity changes by 12 percent and price by 8 percent, dividing gives a magnitude of 1.5. The percentage signs belong to the changes being compared, not to the final coefficient. Saying elasticity is 1.5 percent would mislabel it. A coefficient of 0.5 means the proportionate quantity response is half the proportionate price change, not that quantity is half a unit or price is fifty cents.

Use a consistent reference basis. These worked examples use initial price and quantity as their bases, and prompts state that convention. If a question gives percentage changes directly, divide those percentages rather than calculating another percentage from the coefficient. If the price change is zero, the simple ratio has a zero denominator and cannot provide an ordinary numerical coefficient. Explain the missing information rather than invent a result. This topic links to market forces in 3.2 and pricing policies in 3.5: responsiveness helps explain the effect of a price decision, while demand and supply shifts explain changes in the underlying market conditions. A comparison should name both the percentage response and the underlying side of the market. Two coefficients may have the same numerical value while describing different economic agents. Evidence about buyers cannot be silently substituted for evidence about producers.

## Price elasticity — 3.3.1

### Price elasticity of demand

Price elasticity of demand is the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a price change, measured by percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price. The quantity refers to buyers’ plans. The measure asks how strongly those plans respond to the product’s own price, with other relevant conditions unchanged. If price rises and quantity demanded falls, the signed ratio is negative. For classification, these examples report the absolute magnitude and explain the direction separately. State this convention so a negative sign is not confused with an inelastic classification.

A demand coefficient with magnitude two is elastic, while one with magnitude one half is inelastic. It is the distance from zero relative to one that matters, not whether a negative signed number is numerically less than one. For example, a signed coefficient of minus two has magnitude two and is elastic. Perfectly inelastic demand instead has a zero quantity response over the assumed change. Unit elastic demand has equal proportionate changes. All four distinctions concern responsiveness, rather than how much a household likes the product. Strong desire does not establish ability to pay or a zero response to price. A full application uses the available alternatives, budget circumstances and time period in the case to support the likely responsiveness. A useful test is to compare two equal percentage price changes. If one group reduces quantity by 15 percent after a 5 percent price rise and another by 2 percent, their magnitudes are three and 0.4. The first response is elastic and the second inelastic. The number of physical units initially purchased is not enough to classify either response.

### Price elasticity of supply

Price elasticity of supply is the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a price change, measured by percentage change in quantity supplied divided by percentage change in price. Here the quantity belongs to producers’ planned offers, not buyers’ purchases. In the usual positive supply relationship, higher price encourages greater quantity supplied, giving a positive ratio. Elastic supply responds more than proportionately, inelastic less than proportionately, and unit elastic in equal proportions. A perfectly inelastic supply case has no quantity response in the stated period.

Time and capacity are central to application. An invented sandwich producer with ingredients and unused equipment may expand the amount offered relatively quickly. A grower cannot instantly produce a new season’s crop merely because today’s price rises. Existing stocks might allow more immediate sales, but future production still takes time. These are reasons for differences in responsiveness, not proof of exact coefficients. Demand and supply elasticities must also stay separate: buyers having many alternatives does not directly measure the producer’s capacity to expand. Conversely, spare factory capacity does not tell us how buyers react to a price rise. Link each explanation to the side of the market whose quantity is changing. This connects to the main unit’s calculation: choose the correct quantity before dividing by the price change, then interpret the coefficient for that side and period. The period is part of the comparison rather than a decorative detail. A producer may initially use stock, later arrange overtime and eventually expand capacity. Each step changes what adjustments are feasible. The statement that supply is inelastic today does not mean that producers never respond or that the future coefficient must remain identical.

## Price elasticity — 3.3.3

### Calculating demand elasticity

For an initial-value calculation, find the percentage quantity change as the quantity difference divided by initial quantity, multiplied by 100. Do the same for price. Then divide percentage quantity change by percentage price change. To classify demand responsiveness, use the magnitude of that ratio. Keeping the percentage calculations visible helps identify an incorrect starting value or a reversed division. The final coefficient has no currency or physical unit.

At an invented bag seller, price rises from N$50 to N$54 while weekly quantity demanded falls from 250 to 220. Quantity change is minus 30; minus 30 divided by 250 times 100 gives minus 12 percent. Price change is N$4; 4 divided by 50 times 100 gives 8 percent. The signed ratio is minus 12 divided by 8, or minus 1.5; magnitude is 1.5, elastic. Revenue changes from N$12,500 to N$11,880, a fall of N$620. This supports the interpretation that the quantity loss outweighed the higher price in this example. Do not label the coefficient 1.5 percent. A result mark can be awarded for the requested coefficient even though the teaching explanation shows several steps. The steps are learning support, not extra marks added to the stated tariff. Before calculating, write the initial and new values in pairs so price is not divided by quantity accidentally. After calculating, check whether the magnitude agrees with the percentage comparison: a 12 percent quantity response is larger than an 8 percent price response, so a result below one would signal an error. This independent sense check supports the arithmetic without replacing interpretation of the economic result.

### Calculating supply elasticity

Use quantity supplied, rather than demanded, in a supply calculation. Suppose an invented basket producer increases weekly offers from 400 to 420 when price rises from N$30 to N$33. Quantity rises by 20, which is 20 divided by 400 times 100, or 5 percent. Price rises by N$3, which is 3 divided by 30 times 100, or 10 percent. Supply elasticity is 5 divided by 10, or 0.5. Supply is inelastic for this change: the quantity response is proportionately smaller than the price increase. The coefficient is neither N$0.50 nor 0.5 baskets.

The calculation describes the stated observations under the assumed unchanged conditions. It does not prove the producer is incapable of a larger response next year, or establish why this response occurred without additional evidence. Limited spare capacity could be a possible explanation if the case states it. A different calculation with a 30 percent quantity response to a 10 percent price change gives three, elastic; a 10 percent quantity response gives one, unit elastic. Compare the ratios rather than absolute unit changes. This links the worked supply calculation to the demand example: both divide percentage quantity change by percentage price change, but the quantity and economic interpretation belong to different sides of the market. Use consistent initial values and state the time period in the original data. If percentages are already provided, no initial-value reconstruction is needed. For example, a supplied 6 percent quantity increase and 12 percent price increase give 0.5 directly. Retain enough figures during intermediate calculations to avoid changing a boundary classification through rounding. State the requested coefficient clearly, then use words to distinguish a smaller positive response from a zero response.
