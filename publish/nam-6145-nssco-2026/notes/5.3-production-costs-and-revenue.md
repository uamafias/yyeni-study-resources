## Production costs and revenue — 5.3

### Two different cost classifications

Cost terms answer different questions. Total and average concern measurement: the whole cost or cost per unit. Fixed and variable concern behaviour as output changes over a relevant period and capacity range. The classifications can be used together. Total fixed cost is an aggregate commitment; average fixed cost divides that commitment by output. Calling a cost fixed does not mean the average per unit is unchanged. State the measure before explaining a movement.

For an invented printing firm, monthly premises cost of N$2,000 is fixed over its current capacity range, while materials cost N$4 per booklet. At 500 booklets, total variable cost is N$2,000 and total cost N$4,000; average cost is N$8 per booklet. At 1,000, materials cost N$4,000 and total cost N$6,000; average cost is N$6. Total cost rises while average cost falls because the fixed commitment is shared across more units. This does not show materials became cheaper. The production method and N$4 variable cost per unit are unchanged. The example uses complete stated relevant costs for its illustration, rather than implying that a real printer has no other expenses.

The distinction can be tested by changing just one part of the example. If the premises commitment rises to N$2,500 at the same 500-unit output and N$4 material requirement, total cost becomes N$4,500 and average cost N$9. That is a higher fixed-cost level, not evidence that premises cost varies with each unit. The word fixed describes the relationship to output within the relevant arrangement. A change in the arrangement itself can move the level.

### Revenue and profit comparisons

Revenue is received from sales, while production records output created. If some output remains unsold, multiplying all production by the selling price would overstate revenue for the period. Total revenue records aggregate sales receipts; average revenue divides them by the quantity actually sold. For identical units sold at one price, average revenue equals that price. Mixed-price sales require the actual receipts and sold quantity rather than selecting one advertised price.

Profit deducts total cost from total revenue. A business receiving N$7,200 with total cost N$6,000 earns N$1,200 profit in that period. Calling N$7,200 profit would ignore the resources used. A high average revenue also does not establish high profit per unit unless cost information is available. A comparison should keep periods consistent: monthly receipts cannot be meaningfully compared with annual cost as though they refer to the same activity. Where information is incomplete, describe what can be calculated and state the missing part rather than supply an assumed total. The distinction matters when assessing an output increase: greater receipts can coexist with lower profit if the extra costs exceed them.

### How measures change with output

A cost-output relationship describes how the relevant cost measure changes as output changes under stated conditions. In the short-period illustration, total fixed cost remains unchanged while variable cost grows with output. This can produce lower average cost as fixed cost is spread. It does not imply average cost falls indefinitely. Capacity limits, congestion, overtime or other changing requirements can alter the relationship. A new premises commitment can also change the fixed-cost level when the capacity range changes.

Revenue requires both sales volume and selling terms. At an unchanged price of N$12, selling 500 units gives N$6,000 and selling 600 gives N$7,200. If the price must fall to N$9 to sell 600, receipts are N$5,400 instead. A larger sold quantity therefore does not establish greater revenue without price information. The direction in these examples comes from multiplying the actual pairs of price and quantity. Do not assume a price cut or a larger output in another case unless stated. A sound explanation identifies what is held fixed, calculates the relevant measure and then interprets why it moved.

When explaining the effect on profit, keep the three steps visible: calculate sales receipts, establish complete relevant cost and then subtract. A table containing several output plans can be compared without assuming the largest plan is best. If revenue increases by N$800 while total cost increases by N$1,000, profit falls by N$200 relative to the starting plan. The additional activity has not paid for its additional cost in that comparison. This conclusion concerns the stated feasible options, not an unobserved output level whose costs and receipts are unknown.

### Technology and production conditions

Production technology is the methods and knowledge used to turn inputs into output. A suitable improvement can reduce materials wasted, save input time, improve consistency or expand capacity. It can also change the balance between fixed commitments and variable requirements. A machine might require a recurring equipment charge while using less labour time per unit. The comparison should include the charge and operation costs as well as the labour saving; counting the benefit and omitting the new commitment would bias the judgement.

An invented label maker may use digital layout to reduce repeated setup work for standard designs. If the saved time produces more satisfactory labels with the same resources, productive efficiency improves. Frequent design changes, errors, training or unreliable systems can limit that gain. Increased capacity does not establish that additional output will be sold. Demand and quality still matter to revenue, and complete costs matter to profit. This connects technology to factor substitution in 5.1 and productivity in 5.2. Economies of scale in 5.4 examine another reason costs per unit may change with expansion; do not use that label merely because a new tool is purchased. Distinguish the specific production change, the cost measure affected and the assumptions required for the financial outcome. Output capacity is a technical possibility, whereas sold output also depends on buyers. A cost-saving claim and a revenue claim therefore need their own evidence before being combined into a profit conclusion. The comparison period should also match the intended decision. A temporary setup interruption may dominate an immediate result, while later operation could differ. Without supplied future evidence, keep that possibility conditional and distinguish the measured current outcome from the forecast. This preserves the difference between describing a cost change and claiming a lasting financial advantage.

## Production costs and revenue — 5.3.1

### Total cost

Total cost is the sum of fixed and variable costs at a specified output. It is an aggregate money amount for the relevant period and activity. To calculate it, identify both components and ensure they use the same period. A weekly materials bill and monthly premises charge cannot simply be added as though they describe one week’s complete activity. Convert only when the needed time assumptions are supplied. Completeness matters because profit calculations require total relevant cost, not whichever bill happens to be easiest to observe.

For an invented candle workshop with monthly fixed cost N$1,500 and variable cost N$2,100 at the stated output, total cost is N$3,600. This is not N$3,600 per candle: the aggregate amount still needs dividing by output to obtain an average. A purchase used over several periods may require a period-specific cost treatment rather than treating every cash movement as an identical cost measure. At this syllabus level use the cost figures and period stated in the task. Do not invent depreciation or finance arrangements absent from the case. An answer should label the result as money for the period and recognise whether the supplied components are complete. Total cost changes when either relevant fixed or variable components change, so a higher total alone does not reveal which cause is responsible.

A stated total also needs a scope. The cost of one batch may differ from the whole firm’s monthly cost if other products are made. Use the activity named in the question rather than assign all costs to the visible batch without evidence. Where the illustration explicitly provides complete costs, the later profit subtraction is supported. Where it does not, report a partial result with that limitation. This is a completeness issue, not a reason to invent an omitted expense.

### Average cost

Average cost is total cost divided by output. The unit is money per unit of output, such as N$ per satisfactory candle. It describes the cost spread across the stated output rather than the cost of every individual item having been measured separately. If N$3,600 total cost produces 600 satisfactory candles, average cost is N$6 each. Multiplying N$6 per candle by 600 returns the total N$3,600, providing a useful arithmetic check. The explanation should still identify why the ratio has the stated meaning.

An average can fall while its total rises. If output doubles and total cost rises by less than double, cost per unit falls. For example, N$4,800 over 1,200 candles gives N$4 each, below the previous N$6, despite the larger total cost. This comparison does not prove a particular cause without information about production conditions. Fixed-cost spreading, changed methods or different input prices could matter. It also does not establish that every unit was sold or that profit increased. If output is zero, dividing total cost by zero does not give an ordinary finite average. State that the per-unit measure cannot be calculated at zero output, rather than calling it zero cost. The denominator and the period are part of the measure, not optional labels.

Keep average measures separate from incremental ones. N$6 per unit describes the total cost spread over the stated output; it does not by itself give the cost of producing an additional unit. This topic’s calculation asks for the average, so divide the whole relevant cost by the whole relevant output. The distinction prevents an average being used to justify a new production decision without the appropriate further information.

### Fixed cost and its relevant range

Fixed cost does not vary with output over the relevant period and capacity range, although its level can change over time. A premises payment can remain the same whether a workshop produces 200 or 300 items in a month, provided the same arrangement applies. The payment can be renegotiated, and a larger factory may involve a different commitment. The word fixed relates the cost to current output changes within specified conditions.

A fixed commitment may remain when output temporarily falls to zero. If an invented firm stops production for a week but still owes the same premises payment under the stated contract, output cessation has not removed that commitment. Do not generalise the example to all payments without knowing their terms. The average fixed amount changes with positive output: N$2,000 spread over 500 items is N$4 per item, and over 1,000 is N$2. Its total is fixed in this illustration, while its per-unit amount falls. A common mistake is to write fixed cost falls when the calculation actually concerns average fixed cost. Name the measure precisely. Expanding beyond current capacity may create a new fixed commitment, so the old level should not be carried into another range without justification.

### Variable cost and output

Variable cost varies with the level of output over the relevant period. Materials used for each unit are a straightforward example when the task states the requirement. If every satisfactory item uses N$3 of material, 100 items use N$300 and 200 use N$600. Total variable cost doubles, while the variable cost per item remains N$3. Variable therefore does not mean that the per-unit amount must itself change. It identifies how the total responds to output.

The exact relationship need not be proportional in another case. Wastage, overtime, discounts or changing efficiency may alter requirements, but those mechanisms require evidence rather than assumption. A worker payment can have different cost behaviour depending on the arrangement: a fixed monthly commitment differs from payment explicitly tied to units produced. Avoid classifying an expense solely by a broad name without considering its terms and period. This links to the total-cost identity: add the relevant fixed and variable totals to obtain total cost at a stated output. Then divide the complete total by output for average cost. Classifying behaviour and measuring per unit are separate steps. A complete explanation can show both without treating them as competing descriptions. When output changes, trace the relevant component and keep the units visible so an aggregate N$ amount is not confused with N$ per item.

When an exercise states a constant variable cost per unit, use that assumption consistently rather than adding an unmentioned discount. If another output level has a different per-unit requirement explicitly supplied, calculate its total with that requirement. The explanatory task is to identify which assumption changed and why the resulting totals differ.

## Production costs and revenue — 5.3.2

### Total revenue uses quantity sold

Total revenue is the amount received from sales. For a single product all sold at one price, calculate price multiplied by quantity sold. If an invented notebook seller sells 180 notebooks at N$25, total revenue is N$4,500. Producing 200 notebooks does not turn the unsold twenty into current sales receipts. The relevant quantity is sold output, and the period should be the same as any later cost comparison. A deposit or loan entering the business account is also not automatically sales revenue; identify the source of the receipt.

Price and quantity changes work together. Selling more at an unchanged price increases revenue, while a changed price requires using the new pair of values. Comparing 180 notebooks at N$25 with 220 at N$20 gives N$4,500 and N$4,400. Quantity rises but receipts fall by N$100. This does not establish profit because no total cost is supplied. It demonstrates the combined effect of the actual prices and sales quantities, rather than a rule that larger output must be financially better. For mixed products or prices, add the stated receipts from each group instead of multiplying all units by whichever price is most visible.

Receipts can be reconciled by sale group. If quantities are sold at different prices, calculate each price-times-quantity subtotal and add them. The combined figure measures total sales receipts from the stated groups. It does not count the same unit twice merely because it appears in a production record as well as a sales record.

### Average revenue and price

Average revenue is total revenue divided by quantity sold. With N$4,500 from 180 notebooks it is N$25 per notebook, equal to the common selling price in that case. When prices differ, use total receipts over total units sold. For example, selling 100 units at N$24 and 50 at N$18 gives receipts N$2,400 plus N$900, or N$3,300. Dividing by 150 units gives average revenue N$22 per unit. Taking the simple mean of the two prices would give N$21 and ignore that twice as many units sold at the higher price.

Average revenue is not profit per unit. It has not subtracted average cost, and a high figure may coexist with high production costs. The ratio also differs from total revenue: a seller can have lower average receipts per item but greater total receipts if enough additional items sell. To interpret a change, state whether the question concerns aggregate money or money per sold unit. At zero sales the usual ratio has a zero denominator, so do not assign an ordinary average from that formula. This connects to the parent comparison of totals and averages and to the cost unit: revenue per unit and cost per unit can be compared on a consistent basis, while unsold production and omitted costs must remain visible. The sold-quantity denominator supplies the appropriate weights when prices differ. Check the result lies between the lowest and highest actual selling prices in a positive-quantity example. A result outside that range signals a possible input or division error, while the economic interpretation still needs the stated sales mix.
