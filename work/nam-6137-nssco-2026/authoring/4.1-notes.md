# Geographical enquiry: methods, presentation and judgement

## 01 What geographical enquiry does

A geographical enquiry investigates a question about places, processes or relationships through suitable evidence. It connects a purpose, methods, data, analysis and a conclusion. A coastal enquiry might ask whether pebble size changes along a beach; a tourism enquiry might examine how environmental quality differs between busy and quiet sites. The method must produce evidence relevant to the question rather than collect whatever is easiest.

An aim states the broad purpose, while objectives identify practical parts of the work. A hypothesis makes a testable prediction, such as larger mean pebbles farther along a defined route. It should identify measurable variables and the expected relationship. A statement that a place is interesting is a topic, not a prediction that observations can support or challenge.

Primary data are collected for the enquiry through observations, measurements or responses. Secondary data were collected previously for another purpose and are used through documents or suitable records. Both can be useful, with different limits. A personal observation is not automatically representative, and an official table still needs the correct date, definition and boundary.

The enquiry sequence is purposeful rather than a guarantee of a preferred result. Plan the evidence and safety, collect consistently, analyse and present, then judge the hypothesis using results and limitations. An unsupported hypothesis is still a valid finding when the method is suitable. Changing the prediction after seeing the results would hide what was actually tested and weaken the usefulness of the investigation.

A clear enquiry can therefore produce a useful unsupported prediction rather than being judged by agreement alone.

## 02 A feasible problem and clear objectives

A useful research question is specific enough for available time, access and equipment. “How does mean pebble size vary between three safe stations along this beach?” is more workable than “What causes every coastal change?” The question identifies a feature, area and comparison. A tourism question can similarly name visitor counts, environmental conditions or experiences without assuming that one explains the other before evidence is collected.

Objectives connect the question to operations. For the beach example, locate stations, collect a defined pebble-size sample, calculate comparable means and examine the distance–size pattern. A second hypothesis might concern roundness, requiring a defined roundness measure as well as size. Data collected for one variable cannot silently establish the other. A complete design states how each hypothesis can be tested.

Feasibility includes permission, safe routes, weather or tides, voluntary participation and privacy. A hypothesis does not justify entering dangerous water or pressuring respondents. If a site is inaccessible, record the limit and adapt the design transparently rather than invent observations. The target population and safe eligible area should be clear before selection begins.

A good problem statement also avoids assuming the answer. “Has this management measure reduced litter at the sampled sites?” permits improvement, no change or deterioration. “Why was the measure successful?” presumes a result not yet shown. Neutral framing improves the link between evidence and conclusion and makes an unexpected finding easier to interpret honestly.

Record the two hypotheses before analysing results so the tested predictions remain distinct from later explanations or revised questions.

## 03 Collecting and recording evidence

Data collection begins by defining what will be measured or asked and where the observations apply. Operational definitions make a general concept usable: path width needs stated edges and orientation, a visitor count needs a crossing line and interval, and environmental quality needs scoring indicators. Without those definitions, two observers can produce different records while believing they measured the same thing.

A method should have a reproducible sequence. State the eligible sites or people, selection rule, equipment, measurement or question procedure, units and recording. Include repeats or matched periods where needed. Equipment alone is not a method: naming a tape does not explain where to place it, what to read or how to compare sites. The procedure should let another group understand the intended operation.

Interviews gather responses through conversation. A structured interview uses the same planned questions, while a more open conversation can explore reasons in greater detail. The choice affects comparability and depth. A tourism interview might ask a willing business operator how seasonal demand affects staffing. The response provides that person's account, not a complete employment census or proof of every causal claim.

Interview practice needs neutral wording, appropriate permission and accurate recording. Explain the purpose, allow refusal and avoid unnecessary identifying details. Notes or a permitted recording should distinguish the participant's words from the investigator's interpretation. A leading question such as “How badly has tourism damaged your business?” assumes harm and can distort answers. Ask about experience without supplying the desired conclusion.

Interviews can reveal reasons that a count cannot, but they take time and can be affected by memory, wording, interviewer behaviour or reluctance to disclose. A manager may know enterprise records but not every worker's experience. Select participants appropriate to the question and state the limits. Combining an interview with relevant records can strengthen understanding without pretending that the two measure identical things.

Questionnaires use a planned set of questions or response categories. Closed questions support consistent summaries, while open responses allow explanations but need suitable coding. A neutral tourism item might ask which of several activities the respondent used during the stated visit. Options must be understandable, non-overlapping where a single answer is required and broad enough to avoid forcing an inaccurate choice.

Pilot a questionnaire with a small appropriate group before main collection. Check whether words, categories and the reference period are understood. Revise unclear wording transparently before using the final version, then keep that version consistent. Changing the wording halfway through can make apparent response differences partly a method change. Record which version was used if a change is unavoidable.

Voluntary participation and privacy matter throughout. A respondent can decline a question or the whole survey. Record non-response without treating it as a negative answer. A completed questionnaire count describes respondents, not necessarily everyone eligible. Surveying only people available near one attraction at one hour can omit workers, non-users or other visitor groups relevant to the intended claim.

Sampling selects part of a defined population, area or time range because measuring everything may be impractical. Define the sampling frame before choosing a random, systematic or stratified rule. Random selection uses chance, systematic selection follows a regular interval and stratification represents defined groups. These choices affect coverage and possible bias, and are explained more fully in the next section.

A sampling procedure needs enough detail to operate. Number eligible sites, identify the selection mechanism and state how inaccessible sites or refusals are recorded. Replacing every difficult selection with the nearest convenient option can change the design. Access and safety limits must be respected, but the limitation should be documented. The sample then represents its actual eligible frame, not an imagined larger population.

Observation records visible or otherwise appropriately measurable conditions. A land-use survey can identify frontages using a defined key; a coastal observation can record sediment or wave-related features; an environmental assessment can score conditions using clear categories. Record what is observed separately from inferred causes. A bare patch can be described, while its origin may need more evidence.

Environmental-quality scoring differs from tallying passing objects. A scoring sheet evaluates defined conditions, such as litter level or vegetation damage, on anchored categories. A tally records frequency of events or objects under a counting rule. Both can be useful, but one does not automatically replace the other. If higher score means worse condition, that direction must remain visible in analysis and presentation.

Calibration helps observers use a common scale. Practise on the same site, compare interpretations and clarify descriptors before the main survey. Independent repeat scores can reveal remaining variation. Agreement does not prove every aspect of environmental quality was measured; it improves consistency for the chosen indicators. Subjectivity and indicator coverage should remain in evaluation rather than be hidden by a precise-looking total.

Counts record numbers of defined events or objects. A pedestrian tally needs a line, direction rule and interval; a vehicle count needs categories that avoid double counting. Total passages are not necessarily unique people or journeys. Counts collected over different durations need matched rates or a clear conversion. A ten-minute observation multiplied by six is an hourly estimate under a representativeness assumption, not an observed hour.

Measurements assign quantities and units, such as centimetres of path width or millimetres of pebble length. Define placement and reading, use suitable equipment and retain raw values. Repeat measurements can reduce the influence of random reading error and show variation. They do not correct a tape placed at the wrong angle or a biased station-selection rule. Precision and representativeness are different issues.

A pebble-size investigation can specify the longest dimension, a safe station area and a suitable selection rule rather than picking conspicuously large stones. Record each value in the same unit. Calculate a mean using all valid specified observations, retaining any excluded value and the reason rather than deleting an inconvenient result silently. A roundness score needs its own consistent classification instead of being inferred from size.

Documentary study uses records such as census tables, dated maps, local service data or suitable environmental observations. Check who produced the source, why, when, which area and population it covers, and what units or definitions mean. An old map can reveal historical structure without describing current conditions. A secondary source is not automatically unsuitable because it is old; suitability depends on the question's period.

Different documents may use different boundaries or indicators. A town total cannot be directly compared with a regional total as if the area were unchanged. Missing observations should not be entered as zero. A published rate needs its denominator and interval. Record the source and relevant notes so the analysis can be checked and later users understand what was combined.

A recording sheet organises the actual data. Include site or respondent code, date, time, units, category definitions and space for observations or missing values. A table heading “width” without a unit can make later comparison ambiguous. A zero and a missing measurement need separate codes. Design the sheet before collection, then pilot it so necessary information is not discovered to be absent afterwards.

Raw records should be retained alongside summaries. A mean can hide outliers or missing data, while a total can conceal different component scores. Keeping the raw values allows checks and more useful evaluation. Corrections should preserve what was changed and why. The aim is a transparent evidence trail, not a clean-looking sheet created by removing results that disagree with a preferred hypothesis.

Safety and access are part of the method, not a final sentence added after a risky plan. Coastal work needs suitable conditions and supervision where required; traffic work needs safe positions; questionnaires need voluntary participation. A safer eligible frame may narrow the conclusion, which should be acknowledged. No result is improved by inventing data from an unsafe or inaccessible location.

A suitable combination of methods follows the question. Counts can measure use, measurements can record path form, interviews can explore management and documents can show earlier conditions. Their findings can complement one another while retaining different scales and limitations. A large collection of unrelated numbers is less useful than a small coherent dataset that directly tests the stated hypotheses.

A recording sequence should preserve the number of valid observations. Suppose five tracers are released for a coastal transport exercise, four are recovered and one is not found. The missing tracer has an unknown position, not a measured displacement of zero. A mean calculated from the four recoveries must identify that denominator and the coverage limitation. Including an invented zero would alter the result while pretending the missing value had been observed.

Signed direction can keep a movement record clear. If north is positive, displacements of +3,+2,−1 and +4 metres sum to +8 metres. The recovered mean is +2 metres, or two metres north, for that interval and subset. One recovered tracer moved south, so a net northward result does not mean every tracer moved north. A safe observation method and a stated reference direction are necessary before numbers can be interpreted.

A measurement comparison needs the same feature. Pebble length along the longest dimension differs from width across another axis. If one group measures length and another width, equal units do not make the results directly comparable. A field sheet should name the dimension and a sketch or instruction can clarify placement. A consistent wrong dimension can be reliable as a repeated action while invalid for the intended comparison.

A household or business interview should similarly define its period. A respondent recalling the busiest month may provide useful experience but not a directly observed annual average. Ask neutral follow-up questions and distinguish memory from documentary figures. When exact costs or staffing totals matter, suitable records may be more reliable than an unsupported recollection. The methods can complement each other without treating a person's estimate as an audited total.

Questionnaires need clear response rules. If people may choose several activities, the sum of activity responses can exceed the number of respondents. That is not automatically an error, but a percentage should state whether its denominator is respondents or total selections. A single-choice question requires options that allow the intended answer without overlap. The coding plan should be decided before summarising the results.

Observation can also require a boundary. Counting litter within a ten-metre strip differs from counting everything visible from a viewpoint. Two observers with different sightlines may record different totals. Define the area, object rule and time before comparing. Repeated visits should retain the same area or document a changed boundary so a larger search area is not mistaken for worsening pollution.

Documentary data can provide a control or historical comparison, but only where the methods fit. A beach survey made after a storm may not be comparable with a calm-season record if the question assumes matched conditions. A visitor series can change because the entrance counter or definition changed. Read the source notes and retain those limitations rather than use the longer record simply because it contains more numbers.

A well-designed sheet therefore records context as well as values. For coast work, date, tide stage, safe station code and relevant weather can matter; for tourism, time, visitor category and response rules can matter. Not every enquiry needs every possible variable. Choose the context that affects interpretation of the particular hypotheses and record it consistently.

A field team should agree roles before a timed observation. One person can watch a defined line while another records, with a later check against categories and duration. Swapping roles can reveal inconsistent rules, but should not alter the counting definition. Record interruptions, obstructed views and unusual events rather than silently treating the interval as fully observed. A missed part of a count cannot be replaced with an invented average unless the estimate and its assumptions are explicitly labelled.

A repeated observation should retain the same eligible area and definition, with any interruption recorded before comparing its value.

## 04 Random, systematic and stratified sampling

Random sampling selects through a chance mechanism within a defined eligible frame. In a simple random design, eligible members have a known equal chance of selection. A numbered list and suitable random-number method can choose sites or respondents. Random does not mean selecting whichever location feels convenient or wandering without a rule. The frame and mechanism should be stated clearly enough to repeat the selection process.

A coastal example can number twenty eligible safe stations and draw five distinct numbers without replacement. Record the selected numbers and use the corresponding stations where access permits. If a selected site is unsuitable, follow a stated safe replacement or exclusion procedure and report it. The actual inference concerns the eligible frame. Random selection does not justify unsafe access or guarantee every characteristic is represented in a small sample.

Chance selection reduces some investigator preference, but a small random sample can cluster or miss a rare zone. Repeating selection in a different sample may give different results. A larger appropriate sample can improve coverage, while clear framing remains necessary. A random sample from only one car park cannot represent all residents of a town if many residents never enter the frame.

Systematic sampling selects at regular intervals in space, time or an ordered list, often after a suitable random start. Examples include every tenth metre along a safe transect or every fifth eligible passer-by. State the start, interval and eligibility rule. A systematic design provides orderly coverage and can be practical for comparing gradients, but its interval can align with a repeating feature and distort representation.

Suppose observations are taken every ten metres along a 100-metre safe route, beginning at a stated start. The rule supplies regular spacing, not a random selection of all possible points. If a repeating management feature also occurs every ten metres, the design could overrepresent it. Changing the interval or using an appropriate stratified/random element may address that specific problem. More observations at the same biased interval may leave the problem intact.

Stratified sampling divides the population into relevant groups or zones before selecting within them. A tourism investigation can separate busy and quiet zones or residents and visitors where both populations are relevant. The within-group rule might be random or systematic. Stratification concerns planned group coverage, not permission to choose only the easiest people in each group. Record group definitions and how selection occurs.

Allocation can be proportionate to group size or deliberately equal for a comparison, but the choice affects interpretation. If a population is 80% residents and 20% visitors, a sample of ten from each gives equal comparison groups, not a self-weighting estimate of the combined population. State the design and use appropriate weighting if a whole-population estimate is required. Do not silently treat equal group counts as the actual population mix.

The methods can combine. A stratified random design first defines zones and then randomly chooses eligible sites in each. A stratified systematic design can use a regular rule within each zone. This is not a contradiction: the terms describe different stages. Explain both the group coverage and within-group selection rather than assign a complicated label without an operating sequence.

A teaching diagram can show a safe grid with numbered eligible stations, a regular transect and two shaded habitat zones. Random selection marks a stated random draw; systematic selection marks a stated interval; stratified selection includes sites from each defined zone. The question panel should show the relevant evidence without labelling the answer categories if classification is being tested. Site symbols, boundaries and eligibility must remain clear.

No design removes every bias. Refusals, inaccessible sites, measurement problems, time coverage and an incomplete frame can still affect results. A good evaluation identifies which part is limited and suggests a matched change. Saying “use random sampling” does not solve a badly worded questionnaire, and a larger sample does not calibrate an instrument. Choose the improvement that addresses the actual weakness.

A sample frame can be spatial or social. A numbered grid represents eligible positions; a list represents eligible people or premises; a timetable represents possible observation periods. The random mechanism operates within that frame. If important positions or groups are absent, random selection from the incomplete list does not restore them. Coverage should therefore be assessed before drawing numbers.

A systematic pedestrian sample might use every fifth eligible passer-by after a stated start. The investigator must define who is eligible and avoid resetting the sequence whenever a person looks unwilling or inconvenient. Participation still remains voluntary: refusals are recorded according to the planned rule. This distinguishes selection from consent and prevents a systematic label concealing convenience replacement.

A stratified tourism comparison can select twenty resident respondents and twenty visitors to compare the groups. If twelve residents and sixteen visitors answer positively under the same neutral item, the sample proportions are 60% and 80%. Equal sample sizes make that comparison clear, but do not show that the wider population contains equal numbers of residents and visitors. A combined estimate needs the actual group distribution or an explicitly suitable weighting method.

The proportionate alternative allocates sample size using known group shares where feasible. If the eligible population has eighty residents and twenty visitors, a proportionate sample of twenty would include sixteen and four under that design. The smaller visitor sample may be less useful for a detailed group comparison. The choice depends on the enquiry objective, which should be stated rather than treating equal or proportionate allocation as universally superior.

Sampling bias and random variation differ. A chance-selected small sample can give a variable result without deliberate bias, while a consistently convenient frame can omit the same group repeatedly. A remedy should match the problem. More chance-selected observations can improve precision or coverage, but changing the frame may be necessary to include missing locations or people.

The selected design should serve the hypothesis and eligible frame, with its operating rule retained in the record.

## 05 Information technology and analysis

Information technology can help store, check, calculate and present data where available. A spreadsheet can retain raw readings, compute totals or means and produce suitable charts. A mapping system can connect values to the right places. These tools support the enquiry but do not make poor input data accurate or supply missing definitions. The learner still needs to choose the correct units, denominator and method.

Enter data in a clear structure with one meaning per column and consistent codes. Keep missing values distinct from zero. Check a sample against the raw sheet and test formulas with a simple independently calculated example. If a mean excludes missing values, the valid number of observations should remain visible. An impressive chart cannot repair a column mixing centimetres and metres.

Digital sources require evaluation too. Record the producer, date, area, measure and relevant limitations rather than accept the first search result. A current-looking webpage can host an old observation series. A modelled estimate and a direct count are not identical methods. Information access is useful when source context travels into the analysis rather than disappearing during copying.

Keep an editable data file and the original record so errors can be traced. Avoid storing unnecessary personal information from surveys. A conclusion should explain the geographic pattern, not merely state that software produced a graph. Manual calculation and paper presentation remain valid where technology is unavailable, provided the method and evidence are suitable.

Check a simple formula independently before applying it to the whole column; a repeated error can otherwise affect every summary value.

## 06 Evaluating collection methods

A method's advantage or limitation depends on the question. Interviews can provide depth but may cover few people or rely on memory. Questionnaires provide comparable categories but can be affected by wording or non-response. Measurements can be precise for a defined feature while still coming from an unrepresentative site. Documents can cover a long period but use definitions that differ from the current enquiry.

A useful evaluation names the weakness, explains its effect on evidence and proposes a feasible change. An obstructed view can cause missed pedestrian crossings; moving to a safe clearer position addresses that problem. A one-hour survey cannot describe all seasons; broader matched time coverage addresses that limit. “Be more careful” does not explain what changes or why the result becomes more reliable.

Proxy measures need caution. Counting floors can describe building form but does not give an exact height because floor heights and roof structures vary. A suitable direct or properly specified height method would be needed for a precise comparison. Similarly, counting litter pieces is not the same as scoring every environmental-quality indicator. Identify what the selected measure actually represents.

Advantages and limitations can coexist. Repeating a reading can improve consistency while leaving the site-selection bias unchanged. A larger sample can improve coverage without correcting leading wording. The best judgement therefore follows the evidence chain rather than declares one method universally best. This prepares for presenting results in a form that preserves their meaning and limits.

The strength of a method must therefore be assessed against its intended question and actual implementation, not its label.

## 07 Analysis and presentation

Analysis organises evidence to answer the question; presentation makes the relevant pattern visible. Maps, graphs, tables, isolines and flow charts serve different purposes. Choose according to variable type, spatial or temporal structure and the intended comparison. A chart chosen for appearance alone can mislead if it uses inappropriate units, categories or a hidden denominator.

A data table should have a title, clear headings, units, site or time labels and a source where relevant. Retain raw readings or identify summaries. Missing values need an explicit code. Rows and columns should not silently change meaning. A table of widths in centimetres and depths in centimetres can contain both, but their headings must prevent them being combined as if they measured the same feature.

Totals, means, percentages and ranges answer different questions. A mean uses the valid total divided by the number of readings; a range is highest minus lowest; a percentage needs the stated denominator. Consider widths 10,20,30 centimetres: mean twenty, range twenty, but the equal numbers are coincidental measures of different features. A record should show the calculation and unit before interpreting it geographically.

A bar chart compares separate categories using a labelled value axis and consistent bar treatment. A line graph is useful for a suitable ordered sequence, such as observations through time, where joining the points has meaning. Joining unrelated named categories with a line can imply continuity that the data do not possess. Choose the display for the actual relationship rather than because both forms can be drawn by software.

A pie chart displays parts of a whole. The categories must share a denominator and be exhaustive without inappropriate overlap. A quarter corresponds to ninety degrees and half to 180 degrees. Counts of ten, twenty and ten out of forty correspond to 25%,50%,25% and angles 90°,180°,90°. If responses allow several choices per person, the totals may not form a single non-overlapping whole suitable for an ordinary pie.

A scatter graph plots paired quantitative observations, one variable on each axis. It can show positive, negative or little apparent association and exceptions. Points should retain their pairs; rearranging each variable independently destroys the relationship. Correlation does not isolate causation. Visitor counts and path widths can be associated while slope, construction and earlier use also contribute to the form.

A dispersion display shows individual values and their spread rather than just a mean. Two samples can have the same mean but different variation. For example, 10,20,30 and 18,20,22 both average twenty, with ranges twenty and four. Displaying the values helps prevent the mean from being mistaken for every observation. Repeats and spread can inform reliability without requiring advanced statistical tests.

A map locates evidence spatially. A choropleth shades defined areas by classes of suitable rates or ratios. Raw counts across unequal areas can mislead if the intended comparison is density. Include a non-overlapping key, date and units. A regional average does not describe every point inside the boundary, so the map's scale and aggregation should remain part of interpretation.

A teaching example gives A twenty people per square kilometre and B ten, with classes zero to less than ten, ten to less than twenty and twenty or more. A enters the highest class and B the middle. Both boundaries are unambiguous. Darker shading should consistently represent the intended direction; it must not be reversed halfway through or assumed to mean better conditions without reading the key.

Isolines connect points of equal value in a continuous field, such as elevation, rainfall or another suitable measured quantity. Label the interval and use observations to estimate positions between known points where appropriate. Do not connect every station regardless of value. Isolines should represent the field logically; two different value lines should not cross in a simple single-valued surface.

If station A has value ten and B twenty along a straight segment, a value-fifteen line would cross halfway under a stated linear-interpolation assumption. That is an estimate between observations, not a new measurement. Sparse data or a non-linear field limits precision. The display should retain source points or make their support clear enough to understand where interpolation was needed.

A flow chart shows a sequence, decision or causal structure through boxes and directional arrows. An enquiry chart might connect question, method, data, analysis, conclusion and evaluation, with a revision loop. A process chart should distinguish direction and any branching. It is different from a map flow line showing movement quantities; the key and context identify what the arrows mean.

Display design should preserve comparability. Use consistent units, scales and category definitions across panels intended for comparison. A truncated value axis can exaggerate a bar difference if not clear; unequal observation periods can distort totals. Label sources and uncertainty where relevant. The goal is an interpretable geographic comparison rather than the most dramatic-looking image.

Interpretation then describes pattern with evidence and exceptions. A scatter series of visitor counts twenty, forty, sixty and widths 100,140,120 centimetres is not a width increase at every step: the last point drops. A conclusion must retain that exception instead of rewriting the data into a perfect trend. A table, graph and written answer should all use the same values.

An appropriate presentation can reveal a limitation as well as a result. Missing points, wide spread or unmatched periods should remain visible. Removing them without a documented reason can make the figure look stronger than the evidence. Keep the underlying data and calculation trail so another reader can reconcile the display with the record and the hypothesis.

A coast dataset can connect distance to sediment. At stations zero,fifty and 100 metres along a safe route, longest-dimension readings are 10/12/14, 20/22/24 and 30/32/34 millimetres. The means are twelve, twenty-two and thirty-two millimetres. A scatter or suitable ordered-distance plot can display the increasing relationship. Raw samples remain useful because each mean summarises three values rather than a single pebble representing the station.

A separate gradient record at those stations uses falls of forty,fifty and twenty-five centimetres over ten horizontal metres. Convert the run to 1,000 centimetres before comparing: the ratios are 1:25,1:20 and 1:40. The middle station is steepest and the last flattest, so gradient does not steepen at every step along the route. Grain-size and gradient hypotheses can therefore have different conclusions from the same set of stations.

Presentation should retain a declared sign. For tracer movement, north-positive displacements +3,+2,−1,+4 metres can be shown on a signed number axis or suitable chart. A plot using only absolute distances would hide the southward exception. The missing fifth tracer should have a missing-value note rather than a zero bar. The visual then supports both the net movement and its limits.

A comparison of group percentages can use bars with the same zero-based percentage axis. Resident positives twelve of twenty and visitor positives sixteen of twenty produce bars at 60% and 80%. An axis ending below 100% can be used only with clear labelling and an appropriate purpose, while a truncated baseline can exaggerate the difference. The point difference is twenty percentage points, not twenty people.

An environmental scale must carry direction into every display. Suppose a pollution score runs from zero least to five most pollution. A managed site changes from four to two while a reference changes from three to four. The first improves under the score and the reference worsens. Label the scale and sites so a lower bar is not mistaken for a lower quality outcome.

A flow chart can represent a method without claiming it is a causal experiment. Boxes might show eligible frame, selection, measurement, raw record, summary and hypothesis judgement. A return arrow can show a revision after evaluation. If a decision splits the sequence, label the alternatives so a reader knows what condition directs the next action. Arrow direction is meaningful information, not decoration.

The finished presentation should reconcile with the source table. Recompute means, totals, percentages and category assignments independently, then check each plotted value and label. A correct table can still produce a wrong graph if axes are swapped or a missing value is entered as zero. This cross-check is part of useful analysis rather than a reason to accept software output without inspection.

Units also constrain chart choice. A bar representing a count should not share an unlabeled scale with a percentage or a distance. Separate panels or clearly explained axes can compare related variables without pretending they are the same quantity. A dual-axis display needs especially careful interpretation because apparent line crossings may depend on arbitrary scale choices. A simple matched table or separate plots can often communicate the comparison more clearly. Labels should name the quantity, period and unit, while the written interpretation explains the relationship rather than treating a visual crossing as a measured geographic event.

Read the title, units, date and key together, then compare the displayed quantity rather than an unrelated measure inferred from its appearance.

## 08 Conclusions, evaluation and justified improvements

A conclusion answers the enquiry question or hypothesis using the actual evidence. State whether the prediction is supported, partly supported or unsupported at the measured scale, then give relevant values and exceptions. A result need not support the hypothesis to be useful. The conclusion should not introduce new uncollected facts or extend one short sample into a claim about every season or an entire country.

A tourism hypothesis might predict greater path width at every rise in visitor count. Counts twenty,forty,sixty with widths 100,140,120 centimetres partly fit an initial rise but contradict the every-step prediction at the last site. Describe both parts. The result can show some association without establishing a monotonic pattern or isolating visitor use as the sole cause of accumulated width.

Score direction must be checked before judging environmental change. If zero means low pollution and five means high pollution, a rise from two to four indicates worse recorded pollution under that scale. It does not mean better water quality because the number is higher. A quality scale with the opposite direction could require the opposite interpretation. The label and key determine meaning.

Evaluation examines how method or evidence limits confidence. Distinguish random reading variation, systematic measurement problems, sampling coverage, subjective scoring, non-response, unmatched times and uncertain secondary data. These have different effects. A larger sample addresses some coverage issues but not a wrongly calibrated instrument; repeat measurements address some variability but not a leading survey question.

A justified improvement names the limitation and explains the correction. If widths were measured diagonally at one site, define and use a perpendicular orientation. If a seasonal claim uses one afternoon, repeat matched observations through relevant periods. If respondents were chosen from a single convenient group, use an eligible frame with suitable group or chance coverage. The remedy should be feasible and safe, not a demand to measure everything everywhere.

Reliability concerns consistency under appropriate repeated conditions, while validity concerns whether the method addresses the intended concept or question. A count can be repeated consistently yet fail to measure residents' opinions. A questionnaire can ask relevant questions while its sample omits key groups. These dimensions explain why one improvement may strengthen a method without resolving every weakness.

A cause should not be claimed solely from association. A managed site with less litter after new bins might also have experienced a general clean-up or different weather. A suitable reference site, matched periods and context records can help test alternatives. They strengthen inference without removing every possible uncertainty. A precise conclusion can report improvement while keeping its exact attribution conditional.

Recommendations connect the finding to a relevant action. If litter collection fails at peak use, an appropriate collection response addresses a different issue from a housing or path-width problem. If a result does not show the predicted deterioration, the recommendation should not assume it did. Explain the link from evidence to action, including practical conditions and possible effects on other users.

A report should retain scope and uncertainty. A four-site sample can describe those sites and support a cautious local interpretation. Wider claims need suitable coverage and compatible evidence. An official secondary dataset may provide that wider context, while its definitions and dates still require attention. Combining records is useful only where their meanings align.

The final account should allow a reader to follow question, method, evidence and judgement. Keep raw values, selection rules, calculations, figures and any documented changes together. A neat final number does not make an enquiry stronger than its collection process. Honest limits and specific improvements make the results more useful for later learning and management than a confident unsupported conclusion.

The evidence can support one hypothesis and reject another. In the coastal teaching dataset, mean pebble size rises twelve,twenty-two,thirty-two millimetres, while gradients are 1:25,1:20,1:40. The size prediction of increase is supported at the three stations, whereas a prediction that gradient steepens at every step is not. The conclusion should not combine them into a single claim that all coastal conditions change in the same direction.

For tracers, a +2-metre mean among four recoveries supports net northward movement for the recovered subset, not northward movement of every released tracer. One recovery is southward and one released tracer is missing. A conclusion can state the useful net result while retaining both exceptions. Repeating the release or improving safe recovery coverage could strengthen later evidence without changing what the present observations show.

A group survey can likewise have two judgements. Visitor positives of 80% exceed resident positives of 60%, supporting that comparison.
