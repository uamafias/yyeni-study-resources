# River processes

## 01 Drainage basins, channels and divides

A drainage basin is the area drained by a river and its tributaries. The catchment area is the land area from which water drains towards a common river or outlet. A watershed or drainage divide is the boundary separating neighbouring basins, usually following higher ground. These terms distinguish an area from its edge. In this NSSCO context, use watershed for the divide. Some international sources use watershed to mean the whole drainage area, so the intended meaning must be checked rather than transferred from one source without explanation.

The river channel is the depression through which the river flows. Its bed is the bottom surface and its banks form the sides. A channel can contain water only seasonally; a dry bed does not mean that the landform ceases to be a channel. Conversely, the whole area affected by a flood is not the normal channel. Floodwater can spread beyond the banks onto a much wider floodplain. Channel width, basin area and the length of a divide are different measurements, so their units also differ.

A tributary is a stream that joins a larger stream or river. The point where they meet is a confluence. Tributaries add water and may also supply sediment or dissolved substances. They connect conditions in different parts of a basin to the main channel. Rainfall far upstream can therefore affect a settlement downstream even if little rain falls at that settlement. A distributary is different: it branches away from a main channel, commonly in a delta. Follow the direction of flow before deciding whether a junction represents joining or splitting.

Surface water generally moves downslope under gravity. A divide separates slopes that direct runoff towards different outlets. It need not be a spectacular mountain ridge: in a flat landscape, a slight rise can separate drainage directions. On a contour map, the divide commonly follows ridges and passes between valleys. The river channel occupies a valley floor, not the ridge separating two river systems. A line drawn straight across a river valley is therefore unlikely to be a correct divide unless the resource establishes a special arrangement.

A basin includes more than the water visible in the channel. Rain may infiltrate soil, be stored temporarily, move slowly through the ground, evaporate or be used by vegetation. Not every drop immediately reaches the outlet. For a simple surface-drainage map, the basin boundary identifies the area contributing surface runoff towards that outlet. Groundwater can follow more complex paths, sometimes crossing a surface divide where geology permits. This qualification prevents a useful surface-water model from becoming an unsupported claim that all underground water follows the same mapped line.

To interpret a basin map, begin with the outlet and trace the channel network upstream. Identify tributaries entering the main river, then follow the enclosing high ground around the contributing area. The divide should enclose the relevant tributaries while excluding channels that drain towards another outlet. A basin boundary normally meets the channel at the chosen outlet rather than cutting repeatedly across upstream tributaries. The exact outline depends on which outlet is specified: a tributary has its own smaller catchment nested within the larger river basin.

Consider an original schematic basin with a southern outlet O. Streams from northern valleys A and B join the main river before O. A separate valley C drains east towards outlet E. The basin of O includes the slopes supplying A, B and the main river, but excludes slopes draining into C. The ridge between B and C forms part of the divide. To shade the catchment, shade the contributing land area; to identify the watershed, trace its boundary. Shading a wide strip along the ridge would confuse the edge with the area it encloses.

Suppose a square-grid resource shows twelve full squares and four half-squares inside a clearly drawn basin boundary. If each square represents 1 km², the estimated area is 12 + 4 × 0.5 = 14 km². The calculation estimates land area, not river length. If the main channel measures 7 km on the same resource, that is a separate quantity. Do not add 14 km² and 7 km. Irregular boundary squares make the area approximate; the answer should not claim a precision unsupported by the grid method.

A channel resource may provide three labelled positions. P lies at the bottom of a narrow water-filled depression, Q is on the raised side and R lies on the ridge separating two valleys. P indicates the channel bed, Q a bank and R a possible divide. Their relative heights matter more than the letter sequence. If a key distinguishes a seasonal stream with a broken line from a permanent stream with a solid line, retain that distinction in a description. Do not infer year-round discharge from the existence of a mapped channel alone.

Human actions can affect different parts of this connected system. Removing vegetation on an upper slope can increase sediment delivery under some conditions; discharging wastewater into a tributary can affect places downstream. Paving land may change runoff pathways and timing. The consequence depends on rainfall, soil, drainage design and the scale of the change, rather than the mere presence of one house or field. A basin provides a useful spatial unit because it connects the contributing land to the river, but it does not eliminate the need to identify actual sources and processes.

This distinction between network, area and boundary links river processes to map reading, water management and research skills. In a field investigation, state which outlet and reach are being studied before comparing results. A sample taken upstream of a polluted tributary cannot by itself describe water downstream of that tributary. A basin-scale conclusion needs evidence covering the relevant contributing area and period. Clear definitions at the start prevent later confusion between a problem within a channel, a change across the catchment and a line marking the edge of the system.

## 02 Upper, middle and lower courses

The longitudinal profile follows the river’s height from its source towards its mouth. It is different from a cross-section, which cuts across the channel or valley at one location. A typical long profile becomes less steep downstream, producing a broadly concave shape. Actual rivers may contain abrupt steps, flat reaches, lakes, resistant rock or artificial structures. The profile is therefore a useful model of overall change, not a claim that gradient decreases smoothly at every point on every river.

The upper course is nearer the source. A typical upland upper reach has a steep gradient, a relatively narrow valley and a channel constrained by surrounding slopes. Vertical erosion can contribute to valley deepening, while weathering and mass movement affect the valley sides. Interlocking spurs may develop where the channel winds around projecting hillsides rather than cutting straight across them. A waterfall or rapids can occur where rock structure creates an abrupt change in level. None of these observations proves that a reach is upper course without considering its position and the other evidence.

The middle course commonly has a gentler gradient and a wider valley. Tributaries often increase discharge downstream, although water losses and abstraction can modify that pattern. The channel may become wider and deeper, with lateral erosion and deposition contributing to meander development. The river can still erode vertically in places. It is misleading to say that a river switches off erosion when it enters its middle course: erosion, transport and deposition can operate in different parts of the same reach and at different stages of flow.

The lower course lies nearer the mouth or other terminal area. It commonly has a low overall gradient, a wide valley or floodplain and a larger channel. Deposition may become important where sediment supply exceeds the ability of the flow to carry it onward. However, a large lower-course river may have substantial velocity and transport capacity. Gentle gradient does not automatically mean slower flow than every upstream reach. Channel shape, roughness, water depth and discharge also influence velocity. Distinguish the slope of the valley from the speed of moving water.

Downstream discharge is a volume per unit time, commonly measured in cubic metres per second. It is not simply channel width or the amount of rain at one point. A wider channel can carry more water under otherwise similar conditions, but depth and velocity matter as well. In a simple rectangular approximation, a channel 4 m wide with mean depth 0.5 m has a cross-sectional area of 2 m². At a mean velocity of 0.8 m/s, discharge is 2 × 0.8 = 1.6 m³/s. This is an original teaching calculation, not a measurement of a Namibian river.

A profile resource might record 900 m elevation at distance 0 km, 500 m at 10 km, 200 m at 30 km and 100 m at 50 km. The first reach drops 400 m in 10 km, the second 300 m in 20 km and the third 100 m in 20 km. Converting to metres, the gradients are 1:25, about 1:66.7 and 1:200. The increasing denominator indicates a gentler gradient. These data support a downstream reduction in steepness across the specified reaches; they do not establish the velocity at each point without further measurements.

To describe an upper-course resource, identify the narrow valley, steep sides, high local relief or closely spaced contours where shown. To describe a middle-course resource, use the evidence for a wider valley, developing floodplain, meanders or tributary joins. For a lower-course resource, identify the broad low-lying plain, wide channel or branching mouth if supplied. One familiar word is not enough: a meander can occur outside the textbook middle-course setting, and a waterfall can interrupt a river at a local geological step farther downstream. Combine position with the observed features.

Valley shape results from interaction between channel processes and slope processes. River erosion may deepen or widen a valley, while weathering weakens material and gravity moves it downslope. A V-shaped valley is therefore not made by water cutting a perfect V directly into all surrounding rock. The channel erodes its bed, and the sides retreat or adjust through other processes. In lower-gradient reaches, repeated lateral channel movement and deposition help create a broad floodplain. Landforms record this history over time, not only the condition visible during one dry-season visit.

Sediment size and shape may change downstream as particles are broken and rounded and as material is selectively transported or deposited. Yet a tributary or bank collapse can introduce fresh large angular material farther downstream. A simple rule that every downstream stone must be smaller and rounder would overlook these local inputs. A good description states the trend supported by the resource and any exceptions. An explanation then considers attrition, abrasion, selective transport and sediment supply rather than treating river position as a cause by itself.

Channel management can modify the natural-looking profile and cross-section. A dam can create a reservoir and trap sediment; a weir creates a local step; straightening changes channel length and slope over a reach. If a resource includes one of these features, include it in the interpretation rather than forcing the observations into an unmodified textbook sequence. Similarly, a dry or losing river in an arid region may not show a continuous increase in discharge towards its mouth. The general model needs the conditions of the actual river being studied.

These ideas connect the upper–middle–lower model to map skills and practical investigation. A set of comparable cross-sections can test whether width or depth changes along sampled reaches. A longitudinal profile provides height and distance information, while field measurements provide flow conditions for particular dates. Keep the two evidence types separate. A convincing account combines the overall pattern, selected supporting values and the limits of the data, rather than assuming that the label lower course establishes every physical characteristic without observation.

## 03 How rivers erode

River erosion is the wearing away and removal of material from the channel bed and banks. Several mechanisms contribute, and the distinction between them concerns the action taking place. Hydraulic action involves the force of flowing water. Corrasion, also called abrasion, involves the river’s load scraping or striking surfaces. Corrosion, also called solution in this context, involves chemical dissolution of susceptible material. Attrition is the wearing and rounding of transported particles as they collide with one another. The last process chiefly changes the load rather than directly scraping the channel surface.

Hydraulic action can loosen material through the pressure and turbulence of flowing water acting on the bed and banks. Water entering cracks can exert forces that help detach pieces, particularly where the rock is already jointed or weakened. Powerful flows can also remove unconsolidated bank material and undermine a slope. The description should identify water as the agent applying force, rather than inserting stones into an explanation that is meant to distinguish hydraulic action from abrasion. Several mechanisms may operate together in reality, but a classification task asks which action the supplied observation demonstrates.

Imagine an original observation of a jointed bank during high flow. Water repeatedly surges into an opening and a loose block is detached; no transported stone is seen striking the block. The observation is consistent with hydraulic action because water pressure and turbulence act on a weakness. It does not establish that abrasion caused the detachment merely because sediment exists elsewhere in the river. Conversely, absence of a recorded collision does not prove that abrasion never occurs at that bank. The inference should remain limited to the observed event and the information supplied.

Corrasion or abrasion occurs when transported particles strike, scrape or grind the channel bed or banks. Sand and stones act as tools carried by the flow. Their impacts can remove material and smooth or deepen parts of the channel, depending on the rock, sediment and flow conditions. A river with no available abrasive load cannot demonstrate the same sediment-impact mechanism, even if its water is moving rapidly. Hardness also matters: resistant particles can abrade a less resistant surface differently from easily broken particles moving over harder rock.

A resource showing pebbles swept against a bank, scratches on the surface and fragments removed from that surface supports abrasion. The evidence concerns contact between the load and the channel, rather than collisions solely between pebbles. Do not call the process traction simply because some stones roll along the bed: rolling describes their movement, while the scraping action explains wear. One moving stone may be transported by traction while simultaneously contributing to abrasion. These descriptions identify different aspects of the same event, not necessarily competing explanations.

Corrosion or solution involves dissolution of susceptible minerals in river water. Water containing dissolved carbon dioxide can become weakly acidic and react with carbonate rock such as limestone. Material then becomes part of the dissolved load. This is a chemical mechanism, so the evidence differs from a surface merely being struck or scratched. The term corrosion here must not be confused with corrasion: the similar spelling hides a major difference between chemical solution and mechanical abrasion. Writing the alternative names solution and abrasion alongside them can make the distinction clearer.

Consider a limestone reach where water analysis and surface observations support dissolved carbonate leaving the rock. That evidence is consistent with solution. A photograph of a rounded limestone pebble alone would not establish how much material dissolved, because abrasion and attrition can also alter its shape. Chemical composition, the water’s ability to dissolve the mineral and the presence of dissolved products make the explanation stronger. Do not say that any water dissolves every rock equally quickly. Mineral susceptibility, water chemistry and contact conditions influence the process.

Attrition occurs when particles carried by the river collide, breaking off edges or fragments and becoming smaller and rounder. It concerns load-to-load contact. An angular stone colliding with other stones may lose projecting corners over repeated impacts. A sequence of measured particles becoming rounder can be consistent with attrition, but the evidence must distinguish transported histories from different source rocks. A downstream sample can contain fresh angular material introduced by a tributary, so downstream position alone does not prove that every stone has experienced the same amount of attrition.

The difference between abrasion and attrition can be tested by identifying what collides with what. A pebble scraping the bed contributes to abrasion of the bed. Two pebbles striking each other and losing corners demonstrate attrition of the load. Both may reduce particle size eventually, because abrasion can also wear the tools themselves, but their textbook distinctions concern the main target of wear. A good explanation names the contact and the resulting change. The vague phrase rocks hit things does not establish either mechanism clearly enough to discriminate between them.

Erosion should also be kept separate from transport. Traction is rolling or sliding material along the bed; saltation is hopping or bouncing near the bed; suspension is particles carried within the water; solution transport is dissolved material moving with it. These terms describe movement. Hydraulic action, abrasion, solution erosion and attrition describe detachment, dissolution or wear. The word solution appears in both lists because dissolution can remove rock chemically and the resulting dissolved substances can then be transported. State whether the question concerns the removal step or the subsequent movement of material.

Flow conditions influence whether erosion can occur and which material can be moved. Higher velocity and turbulent stresses can increase the ability to detach or transport certain particles, but particle size, cohesion, channel roughness and sediment supply matter. Fine clay can resist initial erosion because particles cohere, yet remain in suspension once entrained. A coarse gravel particle may be easier to describe as settled, but sufficiently energetic flow can move it. Avoid a universal ordering based solely on particle size without considering whether the task concerns entrainment, transport or deposition.

A four-row data record can make the distinctions explicit. Row A describes water pressure detaching a block from a joint. B describes sand scraping a bedrock surface. C describes carbonate dissolving into the water. D describes two pebbles colliding and losing corners. A supports hydraulic action, B abrasion, C solution and D attrition. To justify each classification, repeat the defining action in your own explanation rather than copying only the process name. If a record merely says that a stone moved, it supplies transport evidence but no clear account of erosion or wear.

These mechanisms connect to the formation of waterfalls, potholes, meanders and gorges. Hydraulic action and abrasion can deepen a plunge pool, abrasion can enlarge a bedrock hollow, and bank erosion contributes to channel migration. Weathering can prepare material for removal, while transport carries it away after detachment. The landform explanation must link the appropriate mechanisms in sequence. Listing four erosion names without showing their role does not explain a particular waterfall or bend. The same discipline applies in research: an observation should support the proposed action, and the conclusion should not claim mechanisms that were neither observed nor otherwise evidenced.

For a short classification exercise, ask whether the observation changes the channel, the particles or merely their position. This gives a practical test when similar-looking arrows appear in a diagram. An arrow alone cannot identify abrasion, attrition or transport; its meaning depends on the contact, the material and the stated change.

## 04 River erosion landforms

River landforms develop through interactions between flowing water, sediment, rock structure and time. A description identifies the visible shape and arrangement; an explanation connects the processes that create or modify it. Waterfalls, rapids, meanders, potholes and gorges have distinctive forms, but they can occur together and can record more than one stage of development. A drawing should therefore be read for its actual rock layers, flow direction and shape rather than matched to a memorised sequence without checking the evidence. Local geology can interrupt the simple upper–middle–lower-course model.

A waterfall is an abrupt drop in the river’s bed over which water falls. A common school model has a more resistant rock layer overlying less resistant material. Water crosses the resistant cap and falls onto the weaker material below. Hydraulic action and abrasion by the load can excavate a plunge pool at the foot. More rapid removal of the less resistant rock can undercut the cap, leaving an overhang. The overhang can collapse when it is no longer adequately supported. Repetition moves the waterfall upstream and can leave a steep-sided gorge downstream of the retreating fall.

The causal order matters. The plunge pool forms through energetic erosion at the foot, undercutting removes support, collapse produces fallen blocks, and further removal allows renewed erosion. A statement that the waterfall moves downstream reverses the retreat in this model. Fallen blocks can temporarily influence the pool and the flow; they do not simply vanish as soon as they fall. The explanation also depends on the stated arrangement of rock resistance. Some waterfalls arise from faults, glacial history or other geological controls, so do not impose a horizontal resistant-over-weaker layer model where a resource clearly shows a different origin.

In a sequence resource, identify the initial lip, the pool and the undercut zone, then compare later positions of the lip. If a clearly labelled upstream position U replaces earlier position D, the fall has retreated upstream. A distance scale and dates can support a rate calculation, but an undated sequence cannot establish metres per year. For example, a supplied record of 12 m of retreat over six years would give an average of 2 m per year for that interval. It would not prove constant annual retreat or a forecast of the same future rate, because collapse can occur in episodes.

Rapids are stretches of turbulent, fast-changing flow over an irregular or steeply sloping bed, often involving a series of smaller steps rather than one large vertical fall. Unequal rock resistance, protruding boulders and an uneven bed can disturb the flow. In a resource, describe several broken-water zones or small drops over the reach, rather than inventing a single high waterfall. The same river may contain rapids above or below a larger fall. Rapids can be hazardous because currents and obstacles vary over short distances, but visible turbulence alone does not supply a measured depth or exact velocity.

A meander is a bend or loop in a river channel. In a typical freely meandering reach, faster flow and stronger erosive action occur towards the outer bank, while lower velocities near the inner bank favour deposition. The outer bank can become a steep river cliff or cut bank. The inner bank commonly has a gentler slip-off slope and a point bar. Flow also has a three-dimensional circulation that moves water and sediment across the bend; a simple plan-view arrow is only part of the model. The bend can migrate laterally and downstream as erosion and deposition proceed.

To explain meander development, link the unequal flow to the contrasting bank processes. Stronger outer-bank erosion removes material and shifts the channel outward. Inner-bank deposition adds sediment where conditions no longer sustain the same load. Together these changes increase or move the bend. A meander is therefore not a purely erosional feature: its form reflects erosion and deposition operating in different positions. Do not place the point bar on the outer cliff merely because that is the side nearest a label. Identify which side is outside the curve, using the flow direction and the channel’s shape.

If two bends approach one another, erosion can narrow the intervening neck. During sufficiently high flow, water may take a shorter route across the neck. Deposition can then reduce connection with the abandoned loop, leaving an oxbow lake that may gradually fill with sediment and vegetation. This extension helps connect meanders to floodplain features. It does not mean every bend inevitably becomes an oxbow within a known time. Rock confinement, engineering, flood history and sediment supply affect whether a cutoff occurs. A resource showing only a curved channel should not be described as already having an isolated lake.

A meander resource might show an outer-bank depth of 2.4 m and near-bank velocity of 0.9 m/s, compared with 0.8 m depth and 0.3 m/s at the inside. These are original illustrative values. The outer side is deeper and has faster recorded flow in this dataset; the inner side is shallower and slower. An exposed inner sand bar supports deposition. The data are consistent with the textbook bend pattern, but a single measurement does not establish year-round conditions. Depth and velocity should be compared in their own units, and a depth difference should not be reported as a speed difference.

Potholes are rounded hollows in a rock bed that can develop where swirling water moves sediment against the surface. An initial irregularity can concentrate turbulence. Stones and sand repeatedly moving within the hollow abrade its sides and floor, enlarging it. The effect is mechanical abrasion of the bed, not simply a claim that the trapped stones dissolve it. Attrition may also wear the moving stones, but that describes a change to the load rather than the enlargement of the hollow. A circular shape is a clue; evidence of bedrock, swirling flow and abrasive material makes the mechanism more specific.

A pothole cross-section should distinguish the channel bed from the hollow and the moving abrasive particles. Arrows should indicate circulating flow within or above the depression where that is the intended mechanism. If the question image labels the hollow with its landform name, it may remove the intended identification demand, so a neutral letter can be preferable. In an answer, explain how repeated impacts deepen or widen the hollow. Do not claim that a stone must remain trapped permanently: flow conditions and the size of the opening can change, allowing sediment to enter or leave.

A gorge is a deep, narrow valley with steep sides. Retreat of a waterfall can create a gorge by leaving a cut channel behind the receding lip, but other histories can also produce gorges. Downcutting associated with uplift or changing base level can incise a narrow valley over time. A resource specifically showing waterfall retreat supports that mechanism; a steep-sided valley without a waterfall sequence does not prove it. Distinguish a gorge from a plunge pool: the gorge is an elongated valley reach, while the pool is a local depression at the foot of falling water.

Comparing views prevents common misidentifications. A plan view shows bends, channel routes and the length of a gorge. A longitudinal section shows changes in height along the channel, including a waterfall step or a series of rapids. A cross-section shows the width and depth of a valley or pothole at one point. Vertical exaggeration can make slopes look steeper than they are. Read the axes and scale before comparing landforms from different drawings. The word deep should be supported by relief or depth information, not merely by dark shading that might represent rock type or water.

These landforms connect to transport and deposition because erosion needs material to be removed and abrasion often uses a moving load. They also connect to weathering, which can weaken the rock around a waterfall or gorge, and to map reading, which supplies contour and channel evidence. Human management can alter the processes: a dam may reduce sediment supply, while bank protection can restrict channel migration locally. Such changes need site-specific evidence. A complete landform explanation follows the supplied mechanism and the sequence of change, while a careful description reports the visible form without claiming an unsupported history.

A composite landform resource can combine a longitudinal section and a bend plan. The section might show a 4 m abrupt drop, a hollow at its foot and a narrow steep-sided reach downstream; the plan might show an outer cut bank opposite an inner sand bar. Describe the waterfall, plunge pool and gorge from the section, then the erosion–deposition contrast from the bend. The four-metre value describes the drop, not the gorge length or the pool depth. Keeping each measurement attached to its feature prevents a labelled value from being reused as evidence for an unrelated part of the landscape.

## 05 How rivers transport their load

The load is material carried by a river. It can include solid particles of different sizes and dissolved substances. Transport describes the movement of that material, whereas erosion describes detachment, wear or dissolution. A stone can be moved without being measurably worn during the observed interval. It can also strike the channel or another stone while being transported. Distinguishing these aspects avoids treating traction, suspension and hydraulic action as interchangeable names. The movement mode depends on flow conditions and particle properties, not solely on where the river lies in its course.

Traction is the rolling or sliding of particles along the bed. Relatively large or heavy particles can move this way when the flow exerts enough force to overcome resistance. They remain in contact with the bed for much of their movement. A diagram should show the particle moving along the surface rather than floating high in the water. The arrow records transport, not necessarily abrasion. If the rolling particle scrapes or strikes the bed, that additional observation can support abrasion, but the word traction itself identifies how the particle travels.

Suppose a labelled cobble changes position along a shallow bed during a high-flow observation while remaining in repeated contact with it. The record supports traction. It does not establish that cobbles travel by traction during every flow condition: the same cobble may remain at rest during lower flow, or stronger conditions may lift it briefly. Particle shape, packing and shelter by other stones affect how readily it moves. A large flat stone may behave differently from a similarly heavy rounded stone, so mass alone does not predict movement in every channel setting.

Saltation is hopping or bouncing movement near the bed. A particle is lifted briefly, travels through a short arc and returns to the bed before being lifted again. The intervals of movement and contact distinguish it from uninterrupted rolling and from particles carried within the water column for longer periods. Sand and small stones can move this way under suitable conditions, but a fixed universal size range is not required for the school-level distinction. The relevant evidence is the repeated lift–travel–contact sequence. Do not describe saltation as the river itself bouncing.

A sequence of positions above and on the bed can demonstrate saltation in a teaching diagram. If the particle is shown only once above the bed, the movement history is uncertain. Adding several time-labelled positions or an explained path can make the evidence clear. In a response, refer to the repeated short hops and bed contacts rather than simply saying that the particle is small. If the flow strengthens, a particle that previously bounced may spend more time in suspension. The modes are responses to conditions, not permanent identities attached to individual grains.

Suspension involves particles carried within the water. Turbulent motions can keep fine material from settling directly to the bed for a considerable time. Silt, clay and fine sand may contribute, depending on conditions. Suspended material can make water appear muddy, but colour alone does not measure particle concentration or prove that all material is suspended. Dissolved substances, organic compounds and lighting also affect appearance. A properly interpreted resource distinguishes visible particles or measured suspended sediment from a vague description of dark water. Some particles can settle when the supporting turbulence declines.

An original container demonstration can illustrate settling. A sample of muddy water is left undisturbed and some particles accumulate at the bottom. The change is consistent with suspended solids settling when turbulence ceases. It does not show that all dissolved material has settled too. A fine filter can retain some solid particles while dissolved substances pass with the water. The exact result depends on filter properties and particle size, so describe the stated observation instead of assuming that a household filter removes every contaminant. Suspension is a transport process, not a guarantee about water safety.

Solution transport occurs when dissolved substances move with the water. They are not visible grains that can be shown accurately as pebbles floating in the channel. Dissolution of minerals can supply dissolved load, and groundwater or tributaries may contribute other dissolved substances. A river can therefore carry a dissolved load even when it looks clear. The chemical removal of susceptible rock is solution erosion; the movement of the resulting dissolved substances is solution transport. State which stage is being described. The same word appears in both contexts because the processes are connected.

A resource showing water carrying dissolved calcium compounds without visible grains supports solution transport. Slowing the water does not make these substances settle in the same simple way as sand. Chemical conditions, evaporation or biological activity may eventually cause precipitation or other removal, but that requires a separate mechanism. Do not add a pile of dissolved salt beneath a slow-flow arrow as if ions were stones dropping from suspension. This distinction is particularly useful when comparing erosion, transport and deposition: not every load component responds identically to reduced velocity.

Several transport modes can occur in one channel at the same time. Coarse particles may roll, sand may hop, fine sediment may remain suspended and dissolved substances may travel with the water. The amount in each category changes with discharge, turbulence, sediment supply and local channel conditions. During a flood, material previously at rest may be mobilised. After the flood, some particles may settle while fine suspended material continues downstream. A description should identify the evidence for each component rather than claiming that the river has only one transport mode because one large stone is visible.

A four-part supplied record distinguishes the modes by action. Particle A rolls along the bed. B repeatedly rises briefly and lands. C is measured within the flowing water for an extended interval without bed contact. D is present as dissolved substances. A supports traction, B saltation, C suspension and D solution transport. To explain the classifications, use contact with the bed, duration of lifting and physical state. “A is largest” is less discriminating unless the task also supplies a movement observation. Size can help explain a mode but should not replace its defining movement.

Transport connects river reaches because material detached upstream may be stored, remobilised and deposited farther along the system. A grain’s journey need not be continuous from source to mouth. It may remain on a bar through a dry period and move again during a later event. Tributaries and eroding banks can add fresh material downstream, complicating simple trends in size and shape. These ideas connect to deposition, floodplain formation and research. A sample records material present at a place and time; it does not by itself reveal every previous movement stage or establish an average annual load.

Sediment concentration is an amount of suspended material per volume of water, whereas sediment discharge is an amount passing a location per time. A muddy sample can have high concentration without representing the largest total sediment movement if little water is flowing. In a simplified example, 100 milligrams per litre in a flow of 10 litres per second gives 1,000 milligrams per second of that suspended component. At the same concentration and 20 litres per second, the amount transported doubles. Keep this calculation separate from dissolved load and from bedload not included in the sample. It illustrates why appearance alone cannot establish the total material being carried.

## 06 Why and where deposition occurs

Deposition is the laying down of material that was being transported. For solid sediment, it occurs when local conditions no longer sustain movement of some or all of the supplied particles. Reduced velocity and turbulence can allow particles to settle or stop moving along the bed. The response depends on particle size, density, shape and cohesion, as well as channel conditions. A river may deposit some material while continuing to transport other material, so deposition does not imply that the water has stopped or that the whole load settles at once.

Velocity is speed of flow, commonly expressed in metres per second. Discharge is volume passing a cross-section per unit time, commonly cubic metres per second. The two are related through cross-sectional area but are not identical. A channel can become wider and deeper as discharge increases, so a change in volume does not determine velocity without considering channel geometry. Likewise, a narrow constriction may have rapid flow even where the river’s overall gradient is gentle. A deposition explanation must follow the actual change described, not substitute volume for velocity as though they were the same measurement.

When water spreads beyond a channel during a flood, friction and spreading can reduce local velocity and turbulence. Sediment then settles across the inundated ground, contributing to a floodplain. Coarser material often accumulates closer to the channel, while finer particles can remain suspended farther away. The exact pattern depends on the event and local conditions. A flood does not produce an equal-depth blanket of identical sediment across the whole plain. Levee development reflects repeated uneven deposition, so a plan or section should show a pattern consistent with the grain-size and distance evidence supplied.

Inside a meander, lower near-bank velocities commonly favour deposition on a point bar. At a river mouth, flow entering a lake or sea may spread and lose the conditions needed to carry part of its sediment load. Whether a delta builds depends on the sediment supplied and the ability of waves, tides and currents to remove or redistribute it. A river mouth is therefore a possible site of deposition, not proof that every river forms a large delta. A lake or reservoir can also trap sediment as flow slows, reducing the amount carried farther downstream.

The load influences how deposition proceeds. Coarse particles usually need more energetic conditions to remain moving than fine suspended particles, while cohesive fine material behaves differently during initial erosion and subsequent settling. A flow decrease may stop gravel moving while fine sediment continues. The fact that clay can be difficult to detach from a cohesive bank does not mean it must settle before every sand grain once suspended. Distinguish entrainment thresholds from deposition behaviour. A simplified teaching account should retain this distinction without pretending that size alone predicts every particle’s movement in a real channel.

Bedrock and channel form influence roughness, gradient, obstacles and available storage spaces. A boulder or rock step can create local zones with different velocities, allowing sediment to accumulate in sheltered areas while adjacent faster flow continues to erode or transport. Resistant bedrock does not itself deposit sediment merely because it is hard. Its effect needs a pathway through the flow or channel geometry. Similarly, permeable ground can allow water losses that influence flow, but the resulting deposition must be linked to changed transport conditions rather than assumed from the rock name alone.

An original observation can illustrate selective deposition. Before entering a wide pool, a stream carries gravel, sand and fine suspended material. In the pool, measured near-bed velocity is lower; gravel accumulates at the entrance, some sand settles farther in and fine material remains in the outflow. This supports a decrease in the ability to move the coarse fraction, not the disappearance of all load. If the sample also contains dissolved substances, those remain in solution unless a separate chemical process removes them. Label the solid fractions and dissolved component separately in a diagram or table.

A controlled comparison might measure 0.9 m/s upstream of a pool and 0.3 m/s within it during the same observation. A new sand deposit in the pool is consistent with lower-velocity deposition, provided sediment is supplied and the measurements represent the relevant flow. It does not prove that the pool is permanently a deposition site. A later flood can remobilise the bar. Repeated measurements and dated photographs would provide stronger evidence of change over time. The distinction between one observed event and a persistent pattern is central to river investigation.

Deposition can also affect people. Reservoir sedimentation can reduce storage space, sand accumulation can obstruct channels or intakes, and floodplain deposition can renew some soil material while damaging crops or buildings during an inundation. These are different outcomes at different places and times. A layer of fertile sediment does not make floodwater harmless, nor does sediment in a water supply mean that all particles have the same health effect. Explain the material, location and consequence rather than treating deposition as uniformly beneficial or harmful.

The connections to erosion, transport and depositional landforms are direct. Sediment must be supplied and moved before it can accumulate at a particular location. A local reduction in transporting conditions can produce a bar, levee or delta, while later stronger flows may erode it. Describing the sequence avoids circular explanations such as sediment is deposited because the site is depositional. In a resource task, identify the changed flow, the load component affected and the observed accumulation. Then state the condition under which the explanation holds, using the supplied data rather than an assumed river-course label.

For example, an original rectangular-channel comparison has area 2 m² and mean velocity 1 m/s at the first section, giving discharge 2 m³/s. Farther along, area is 8 m² and mean velocity 0.5 m/s, giving discharge 4 m³/s. Discharge is greater at the second section even though velocity is lower. If the resource shows sediment accumulating there, reduced local velocity is the relevant explanation, not an incorrect claim that the volume passing per second must have decreased.

## 07 Depositional landforms and seasonal channels

Depositional landforms develop where transported material accumulates over time. Their shapes depend on the sediment supplied, changing flow conditions, vegetation, underlying relief and the ways later water movements redistribute the deposits. Deltas, natural levees, marshes and floodplains can occur together but are not synonyms. Inland depositional systems and seasonal oshanas also need their own setting. Identifying each from a resource requires the visible arrangement, water pathways and the meaning of the key, rather than assuming that any flat wet area is a delta.

A delta is a depositional landform built where a river enters a standing body of water, such as a lake or sea, and accumulates sediment faster than it is removed from the site. Flow spreads and some load settles. Repeated deposition can build the land outward and cause channels to divide into distributaries that carry water across the growing deposit. A delta does not have to be a perfect triangle. Waves, tides, river flow and sediment supply influence its outline, and different channels may be active at different times. Describe the shape shown rather than enforcing one geometric form.

In an original mouth resource, the main river enters a lake, divides into three outward branches and crosses a sediment-built area extending into the water. The branching channels are distributaries because flow splits away from the main channel. This contrasts with tributaries that join it upstream. The extending deposit supports a delta interpretation, especially if repeated surveys show accumulation. A channel split alone is not conclusive: rivers can divide around islands elsewhere. The mouth location and evidence of deposition are therefore important parts of the identification.

A natural levee is a raised bank built by river deposition beside the channel. During overbank flooding, water spreads across the surrounding ground and its local transporting conditions change. Coarser suspended material commonly settles relatively close to the channel, while finer material can travel farther across the floodplain. Repeated events build a low ridge next to the river. Natural levees are not the same as engineered embankments constructed by people, although both may lie along a channel. Use the resource’s origin information before classifying a raised bank.

A cross-section might show the main channel flanked by slightly raised strips, with lower, finer-grained ground farther away. That arrangement is consistent with natural levees and backswamp areas. The raised strips can influence which places flood first, but they do not guarantee protection. Water can overtop or breach a levee, and routes of inundation depend on river level and the wider plain. A claim that natural levees prevent flooding would ignore their limited height and discontinuities. Their origin is flood deposition, so the landform itself records a history involving inundation.

A marsh is a wetland commonly dominated by non-woody vegetation such as reeds, grasses or sedges. Water may cover or saturate the ground permanently or seasonally, depending on the setting. Fine sediment accumulation, slow drainage and vegetation can contribute to its development. A marsh can lie beside a river, behind a levee or within a delta, but it is not defined simply by being at a river mouth. A wetland dominated by trees has different vegetation characteristics. In a simple resource description, identify the waterlogged ground and the type of plant cover actually shown.

A plan or photograph record might identify shallow standing water, extensive reeds and a location on low ground away from the main channel. These observations support a marsh interpretation. They do not establish the water’s depth throughout the year or prove that the area is unused by people. Seasonal changes can transform access, grazing and habitat conditions. If the record includes dates, compare them explicitly. An area that looks dry in one photograph may still be an important seasonal wetland, while a single flooded field is not enough to establish a persistent marsh ecosystem without further context.

A floodplain is the relatively flat ground beside a river that is formed and modified by fluvial processes and can be inundated when water leaves the channel. Lateral channel migration can erode parts of the valley floor, while repeated overbank deposition builds layers of alluvium. It is therefore not solely a pile of material deposited during one flood. Meander migration, abandoned channels, levees and wet depressions may all appear across the plain. The surface is broadly flat at the scale of a valley, but local ridges and hollows can strongly influence flooding and land use.

A contour map can support floodplain identification through a broad low-gradient valley floor beside a winding channel. Widely spaced contours indicate gentler slopes, but the absence of contour lines on a small flat area is not sufficient on its own to prove a floodplain. Look for the channel relationship, low relative elevation, alluvial deposits or flood information where supplied. A floodplain may extend farther than the water reached during one recent event. Different flood magnitudes inundate different areas, so a single flood outline should not automatically be treated as the permanent edge of the landform.

An inland delta is a branching depositional system that spreads within an interior basin rather than building directly into the sea. Flow paths, evaporation, infiltration and seasonal inundation can be important. Some inland systems terminate in pans or wetlands instead of maintaining one continuous channel to an ocean. The broad term should not erase local distinctions between distributary networks, shallow seasonal channels and terminal depressions. When a resource names a particular system, describe its documented characteristics rather than assuming that it behaves like a coastal delta with tides and marine waves.

The Cuvelai system in north-central Namibia contains shallow seasonal watercourses called oshanas. Water spreads through a low-relief landscape, and flooding varies between seasons and years. The Ramsar account of the Etosha Pan–Lake Oponono–Cuvelai drainage describes ephemeral rivers, pans and associated seasonal wetlands. This supports teaching the system as a connected seasonal landscape rather than as a permanently flowing single river. The historical site account is not a source for a current population percentage or a present-day flood forecast, so those changing figures are not needed to explain the basic landform and drainage setting.

Water in the Cuvelai can come from local rainfall and inflow from farther north, including Angola during appropriate flood conditions. Shallow channels can be dry for a substantial part of a season and still carry important flows when the basin receives water. Building across or within an oshana can obstruct local movement or expose structures to inundation when water returns. A dry surface at the time of construction is not proof that the channel has ceased to function. The mechanism links seasonal flow, low relief and the use of space, rather than treating flooding as an unexplained surprise.

A supplied comparison can distinguish a coastal delta, a floodplain and an oshana system. Resource A shows distributaries entering the sea across a growing sediment deposit. B shows a broad alluvial valley floor alongside a meandering trunk river. C shows shallow seasonal channels linking depressions across an inland plain. A supports a coastal delta, B a floodplain and C an oshana landscape if the regional context is specified. Their shared low relief does not make them identical. The water pathway, terminal setting and seasonality provide the discriminating evidence.

Descriptions should separate observations from explanations. A fan of channels, a raised river-side strip, reed-covered shallow water and a broad flat valley floor are observable forms. Sediment settling as flow spreads is a mechanism. A defensible answer connects them only where the resource supports the connection. If a raised strip is labelled an artificial flood embankment, it should not be redescribed as a natural levee merely because it occupies a familiar position. If an inland channel is dry on the date shown, describe it as dry then, without inferring that it never floods.

These depositional settings connect river processes to water resources, agriculture, wildlife and settlement. Their sediments and seasonal water can support livelihoods and habitats, while inundation, waterlogging and channel changes create constraints. Management needs the spatial details: a raised site within a floodplain differs from a low channel crossing, and an active distributary differs from a stable dry surface above the flow paths. A map, dated photographs and local flood records together can support a stronger account than one image. The landforms explain possibilities and risks, but actual decisions also need evidence about people and use.

A sediment transect can help distinguish a natural levee from an assumed uniform deposit. Suppose flood sediment beside the channel is relatively coarse and 12 cm thick, while the deposit farther across the plain is finer and 3 cm thick. The contrast supports stronger near-channel accumulation during that stated event. It does not establish the total age of the levee or the average deposit from every flood. To investigate development over time, repeat the transect after later events and keep positions comparable. A bank constructed from imported gravel would require a different origin explanation even if its shape resembled the natural ridge.

## 08 Opportunities and constraints in wetlands

Wetlands such as floodplains and deltas provide water, habitats and resources that can support people. Rivers can supply domestic and livestock water where quantity and quality are suitable. Deposited alluvium can support productive farming in some places, while seasonal grazing and fishing contribute to livelihoods. Reeds and other vegetation may provide useful materials, and distinctive landscapes and wildlife can attract visitors. The benefit depends on access, the timing of water availability and the condition of the resource. A wetland should not be described as uniformly fertile, permanently watered or free from competing uses.

A floodplain can provide level land for cultivation and settlement, but the same low position exposes it to inundation. A flood can damage houses, crops and stored food, contaminate water sources and interrupt roads or schooling. Waterlogging may prevent field operations even after visible floodwater has receded. Sediment deposition can renew some soils while also burying crops or blocking drainage. These outcomes are compatible: a process can provide long-term opportunities and create short-term losses. State the time scale and affected activity rather than labelling the wetland simply good or bad.

Seasonal wetlands can support fish breeding or access to food resources during particular periods. Overfishing or barriers that disrupt movement can reduce those benefits. Vegetation can provide habitat and influence water movement, but it can also complicate access for some activities. Standing or slow water may support disease vectors under suitable conditions, while unsafe water and damaged sanitation can create additional health problems. Avoid claiming that every marsh directly causes a particular disease. Health outcomes depend on organisms, human exposure, water quality and prevention or treatment arrangements.

The Cuvelai oshanas illustrate the importance of seasonality. Shallow watercourses and seasonal wetlands have supported local water use, grazing, cultivation and fishing, while houses or roads placed across active flow paths can be flooded when water returns. Research reported through Namibia’s Environmental Information Service describes the problem of building within apparently dry oshanas and later experiencing inundation. This is a documented pattern, not a measured claim about every settlement. It links the benefit of access to a seasonal landscape with the risk of treating a dry-period observation as a complete picture of its behaviour.

The Zambezi floodplain in northeastern Namibia provides another useful setting for considering agriculture, water and flood exposure. Environmental assessment information for the region describes extensive low-lying floodplains and seasonal wetland conditions. Such a setting can support flood-related livelihoods while creating access and inundation constraints. The teaching point is the relationship between seasonal water, low relief and human use. Current crop yields, household losses or the date and extent of a particular flood would require their own dated evidence and should not be invented to make the case appear more detailed.

Consider a hypothetical family choosing between a lower field beside a seasonal channel and a slightly raised field farther away. The lower field offers easier water access in the wet period but is inundated more frequently in the supplied record. The raised field is less exposed in that record but needs water transport during dry conditions. A judgement should compare the intended crop, flood timing, access and available resources. Choosing the lower field because floods bring nutrients would ignore possible crop damage; choosing the raised field because it is higher would ignore the water-access cost.

Wetland benefits are not distributed equally. A fishing household, a livestock keeper, a downstream water user and a road operator may value the same seasonal flood differently. An embankment protecting one settlement can alter flow or water access elsewhere. A water abstraction supporting irrigated crops can reduce water available to a wetland during sensitive periods. These links make basin-scale and community evidence useful. A supported discussion identifies who benefits, who bears a cost and under what conditions, rather than listing unrelated advantages and disadvantages with no connection to the place.

This connects wetlands to population, settlement, agriculture and water management. Use a named example to explain a specific link, such as dry-season building within an oshana followed by exposure when flow returns. A place name by itself does not supply a mechanism. For a resource judgement, use the actual flood record, map or livelihood information provided, and retain uncertainty where the evidence covers only one season. The objective is a balanced understanding of opportunities and constraints, not a rule that people should always settle in wetlands or should abandon every floodplain.

## 09 Human impacts and wetland management

People can alter wetlands through drainage, cultivation, settlement, abstraction, pollution, grazing, dam construction and the building of roads or embankments. The effect depends on the scale, location and timing of the activity. Drainage can make land available for a particular use but reduce waterlogged habitats and alter storage or flow pathways. Abstraction can reduce water available to downstream ecosystems during sensitive periods. Pollution can affect water quality and organisms. These mechanisms should be explained separately rather than grouped into a vague claim that development destroys nature.

Removing vegetation can expose soil to erosion and reduce habitat cover. Sediment carried into a wetland can change water depth, smother some habitats or accumulate in channels. Overgrazing near water points may concentrate trampling and bank damage, while carefully managed grazing can have different effects. Wastewater and agricultural runoff can introduce substances that change water quality. The presence of a farm does not itself establish pollution; evidence about the substances, pathway and receiving water is needed. A causal account identifies the source, how material reaches the wetland and the resulting effect.

A road crossing can obstruct or redirect shallow flow if it lacks adequate openings. In an oshana landscape, this can cause water to accumulate on one side or move through concentrated paths. Culverts or bridges designed for the relevant flows can maintain connectivity, but their capacity, position and maintenance matter. A blocked culvert does not perform like an open one shown on a plan. Comparing water levels, flow paths and dated road construction can help investigate the effect, while recognising that rainfall differences between years may also influence the observed flooding.

Management can include protecting sensitive areas, maintaining buffer vegetation, controlling waste discharges, limiting abstraction where appropriate, restoring flow connections and planning settlement outside the most exposed active channels. Such measures need monitoring and enforcement, not only written rules. Local knowledge of seasonal routes and past floods can improve planning, especially where short records make rare events difficult to assess. Protection should also consider livelihoods: restrictions that remove access to water or grazing without alternatives can create hardship and reduce support for the management approach.

Restoration aims to recover functions that have been degraded, such as water movement, habitat or vegetation cover. Reopening a blocked channel or rehabilitating damaged banks may help, but a restored area will not necessarily return quickly to a previous condition. Sediment, water quality, land use and upstream controls can limit recovery. A management proposal should state its intended outcome and how success will be checked. Repeated observations of flow, vegetation, water quality or resource availability are more useful than declaring success because a structure was built or a meeting was held.

In a hypothetical wetland crossing, households report higher water on the upstream side after a road was raised. The resource also records a damaged, sediment-filled culvert and unusually heavy rainfall that year. Clearing and assessing the culvert is a relevant response because it addresses a documented obstruction, but the observations do not isolate the road as the sole cause of high water. A stronger investigation compares conditions before and after repair, records rainfall and monitors both sides. This example shows how an immediate practical action and a cautious causal conclusion can coexist.

Management choices can create trade-offs across the basin. Preventing all seasonal inundation of one low-lying area could remove water needed by fisheries or grazing systems elsewhere. Conversely, maintaining natural flows without considering homes and routes can leave people exposed. The aim is not to freeze every landscape change but to understand functions, risks and uses well enough to choose defensible measures. Agreements on timing, access, abstraction and maintenance can be important alongside physical works. The best measure depends on the stated problem and the evidence, not on its name alone.

These ideas connect to research skills and water-resource management. A useful conclusion relates a specific human action to a documented pathway and evaluates an intervention against a clear objective. If the objective is keeping an oshana crossing open during seasonal flows, culvert placement and maintenance are relevant; a distant tree-planting project may not address the immediate obstruction. If the objective is water quality, identifying and treating pollutant sources may be more direct. Explain the conditions and limitations of the proposed response, rather than assuming complete protection from a single intervention.

A practical monitoring plan can assign named responsibilities without assuming that responsibility alone ensures action. A local group might record whether crossing openings remain clear before the wet season, while technical staff assess whether their size and position suit the expected flows. Dated photographs and repeated upstream/downstream water-level observations provide evidence of performance. If a repaired crossing still causes ponding, the response needs reassessment rather than an automatic claim of success. The observation period should include relevant flow conditions; a dry-season inspection can find blockage but cannot demonstrate how the crossing performs during a flood.

## 91 Measuring a river channel

A river investigation begins with an aim, a safe site and permission or supervision arrangements. Do not enter deep, fast or rising water to obtain a measurement. Use an approved safe reach or supplied data instead. At a suitable shallow site, a tape stretched across the channel at right angles to flow can establish width. Measure depth at stated regular intervals along that line, recording each position and value. Keep the depth measure vertical and use consistent units so another group can understand the cross-section.

Represent the results on supplied axes with distance across the channel horizontally and depth vertically, making the direction of the depth scale clear. Plot the measured points and connect them appropriately for the stated task. A single deepest reading is not the mean depth. For a simple calculation, average comparable depth observations according to the method specified; estimating area with equal-width strips can be more informative where the bed is irregular. State any bank-edge or missing-depth assumptions instead of treating them as observations.

Repeat measurements where practical and record the date, recent weather and relevant water level. A cross-section represents one reach and set of conditions, not every place or season on the river. Differences between sites may reflect rock, bank shape, recent flooding or human structures as well as downstream position. A useful conclusion reports the measured pattern, supports it with selected values and identifies the limits of the sample. This links the investigation to map profiles, discharge estimates and wider research skills without requiring unsafe fieldwork to complete the learning task.
