# Weather and climate

## 01 Short-term conditions and longer-term patterns

Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time or over a short period. It includes temperature, precipitation, humidity, wind, pressure, cloud and sunshine. Climate describes the longer-term patterns and variability of weather in a region, commonly assessed using records spanning decades. A climate description includes seasonal differences and the range or frequency of events, not simply one average. A hot afternoon is a weather observation; a pattern of hotter summers across a long record concerns climate.

The distinction is mainly scale of time and the evidence used. One wet day does not establish that a desert has become a humid climate, and one cool day does not disprove a longer-term warming trend. Short events occur within a variable climate. To judge change, compare appropriate periods, comparable observations and their variation. A statement that a place is usually dry can coexist with an occasional heavy rainfall event. The event should be recorded accurately rather than discarded because it seems inconsistent with the general pattern.

A supplied record might give today’s maximum temperature as 29°C and rainfall as 0 mm, while a multi-decade summary identifies a dry season with occasional rain. The first describes weather; the second summarises climate. Neither alone predicts the conditions on every future day. This connects weather measurement to climate graphs, climatic regions and environmental change. The quality and length of the record matter: a short school investigation can compare local observations, but it cannot substitute for a long-term climate series merely by calculating an average of a few days.

## 02 The Stevenson screen

A Stevenson screen is an instrument shelter designed to help measure representative air temperature and humidity while reducing direct effects of sunshine, precipitation and nearby heated surfaces. The familiar school model is a raised, white, louvred enclosure. Its slatted sides allow air circulation while shielding instruments. The white exterior reflects much incoming solar radiation, reducing heating of the shelter relative to a darker surface under similar conditions. A roof provides protection from direct radiation and precipitation; designs can include a double roof to reduce heat transfer. Modern shelters can use different materials, so the function should be understood rather than reduced to wood alone.

Thermometers need exposure to the air being measured. Direct sunshine on a sensor can heat it above the surrounding air temperature, while contact with a strongly heated wall or ground surface can introduce a different influence. The screen therefore shields the sensor without sealing it in a stagnant box. Louvre ventilation and suitable siting work together. A white sealed cupboard is not equivalent to a ventilated screen, and a louvred box against a heated wall may still produce an unrepresentative observation. Describe the design feature and then connect it to the error it helps reduce.

The screen is normally placed above a representative natural surface, often short grass, with instruments at a standard specified height. Different observing standards give precise height requirements, so follow the stated method rather than inventing one universal number from a drawing. Raising the instruments reduces the direct influence of surface heating while keeping the reading relevant to near-surface air. A screen on a hot paved courtyard can measure a different local environment from one over grass. That difference may be a legitimate object of study, but it should not be hidden within an attempt to compare standard observations.

An open site away from immediate shading and heat sources helps comparability. Buildings, trees, vehicles and reflective surfaces can affect airflow, radiation or local temperature. The aim is not to remove every influence from the atmosphere but to document exposure and reduce avoidable measurement effects. The screen should be maintained, with louvres and instruments in suitable condition. A damaged door, blocked ventilation or a sensor touching the enclosure can alter performance. Record a site change because a time series before and after moving a screen may not be directly comparable without considering the new exposure.

Door orientation should reduce direct sunlight entering while observations are taken. In the Southern Hemisphere, school siting guidance commonly uses a south-facing door; a UK north-facing instruction belongs to the opposite hemisphere and should not be copied without adjustment. The principle is shielding the instruments during reading. Open the door only as needed, avoid shading or warming the sensor unnecessarily with the observer’s body and read the appropriate scale without parallax. A thermometer removed and held in a warm hand no longer represents the same exposure as an undisturbed screen reading.

A useful supplied comparison places screen A over short grass with free airflow and screen B against a sun-heated wall with obstructed louvres. Higher readings at B could reflect the different exposure, so they should not immediately be interpreted as a regional climate difference. The comparison identifies plausible measurement influences but does not isolate which one dominates, because surface, wall proximity and ventilation all changed together. A stronger test varies one feature while controlling the others or records enough information to assess their combined effect.

Only suitable instruments belong inside the screen. Air-temperature and humidity instruments need its sheltered ventilation, while a rain gauge must collect precipitation openly and an anemometer needs appropriate wind exposure. A sunshine recorder must receive sunshine rather than be hidden in the enclosure. A station plan should therefore match each instrument to the variable and exposure it requires. Placing every instrument in one protected box would prevent several from measuring the intended element. This is a practical consequence of understanding function rather than merely recognising a labelled drawing.

These precautions connect equipment to data quality. A daily range calculated perfectly from poorly exposed thermometers can still misrepresent the intended air temperature. Consistent reading times, calibration or checks, reset procedures and clear records are needed alongside suitable siting. A local school investigation should state its exposure and limitations rather than claiming to reproduce every national observing standard. The screen reduces important unwanted influences, but it does not make all measurements from all sites identical. A supported interpretation considers both the atmospheric pattern and how the observations were obtained.

## 03 Weather instruments

A weather station uses different instruments because temperature, rainfall, humidity, pressure, wind and sunshine are different variables. Identify the instrument from its working parts and purpose, not simply its position in a familiar picture. A funnel and collecting vessel suggest a rain gauge, paired thermometers with a wet covering suggest a psychrometer, and rotating cups suggest an anemometer. The unit belongs with the variable: degrees Celsius for temperature, millimetres for rainfall depth, hectopascals for pressure and an appropriate speed unit for wind. An instrument name without its measured element is incomplete understanding.

A rain gauge collects precipitation through an opening of known area. In a simple manual design, a funnel directs water into a container that is emptied into a suitable graduated measuring cylinder. The cylinder or system is calibrated to express rainfall as an equivalent depth over the collecting area. Rainfall depth is not the same as the raw depth of water in any arbitrary bottle. A narrow cylinder can contain a deeper column than the amount of rain represented, because the funnel has collected water from a wider area. Use the supplied calibration rather than treating the apparatus as an unscaled container.

The gauge rim should be level and appropriately exposed, with nearby obstacles and splash effects considered. Trees can intercept water or drip into the opening; buildings can shelter it or disturb airflow. A blocked funnel, tilted gauge or unrecorded overflow can reduce the reliability of the result. In a manual method, read at the defined time, record the interval and empty or reset according to the procedure. A gauge containing two days of rain cannot be labelled a one-day total unless the collection history supports that interval. Missing or compromised readings should be marked rather than converted into zero rainfall.

A maximum thermometer records the highest temperature reached since its last reset, while a minimum thermometer records the lowest. A combined maximum–minimum or Six’s thermometer uses indices to preserve the extremes. The current liquid level is not necessarily the extreme indicated by an index. Read the labelled scale and the specified end of the index, because the orientation and arrangement of scales can differ. Reset only after recording, using the approved method for the particular instrument. A missing reset changes the period represented by the next observation, so it belongs in the record.

An original instrument resource might label the maximum index at 28°C, the minimum at 12°C and the current reading at 20°C. Maximum and minimum refer to the interval since reset, not to future forecasts. The daily range for a correctly specified daily interval is 28 − 12 = 16°C. Reading the current 20°C as the maximum would confuse the variable being recorded. A question image should leave indices and scales readable; a low-resolution drawing that makes 26 and 28 indistinguishable cannot support a precise numerical answer fairly.

A wet-and-dry-bulb hygrometer, or psychrometer, uses paired thermometers. The wet bulb has a covering supplied with water; evaporation removes heat and can cool it below the dry bulb. The difference depends on humidity and ventilation. In drier air, evaporation is generally greater under comparable conditions, producing a larger depression. If wet and dry readings coincide under the appropriate functioning conditions, the air is saturated and relative humidity is 100%. A dry wick or poor ventilation can undermine the inference, so equality of readings should not be interpreted without considering the instrument’s condition.

Relative humidity is obtained using the dry-bulb temperature, wet-bulb depression and an appropriate supplied table or calculation. Do not interpret the depression itself as a percentage. For example, dry bulb 25°C and wet bulb 20°C give a depression of 5°C. If the supplied table for the stated conditions gives 63%, that is the relative humidity value to report. A different table or pressure assumption can affect the conversion, so use the resource provided. The wet-bulb temperature is not automatically the dew point, although they coincide with air temperature at saturation in the relevant limit.

A barometer measures atmospheric pressure. It can use a liquid column or a calibrated sensor such as an aneroid mechanism. A barograph records pressure over time, historically with a pen and chart and in modern systems often digitally. A single barometer reading gives pressure at that observation; a recording trace shows changes during a period. Check whether values are station pressure or adjusted to a reference level when comparing places of different altitude. Otherwise a difference due to elevation can be mistaken for the same kind of weather-system contrast shown on a sea-level pressure chart.

Pressure is force per unit area, not a temperature scale. A barometer reading of 1,010 hPa does not mean that the air is 1,010 degrees or that a lower pressure centre must have lower air temperature than every surrounding place. A falling trace can be relevant to an approaching change in conditions, but it does not alone establish the amount or timing of rainfall. The wider system, moisture and air motion matter. A barograph resource should have legible pressure and time axes, a stated interval and a clear meaning for any reference adjustment.

An anemometer measures wind speed. A common cup design rotates as air moves past it, and the rotation is converted through calibration to speed. Different designs use other sensing methods, so cups are a recognisable example rather than the definition. Appropriate exposure matters: a sheltered courtyard reading may represent that courtyard rather than an unobstructed regional wind. Record the unit, height or site context and averaging interval where supplied. A gust is not the same statistic as a longer-period mean. Comparing one site’s peak gust with another’s mean without explanation can create a misleading contrast.

A wind vane indicates wind direction. Its directional indication is interpreted as the direction from which the wind comes, according to the instrument’s design and reference. A northerly wind comes from the north and moves generally towards the south. A diagram must show the cardinal directions or orientation and enough information to distinguish the vane’s pointing end. Do not assume that an arrow on a decorative drawing has the same meaning as a meteorological wind arrow or barb. Read the key and the stated convention before converting the picture into a direction.

The vane needs to turn freely and be correctly aligned with north. Nearby structures can change local flow, while a stuck vane can produce repeated readings that look consistent but are wrong. Calm conditions may have no meaningful prevailing direction, so a calm record should not automatically inherit the previous direction. If direction changes during an interval, record or summarise it using the stated method rather than inventing one continuous arrow. Wind direction is circular data: a simple arithmetic mean of degrees can fail near north, where 350° and 10° are close rather than opposite.

A sunshine recorder measures sunshine duration, not air temperature or total daylight length. A traditional Campbell–Stokes recorder uses a glass sphere to focus sunlight onto a suitable card, leaving a trace during sufficiently bright conditions. Read the burnt sections against the card’s time markings, distinguishing gaps. Modern electronic sensors use other methods. A six-hour sunshine total does not mean the sun was above the horizon for only six hours; clouds and the instrument’s detection conditions affect recorded bright sunshine. The recorder requires an appropriate unobstructed exposure and correct setup for the date and site.

An original card can show burn intervals from 08:00–10:00, 11:00–13:30 and 15:00–16:30. Their durations are 2, 2.5 and 1.5 hours, giving six hours in total. Adding the whole interval from 08:00 to 16:30 would incorrectly include the unburnt gaps. A drawing should show the time scale clearly enough to support that reading. Likewise, a sunshine graph labelled hours cannot be interpreted as rainfall in millimetres just because both quantities use bars in some other diagram. Axis labels and units determine the variable.

Cloud amount is often estimated by observation rather than one of the instruments just described. A standard school system uses oktas: eighths of the visible sky covered by cloud. Zero indicates clear sky and eight an overcast sky under the stated observation convention. Cloud type describes form and level, which is different from amount. An observation of extensive thin high cloud and one of a small low dark cloud can differ in type and coverage. Do not infer rainfall directly from cloud amount alone; cloud depth, moisture and the wider atmospheric conditions matter.

Instrument siting should match purpose. The screen shelters temperature and humidity sensors, the rain gauge collects precipitation openly, the wind instruments need suitable airflow, and the sunshine recorder needs an unobstructed view for its measurement. A station plan should show these functions coherently rather than cluster everything under a tree for protection. If the aim is comparing sheltered and exposed microclimates, non-standard sites may be deliberately selected, but their exposure then becomes part of the investigation and must be recorded. Standardisation and deliberate local comparison are different research designs.

An original matching resource can include a funnel-and-vessel instrument, paired wet/dry thermometers, a rotating-cup instrument, a direction vane and a recording pressure trace. Match each to rainfall depth, humidity inference, wind speed, direction and pressure change respectively. The key should state any unfamiliar design. A fair interpretation does not require a learner to infer an invisible wick or an unreadable scale. Providing the relevant working part makes recognition an application of the physical principle, rather than a guess from where the picture is placed on the page.

These instruments connect observation to weather statistics, synoptic charts and climate records. An accurate sum or mean cannot compensate for an instrument measuring the wrong element or a record with an unknown interval. Check variable, unit, calibration, exposure and time before calculating. Then distinguish measured values from interpretations such as low pressure or an unusually wet period, which require a reference. This sequence creates a traceable account from apparatus to observation to conclusion, and it helps explain why apparently precise weather numbers can still carry important limitations.

A rain-gauge diagram should distinguish the collecting opening from the measuring scale. If a key states that 20 mL in the measuring vessel corresponds to 1 mm of rainfall for that collector, a 120 mL observation represents 6 mm. This stated calibration is a teaching example; it cannot be transferred to another funnel size without justification. The volume in millilitres and rainfall depth in millimetres are related through the collecting area, not interchangeable units.

A combined thermometer image must also make the reset period explicit. Indices recorded at 09:00 can represent extremes since the previous reset, while a simultaneous dry-bulb value is the temperature then. The three values need not coincide. If the maximum index was not reset yesterday, today's indicated maximum may span a longer interval and cannot be compared uncritically with a neighbouring daily record. Marking the data limitation preserves information instead of inventing a correction.

For wind instruments, inspect the reference as well as the moving part. A vane can rotate freely but be misaligned with the north marker, producing a consistent directional error. An anemometer can provide speed while supplying no direction on its own. Combining their readings requires the same or clearly related observation interval. A pressure trace, by contrast, uses a time axis, and the pen or sensor location should not be interpreted as a compass bearing. These distinctions help decode an unfamiliar instrument resource from its declared function.

Read a graduated scale at the stated index or meniscus and with a suitable line of sight to reduce parallax. Check the unit and smallest marked division before interpolating. A value more precise than the visible scale can justify should not be invented.

## 04 Recording and analysing weather statistics

Weather statistics begin with a clear variable, unit, place and observation interval. Temperature, rainfall, pressure, humidity, cloud, sunshine and wind describe different properties, so the operation used to summarise one may not suit another. A total answers how much accumulated; a mean describes an average of comparable observations; a range describes the difference between the highest and lowest. Decide which quantity the task requests before calculating. A neatly presented answer to the wrong operation remains the wrong statistic, even if the arithmetic is correct.

For a daily maximum of 28°C and minimum of 12°C, diurnal range is 28 − 12 = 16°C. The midpoint of those two extremes is (28 + 12)/2 = 20°C. This midpoint can be a specified school estimate of mean daily temperature, but it is not identical to the mean of every observation or a continuous record unless the method makes that equivalence. State the method used. A monthly mean temperature summarises appropriate daily or repeated observations over a month, while annual temperature range commonly compares the warmest and coolest monthly mean values, not one afternoon’s maximum and night’s minimum.

Temperature differences retain degrees as their unit. Subtracting a negative value needs care: if maximum is 6°C and minimum −4°C, range is 6 − (−4) = 10°C. A range cannot be made negative by reversing the subtraction. In an original three-observation set of 10, 20 and 30°C, the sum is 60°C as an arithmetic total, mean 20°C and range 20°C. The last two happen to share the numerical value here, but represent different ideas. A different set can separate them more clearly, so do not infer that mean and range are interchangeable.

Rainfall is usually recorded as a depth over an area for an interval, commonly millimetres. Adding comparable non-overlapping daily depths gives a period total. For original daily values 0, 4, 6 and 2 mm, the four-day total is 12 mm and mean per day is 3 mm. The range of daily amounts is 6 mm. The total is not obtained by subtracting wettest and driest days. A missing day must not be silently assigned zero: no observation and a measured dry day are different. If a gauge was not emptied, its accumulated reading may cover more than one day.

Rainfall intensity is amount per unit time, whereas total rainfall is an accumulated amount. Ten millimetres in one hour and ten millimetres across ten hours have equal totals but different average intensities. This matters when interpreting runoff or short-term effects. A monthly climate graph usually shows totals by month, so adding its bars gives an annual rainfall total if all twelve are complete and comparable. Averaging those bars gives mean monthly rainfall, not annual rainfall. Read the label and interval rather than deciding from the use of bars alone.

Relative humidity expresses the water-vapour state in relation to saturation at the relevant temperature, usually as a percentage. It does not directly give the mass of vapour in a cubic metre or the amount of rain expected. Temperature changes can alter relative humidity even where vapour content changes little, because saturation conditions depend on temperature. The statement that air has a low relative humidity therefore does not by itself establish a lower absolute vapour amount than all cooler air. Use the variable actually measured and avoid treating the percentage as a rainfall probability.

For paired thermometers, wet-bulb depression is dry minus wet temperature. At dry 25°C and wet 20°C, depression is 5°C. Use the dry value and depression to find the relative humidity in the supplied table for its stated conditions; a sample lookup giving 63% is not the same as the depression of 5°C. If the readings coincide at 20°C with a functioning wet bulb and appropriate ventilation, the indicated state is saturation. If the wick is dry, however, the instrument setup is compromised. A data-quality note should remain attached to such a reading rather than treating it as sound evidence of saturated air.

Atmospheric pressure commonly uses hectopascals, with one hectopascal numerically equal to one millibar. A recording trace can show rising, falling or steady pressure over a stated interval. Original readings of 1,016, 1,012 and 1,008 hPa show an 8 hPa fall from first to last. They do not show an 8°C cooling. If the times are six hours apart, the full first-to-last interval is twelve hours, not six. A rate of change needs both the pressure difference and correct time interval. Pressure relative to surrounding places is important for identifying a high or low system.

A station at high elevation may have a lower unadjusted pressure than a coastal station because less atmosphere lies above it. Synoptic charts often use pressure adjusted to a common reference so the mapped gradients concern weather systems rather than merely topography. Check the resource’s convention. A comparison mixing one station’s raw value with another’s adjusted value may be misleading. Similarly, a low pressure designation is not a direct statement about temperature. Analyse pressure, temperature, moisture and air movement as related but distinct variables.

Cloud amount can be estimated in oktas. A record of 2 oktas corresponds to roughly two eighths of the sky covered under the observation convention, while 6 oktas is six eighths. Comparing the values shows greater cloud amount at the second observation, not necessarily thicker cloud or heavier rainfall. Cloud type adds information about form and level. A school resource may identify low layered stratus, heap-like cumulus or high wispy cirrus, while cumulonimbus can indicate deep convective development. The label and image evidence should support the classification rather than using colour alone.

An original sky record can show 2 oktas of high thin cloud at A and 6 oktas of low layered cloud at B. B has greater coverage and a different type in the supplied record. A conclusion that B must have heavier rain is unsupported without precipitation or suitable cloud-process evidence. Darker appearance can reflect thickness, lighting or image settings. A satellite infrared image adds cloud-top information but is not equivalent to looking upward and estimating the local sky in eighths. Keep the observation method clear when comparing these resources.

Sunshine duration measures the time for which sufficiently bright direct sunshine is recorded. It differs from daylight, which concerns the time between sunrise and sunset, and from the total energy received. An original sunshine card with intervals 08:00–10:00, 11:00–13:30 and 15:00–16:30 records 2 + 2.5 + 1.5 = 6 hours. The unmarked gaps must not be added. A cloudy day can still have long daylight but short recorded sunshine. A clear-looking satellite pixel at one moment does not establish the day’s total duration.

For a weekly set of sunshine totals, state whether the task wants the total hours or mean hours per recorded day. If values are 4, 6 and 8 hours for three comparable days, the total is 18 hours and the mean is 6 hours per day. Dividing by seven would treat four missing days as if they had recorded zero, which is unjustified. A zero means the instrument recorded no qualifying sunshine for that interval; a missing mark can mean absent data. Data presentation should preserve this difference with a clear code.

Wind speed needs an appropriate unit and averaging period. Metres per second, kilometres per hour and knots are not interchangeable numbers. A stated conversion can be used where required; for example 5 m/s equals 18 km/h because one metre per second is 3.6 kilometres per hour. A gust maximum is different from a mean over ten minutes. If A has a 20 km/h mean and B a 30 km/h gust, the resource does not establish that B’s mean wind was stronger. Compare like statistics or explain the mismatch.

An original speed record of 2, 4 and 6 m/s has mean 4 m/s and range 4 m/s. Again, numerical equality does not make the operations identical. The mean describes the average of those samples, not necessarily the entire day if they were taken only during one short interval. Surface obstacles and instrument height can also affect comparability. A measurement in a sheltered yard may be valid for the yard while unsuitable as a direct substitute for an exposed coastal station. State the purpose before deciding whether the exposure is an error or a deliberate study condition.

Wind direction names where the air comes from. A west wind arrives from the west and flows generally eastward. Bearings use a north reference and a stated convention. Near north, directions such as 350° and 10° are only 20° apart, so their simple arithmetic mean of 180° would incorrectly suggest south. A compass or circular summary is needed. At school level, a frequency table or wind rose can show which source directions occur most often. Calm observations should be distinguished rather than assigned an invented direction.

An original wind-frequency table might record N on two occasions, E on six, S on one and W on one, with two additional calm intervals. East is the most frequent observed direction, but the denominator matters if calculating a percentage: six of ten directional observations is 60%, whereas six of twelve total intervals is 50%. State which question is being answered. The calm intervals are valid information, not missing values. A wind rose should make its frequency scale and treatment of calm clear, while any speed categories need a separate legend.

A station table can combine all elements, but interpretation should not confuse their axes. A day with 0 mm rainfall, 6 hours sunshine, 2 oktas cloud and pressure 1,018 hPa has several measured properties. It does not follow that every high-pressure day has those exact values or that sunshine caused the pressure reading. To analyse a relationship, use multiple comparable observations and consider mechanisms and other factors. A short association can suggest an explanation while remaining insufficient for a general climate rule. Distinguish a description of the table from the wider physical inference.

Choose graphs according to the variable and purpose. A time line can show temperature or pressure change; rainfall totals often use bars; a climate graph combines monthly rainfall bars with a temperature line and separate labelled units. A wind rose represents direction frequencies. Scales must cover the data and intervals should be clear. A change of axis range can exaggerate or conceal apparent variation, so compare values as well as shapes. A graph without its unit, period or key leaves interpretation uncertain even if its points are plotted neatly.

These statistics connect instrument observations to atmospheric processes, climate regions and enquiry conclusions. Preserve missing values, stated limitations and unusual events rather than editing the record to fit an expected pattern. Repeated checks can identify a transcription or reset error, but a surprising value is not automatically false. A defensible conclusion selects the correct operation, shows enough working to identify it, reports the unit and time frame and states what the sample supports. The result should answer the particular question rather than offering an unrelated total, average or range because it was easier to calculate.

A combined original station record can test several operations without conflating them. On three comparable days, maximum temperatures are 24, 28 and 26°C, rainfall totals 0, 8 and 4 mm, and sunshine totals 8, 3 and 6 hours. Mean maximum temperature is 26°C; its range is 4°C. Rainfall total is 12 mm and mean daily rainfall is 4 mm. Sunshine total is 17 hours. Each result belongs to its variable and interval. The coincidence that the temperature range and mean daily rainfall are both numerically four does not give them the same unit or meaning.

If the question asks for the wettest day, identify the 8 mm observation rather than reporting the 12 mm total. If it asks for the range of maxima, use 28 minus 24, not the third day's 26°C. If a graph joins the three temperature values, the points represent the supplied daily maxima, not continuously measured temperatures between them. A smooth joining line is a presentation convention and does not establish every intervening value. This distinction is useful when interpreting both weather and climate graphs.

A source table should state whether a symbol means zero, trace, not observed or instrument failure. Replacing all symbols with zero can change means and totals in different ways. A missing value may justify reporting a partial total with an explicit coverage note rather than a complete-period total. A trace of rainfall can be recorded under the specified convention without inventing an exact depth below the gauge's resolution. The objective is a faithful account of what was measured, including the limitations.

A numerical result should also have sensible precision. If rainfall is recorded to the nearest millimetre, a mean calculated from several days may have a fractional value, but many decimal places do not create finer supplied observations. State the rounding used where appropriate. Keep the working long enough to show the selected operation before rounding the final answer. This avoids different intermediate rounding decisions obscuring an otherwise correct comparison.

## 05 High and low pressure systems

A high-pressure system is an area of relatively higher atmospheric pressure than its surroundings, while a low is a relative minimum. The comparison concerns pressure, not the labels hot and cold. Large-scale surface flow tends to diverge from highs and converge towards lows, with rotation influenced by the Earth’s rotation and friction. In the Southern Hemisphere, surface circulation around a low is generally clockwise and inward, while circulation around a high is generally anticlockwise and outward. Northern Hemisphere diagrams use the opposite rotational sense, so the hemisphere must be stated.

Sinking air is commonly associated with high pressure. As air descends into higher surrounding pressure, it is compressed and warms. Its relative humidity can decrease, reducing the likelihood of cloud forming from that descent alone. High-pressure conditions are therefore often associated with settled, clearer weather. This is a tendency with conditions, not a guarantee that every high contains no cloud. Fog, low cloud, inversions or local moisture can occur in some situations. Temperature at the ground also depends on season, radiation, surface and air mass rather than pressure label alone.

Rising air is commonly associated with low-pressure systems. As it rises into lower surrounding pressure it expands and cools. If it cools sufficiently for saturation and condensation, cloud can form, and suitable cloud processes can produce precipitation. Moisture and sufficient ascent matter: rising air can remain without measurable precipitation when moisture or cloud development is insufficient. A dry low can exist, and a low-pressure reading does not mean the air must be colder than air in a nearby high. Explain the vertical motion and moisture conditions rather than treating pressure and temperature as the same quantity.

An original pair of resources can make the contrast explicit. A shows a relative high with descending air, warming during descent and reduced relative humidity. B shows a relative low with converging flow and moist air rising, cooling and condensing. A supports a tendency towards reduced cloud formation from descent; B supports cloud development under the supplied moist conditions. Reversing the causal chain to say that sinking cools the air and necessarily produces rain would be incorrect. The difference is pressure-related expansion or compression, not a rule that all movement changes temperature in the same direction.

The pressure gradient influences wind, while friction and rotation affect its path. Closely spaced isobars on a chart generally indicate a stronger horizontal pressure gradient than widely spaced isobars at a comparable map scale. That can support an expectation of stronger winds under otherwise comparable conditions. Spacing is not itself a measured wind speed in kilometres per hour. The map scale, location and other factors matter. A resource should therefore distinguish a qualitative gradient inference from an actual anemometer value or a forecast supplied by a model.

High and low systems can move and change, so a station’s sequence may show pressure falling as a low approaches and rising as conditions change. The accompanying weather depends on the system’s structure, fronts and moisture. A single falling reading cannot establish the amount of later rain. Comparing charts and observations through time gives a more supported explanation than reading one symbol as a complete forecast. If the question asks for observed conditions, use the station data; if it asks for likely conditions, make the inference conditional and explain its physical basis.

These distinctions connect pressure systems to rainfall types, synoptic interpretation and Namibia’s climate controls. Subsiding air can contribute to dry conditions over a region, while moisture-bearing winds and uplift can produce different weather elsewhere. The mechanisms interact with ocean currents, altitude and seasonal changes. A useful account states the system, hemisphere, vertical motion and moisture conditions before describing the likely outcome. It does not infer low temperature from the word low or erase a cloudy observation because the simple high-pressure model suggests clearer weather.

An original pressure record can pair a relative low of 1,004 hPa with an air temperature of 27°C and a relative high of 1,020 hPa with 18°C. The low is warmer in this example, showing why the words low and high cannot be read as temperature labels. The pressure comparison still needs the same reference convention, while likely cloud or rain also needs vertical motion and moisture information. The values illustrate a distinction rather than a universal ordering of the temperatures of highs and lows.

## 06 Convectional, cyclonic and relief rainfall

Rainfall requires more than water vapour being present. Air commonly needs to rise and cool sufficiently for condensation, with subsequent processes growing droplets or ice particles that can fall as precipitation. Convectional, cyclonic and relief rainfall are distinguished mainly by the mechanism lifting the air. They can overlap in real weather systems. A mountainous place can receive frontal rain, and daytime heating can influence clouds in a wider low-pressure system. Identify the lifting evidence supplied rather than assigning a rainfall type solely from the place name.

Convectional rainfall begins when the surface heats the air above it. A sufficiently warm, buoyant parcel can rise, expand and cool as surrounding pressure falls with height. If it contains enough moisture and reaches saturation, condensation forms cloud. Continued development under suitable conditions can produce showers or thunderstorms. Strong daytime heating can favour this sequence, but sunshine alone does not ensure rain. A dry or stable atmosphere may limit cloud growth. The explanation needs heating, buoyancy, ascent, cooling and moisture rather than a single statement that the sun makes rain.

An original time record can show surface warming through the morning, rising air over a heated area, cloud growth and an afternoon shower. The sequence is consistent with convectional rainfall if the moisture and ascent are established. It does not mean that every afternoon shower at every site is purely convectional or that one temperature reading proves the whole sequence. A diagram should place the heating at the surface and rising air above it, rather than showing rain rising from the ground. Condensation is associated with cooling during ascent, not with the air becoming progressively hotter simply because it rises.

Relief or orographic rainfall occurs where moving moist air is forced to rise over higher land. Rising air expands and cools, and condensation can produce cloud and precipitation when conditions are suitable. The windward side faces the incoming flow; the leeward side is downwind. After crossing the high ground, descending air can warm by compression and become relatively drier, contributing to a rain-shadow effect. The contrast depends on moisture, mountain height, wind and the wider weather, so it is not a promise that every leeward location receives no rain.

A resource showing moist onshore wind approaching a ridge, cloud and rainfall on the rising side and descending warming air beyond supports relief rainfall. Identify windward and leeward from the arrows, not from a fixed left/right convention. If the diagram is reversed, the named sides reverse too. The summit is not automatically the wettest point without observations, and rainfall can extend beyond the crest. A supported description uses the supplied distribution. The process explains a contrast, while the actual measured amounts belong to the resource rather than to an invented universal ratio.

Cyclonic rainfall is associated with rising air in a low-pressure system. In a frontal depression, warmer air is lifted where it meets denser cooler air, expanding and cooling as it rises. Condensation and precipitation can occur if the moisture and cloud processes are suitable. At a warm front, warm air commonly rises over a gentler frontal slope; at a cold front, advancing cooler air can force warmer air upward more steeply. The exact weather depends on the system. The term cyclonic does not mean that all rainfall comes from a tropical cyclone or that every low necessarily has the same fronts.

An original cross-section can show a wedge of cold dense air advancing beneath warmer moist air, forcing it upward along the boundary. Cloud and rain located near the ascent support frontal cyclonic rainfall. A plan-view low symbol by itself supplies less detail: it identifies pressure structure but not every air mass, moisture condition or lifting slope. If the question asks for formation, use the warm–cold interaction and rising/cooling sequence rather than merely naming a depression. A frontal line is not a mountain, so the same sloping shape in a simplified diagram does not make the rainfall orographic.

A three-panel comparison should keep the common and different parts clear. All panels may show rising moist air, cooling, condensation and precipitation. The cause of ascent differs: surface heating in convection, a mountain barrier in relief rainfall and low-pressure convergence or frontal lifting in cyclonic rainfall. Naming the common cooling step alone does not distinguish the types. Conversely, naming the lifting cause without explaining how cloud develops leaves the rainfall sequence incomplete. The best short explanation connects the relevant trigger to ascent and cooling under the stated moisture conditions.

Rainfall observations can support but do not always uniquely identify the mechanism. A short intense shower after strong heating is consistent with convection, but a complete attribution may need radar, satellite or weather-system evidence. Rain on the windward side of a ridge supports an orographic contribution, while a moving band along a front supports frontal organisation. Real events can combine triggers. This connects rainfall types to synoptic charts and enquiry: use the observations to judge a proposed explanation, state what is missing and avoid forcing every event into one exclusive box without evidence.

A rainfall process explanation should distinguish condensation from precipitation reaching the ground. Condensation creates tiny droplets or ice particles within cloud under suitable conditions; further growth and survival during descent are needed for rain or other precipitation to arrive at the surface. A visible cloud alone does not establish a measured rain total. In a dry lower layer, some falling material can evaporate before reaching a gauge. This is another reason to use the station rainfall record alongside cloud imagery rather than treating the two as identical observations.

For a discrimination exercise, look for the lifting cause: a heated surface, a mountain barrier or frontal/convergent motion. The common cooling stage explains cloud formation, while the distinct cause identifies the rainfall mechanism in the supplied model.

## 07 Synoptic charts and weather satellite images

A synoptic chart represents weather conditions or an analysis across a region at a stated time. It can show pressure through isobars, high and low centres, fronts and station information. A satellite image observes features from above at a stated time and wavelength or product type. These resources are complementary, not identical. A pressure contour is not a cloud edge, and a bright satellite area is not automatically the centre of a low. Read the key, valid time, hemisphere and map orientation before combining their evidence.

Isobars join places with equal pressure according to the chart’s stated reference. If successive lines are labelled 1,012, 1,008 and 1,004 hPa, their interval is 4 hPa. Lower values towards the centre identify a low; higher values towards the centre identify a high. The number of lines is not the pressure value. If a station lies between two labelled lines, an estimate needs their spacing and the chart’s conventions rather than an arbitrary exact value. A contour interval can be calculated from adjacent labels, but it must not be assumed identical on every map.

At a comparable scale, closer isobar spacing generally supports a stronger pressure-gradient interpretation. It does not give an exact wind speed unless the resource supplies a suitable conversion or further information. In the Southern Hemisphere, low circulation is generally clockwise and high circulation anticlockwise at the surface, with inward and outward components respectively. A chart copied from the Northern Hemisphere may reverse the rotation. A correct interpretation identifies the hemisphere explicitly rather than applying one memorised swirl direction to every diagram.

Station symbols can encode temperature, cloud amount, wind direction and speed. The exact layout and wind-barb conventions need a key. Wind direction is conventionally where the wind comes from, while barbs indicate speed under the stated system. A partially shaded circle can indicate cloud amount rather than rainfall quantity. Do not read a station’s temperature number as pressure merely because both appear nearby. If the question supplies a simplified symbol, use that declared layout rather than assuming every professional chart uses the same arrangement.

Visible satellite imagery shows reflected sunlight and is affected by illumination. Infrared products can provide information related to emitted radiation and cloud-top temperature under their stated processing. Many weather displays use colour scales that must be read from the key. A bright or coloured area may represent high cold cloud in one product, reflectivity in another or a selected temperature range. Without the product description, its meaning is uncertain. Cloud on an image does not by itself establish rainfall reaching the ground at a particular station.

An original combined resource can place a cloud band near a mapped front and a low-pressure system. The spatial alignment is consistent with frontal cloud, particularly if movement through successive images supports the relationship. It does not prove that every pixel in the band is producing the same rain rate. A station outside the band may still have local cloud or precipitation. Combine the scales and times carefully: a morning chart and an afternoon image may represent a moving system, so a positional difference is not automatically an error.

A short time sequence can reveal movement and development that one image cannot. Compare the same region, use the time labels and avoid mistaking a change in colour scale or projection for physical change. A cloud band shifting eastward can be described as such when the orientation and image registration support it. To infer speed, a known distance and time interval are needed. A picture that looks displaced on the page does not provide a distance in kilometres without scale or geolocation information.

These resources connect measurement to atmospheric processes. A supported response first describes the pressure or cloud pattern with selected evidence, then explains a relevant likely consequence under stated conditions. It does not claim that low pressure equals low temperature or that all cloud guarantees rain. Where the key, time or product information is missing, identify the limit rather than inventing it. The same evidence discipline applies to maps, climate graphs and field data: the form of a resource determines what can be observed, calculated or inferred from it.

Before calculating a chart interval, verify that the labels are on successive isobars rather than separate stations. Values at two stations can differ without establishing the contour interval. Likewise, a labelled central pressure and an outer contour need the intervening lines counted correctly. Record the hPa unit and explain which pair of adjacent labels supports the result.

## 08 Factors influencing climate

Climate reflects interacting controls on energy, moisture and atmospheric circulation. Latitude, altitude, ocean currents, distance from the sea, prevailing winds and pressure systems are useful organising factors. None should be treated as an isolated switch that determines every observation. A coastal desert, for example, combines a marine setting with limited rainfall; proximity to water alone is not sufficient to explain its climate. A sound account identifies the physical pathway through which a factor affects temperature or rainfall and then considers the other relevant conditions.

Latitude affects the angle at which solar radiation reaches the surface and the seasonal pattern of day length. At lower latitudes, the Sun is generally higher in the sky at comparable seasonal positions, so energy is concentrated over a smaller surface area than when rays arrive at a low angle. At higher latitudes, the same incoming beam is spread across more ground and has a longer atmospheric path. These geometric differences help explain broad temperature contrasts. They do not mean every low-latitude place is hotter than every high-latitude place on every day, because altitude, cloud, currents and season also matter.

The Earth’s axial tilt produces seasonal changes in solar angle and daylight. The Southern Hemisphere’s seasons are opposite those of the Northern Hemisphere. A southern location can have its warmest season around December–February while a comparable northern one has a different annual timing. A climate graph must therefore be read with location and month labels, rather than assigning summer to June everywhere. Near the Equator, day length varies less through the year than at higher latitudes, but rainfall patterns can still have strong seasons through circulation and moisture changes.

An original latitude comparison can hold altitude and exposure broadly similar while comparing annual temperature summaries at two latitudes. A warmer low-latitude pattern is consistent with the radiation mechanism, but the comparison still needs information about other controls before claiming latitude alone caused the full difference. A diagram of rays should spread equal energy over different areas coherently. It should not draw a larger total incoming energy beam for one location and then claim the illustration has isolated angle alone. The geometry must match the explanation being tested.

Altitude commonly influences temperature in the troposphere: higher sites are generally cooler than comparable lower sites. Rising air expands in lower surrounding pressure and cools, while the environmental temperature profile also reflects heating, mixing, radiation and atmospheric structure. A temperature inversion can reverse the usual decrease over a layer, so the relation is a broad tendency rather than an absolute rule for every height interval. A mountain summit and a sheltered valley floor may also have different local exposure and night-time conditions.

For a school calculation, use the lapse rate supplied rather than assuming one fixed value for all places and weather. If the stated rate is a 0.6°C decrease per 100 m, an 800 m rise corresponds to a 4.8°C decrease under that model. From a reference temperature of 24°C, the estimated higher-site value is 19.2°C. These are original teaching values. The result is a model estimate, not an observed temperature. If a resource provides actual readings that differ, investigate other controls or the model’s limits rather than altering the measurements to fit the expected number.

Ocean currents redistribute heat and influence the sea surface beside a coast. A warm current can raise the temperature of adjoining marine air relative to a colder sea setting, while a cold current can cool the near-surface air. The effect on rainfall depends on stability, moisture transport, uplift and wider circulation. A cold ocean does not simply remove every cloud. Cooling moist air can support fog or low cloud even where the atmosphere resists the deep ascent needed for substantial rain. Distinguish near-surface saturation from the full vertical development of rain-producing clouds.

The Benguela system off southwestern Africa brings a cold marine influence along Namibia’s coast, with upwelling contributing to cool surface waters. Namibia’s coastal fog illustrates that visible moisture and limited rainfall can coexist. NASA accounts connect fog over the Namib with the cool coastal ocean and surrounding atmospheric conditions. This is a useful mechanism-based example, not a claim that the current alone explains the entire desert or that fog forms at the same intensity every day. Pressure-related subsidence and local weather also influence the vertical structure and persistence of the coastal layer.

A coastal temperature record can therefore be relatively cool despite a low latitude. That does not contradict the latitude mechanism; it shows another factor modifying the result. Similarly, a warm ocean surface does not guarantee rainfall on adjacent land if winds carry air away or a stable layer prevents effective ascent. A supported explanation follows the path from sea temperature to air conditions and then considers movement and uplift. The phrase warm currents bring rain needs those conditions rather than being used as an unrestricted rule.

Distance from the sea influences how quickly local temperatures respond to heating and cooling. Water stores and redistributes heat through its heat capacity, mixing and evaporation, while land surfaces often warm and cool more rapidly. Marine influence commonly moderates coastal temperature ranges compared with similar inland locations, especially where winds carry that influence inland. The effect can be weakened by mountains, offshore flow or other controls. The relevant comparison needs similar latitude and altitude where possible, rather than treating distance as the explanation for any pair of different cities.

An original monthly summary can compare coastal A, with warmest monthly mean 22°C and coolest 16°C, and inland B, with corresponding values 29°C and 11°C. Annual temperature range is 6°C at A and 18°C at B. The contrast is consistent with a stronger moderating marine influence at A under the stated comparable conditions. It does not establish that every coastal day is cooler or that the inland site receives less rainfall. Temperature range, mean temperature and rainfall are different variables whose relationships require their own evidence.

Prevailing wind is the direction from which wind occurs most frequently over a relevant period. Its influence depends on the air’s source, route and modification along the way. Air arriving across an ocean can carry marine moisture and temperature characteristics, while air crossing dry land may have different properties. A mountain barrier can force ascent and precipitation before air descends on its other side. The same compass direction can therefore have different climatic implications in different places. Identify where the air has travelled, not just the letter used to name the wind.

Onshore flow means movement from sea towards land; offshore flow is from land towards sea. A wind called southerly comes from the south, but it is not automatically onshore or offshore without the coastline orientation. An east-coast site and a west-coast site can interpret the same directional label differently. This links climate controls to the wind-vane convention and map reading. A useful resource includes land–sea position, direction arrows and the relevant relief so the learner can follow the actual moisture pathway rather than infer it from a regional stereotype.

Pressure systems influence convergence, divergence and vertical air movement. Persistent or seasonal high-pressure influence can favour subsidence and suppress deep cloud development under suitable conditions. Low-pressure convergence can support ascent and rain if enough moisture and effective cloud processes are present. Seasonal shifts in these systems change where particular airflows and lifting conditions occur. The explanation should not equate a high with high temperature or a low with cold air. Temperature, pressure and moisture interact but retain different meanings and measurements.

Large-scale convergence zones can move seasonally and help organise rainfall patterns in tropical and subtropical regions. Their influence at one location depends on how far the circulation shifts and on the regional geography. A summer-rainfall pattern in part of the southern African interior can be connected to seasonal moisture and ascent, while the coastal setting may retain stronger marine and stability controls. Do not assume that one monthly graph represents every place within Namibia. Local and regional records are needed to specify the pattern at the named site.

A combined supplied resource can make the interaction visible. Site A is low-latitude but coastal beside cold water and under frequent subsiding-air influence. B is at similar latitude but higher altitude and receives moist air forced over relief. A could have cool near-surface conditions and little rain with fog possible; B could be cooler because of height but receive more rain through uplift. The comparison cannot be explained by latitude alone. Its purpose is to connect each supplied control to a distinct pathway, while keeping the conclusions conditional on the resource’s stated evidence.

When judging an explanation of climate, distinguish the observation from the proposed control. A graph can show a small temperature range or a wet summer, but its shape alone may not identify the current, wind route or pressure pattern responsible. A map or circulation resource supplies additional evidence. Use several relevant controls without turning the answer into an unconnected list. The chain should state factor, mechanism and climatic result, and it should recognise conditions such as moisture availability, wind direction or altitude differences that make the link work in the particular comparison.

These factors connect weather observations to climatic regions, vegetation and environmental change. Vegetation responds to water and temperature conditions over time, while land use can modify local surface properties and exposure. A climate explanation should therefore be clear about scale: one shaded school courtyard is a microclimate, whereas an ocean current helps influence a much wider coastal setting. Both are geographical, but they do not provide interchangeable evidence. A supported conclusion uses a matching spatial and time scale and avoids treating one familiar factor as proof of every temperature or rainfall difference.

A useful explanation can combine two controls without claiming that either acts alone. In a coastal-versus-inland comparison at similar latitude and altitude, ocean moderation can help explain the smaller coastal temperature range, while an offshore prevailing wind can temporarily weaken that marine influence. In a higher-versus-lower comparison, altitude may be relevant but the sites can also differ in cloud or slope exposure. State the controlled similarities and remaining differences before attributing the full result to one factor.

## 91 Investigating and recording weather

A weather investigation needs a clear question, suitable instruments and a repeatable observation schedule. State whether the aim concerns changes through time, differences between sites or an instrument/exposure effect. Choose the variables needed and define units, times and locations before collection. Comparable readings reduce avoidable confounding. A temperature comparison using one site at noon and another at dawn mixes site and time effects; the record should not attribute the whole difference to location without accounting for that design.

Use appropriate exposures and the approved instrument procedures. Record maximum/minimum intervals and resets, rain-gauge collection periods, wind averaging times and any missing or compromised values. Keep a traceable table rather than writing only the final mean. Repeated or checked readings can reveal transcription errors, but an unusual observation should remain unless there is evidence that it is invalid. A note about a dry hygrometer wick or a moved screen is more informative than silently replacing a difficult value with an expected one.

Present the result using a graph or statistic suited to the question. Report sample size, selected evidence and limitations. A few days of local readings can support a short comparison, not establish a long-term regional climate trend. Suggest improvements that address actual weaknesses, such as simultaneous readings, consistent exposure or longer coverage, rather than saying only use more data. This links the practical work to weather statistics, instrument design and the broader requirement that a conclusion follow the observations rather than a result chosen in advance.

## 92 Human climate influences and environmental change

Human activities can affect the climate through changes in greenhouse gases, land cover and other influences. Carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases interact with outgoing infrared radiation, contributing to the atmosphere’s energy balance. Adding long-lived greenhouse gases can strengthen warming influence, while land-cover change can affect reflection, moisture exchange and local conditions. A complete account distinguishes the mechanism from a particular observed event. One hot afternoon is not sufficient by itself to attribute a long-term trend to a named activity.

Ozone depletion and the enhanced greenhouse effect are different issues. Stratospheric ozone absorbs much harmful ultraviolet radiation, while greenhouse gases affect the Earth’s energy balance through infrared interactions. A claim that global warming is simply caused by a hole allowing more sunlight through the ozone layer confuses the mechanisms. Environmental management can address emissions, land use and adaptation, but the expected effect depends on the action and scale. A local shade project can change a school microclimate without by itself establishing a measurable global-temperature change.

In a supplied resource, one dataset shows a multi-decade temperature trend and another an isolated day’s maximum. The first is more appropriate for considering longer-term change; the second describes weather. Even a trend needs suitable source quality, comparable methods and broader attribution evidence before assigning a unique cause. This connects environmental change to climate records, instrument consistency and geographical judgement. State the observed scale, explain the relevant process and retain uncertainty rather than turning a familiar environmental term into an explanation for every unusual rainfall or temperature observation.
