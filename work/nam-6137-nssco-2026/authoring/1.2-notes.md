# Weathering and Erosion

## 01 Breakdown in place and removal

Weathering is the breakdown or chemical alteration of rock where it lies. Mechanical weathering breaks it into smaller pieces without changing the minerals. Chemical weathering changes susceptible minerals through reactions. Weathered fragments may remain beside their parent rock. Erosion involves wearing away and removal by an agent such as moving water, waves or wind. The distinction concerns the action and whether material is removed, rather than whether the process occurs quickly or slowly.

Consider two supplied observations from an unnamed outcrop. At A, a block has split into fragments that remain immediately beneath the parent rock. At B, flowing water repeatedly wears the surface and removes fragments downstream. A supports weathering in place; B supplies evidence for erosion by water. Splitting alone does not prove erosion. Equally, weathering and erosion can operate on the same outcrop: weathering loosens material and an agent later removes it. The later removal does not change the earlier breakdown into a different process.

Smaller stones in a photograph do not establish the full sequence that produced them. Look for evidence of an active agent, changed minerals, widened joints, detached pieces or removed material. Distinguish an observation from an inference: a crack is visible, but ice within it may not be. If the resource does not show or describe freezing, a freeze-thaw explanation needs qualification. This connects weathering to the river, wind and coastal processes that transport its products. It also connects to map and photograph interpretation, where descriptions must remain tied to the supplied evidence rather than to an imagined event.

## 02 Physical, chemical and biological weathering

Weathering can be classified by the kind of change involved and by the agents contributing to it. Physical or mechanical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing the chemical identity of its minerals. Chemical weathering changes minerals through reactions, sometimes producing dissolved substances and new minerals. Biological weathering involves organisms. Organisms can produce mechanical effects, such as roots widening joints, and chemical effects, such as acids associated with biological activity. The categories therefore describe overlapping aspects of a real process, rather than mutually exclusive boxes into which an entire landscape must be placed.

A joint is a fracture in rock that can provide a route for water, air or roots. Where water enters joints and conditions permit freezing and thawing, ice-related stresses can gradually widen cracks and loosen fragments. This is physical weathering because the rock can break without its minerals being chemically transformed. A block split by frost does not become a new chemical substance just because its shape changes. To support this explanation from a resource, look for accessible cracks, available water and evidence that temperatures cross the freezing point. Cold conditions without liquid water entering the rock do not establish an effective repeated process.

Temperature changes can also contribute to mechanical stress. An exposed rock surface can heat and cool more rapidly than its interior. Differential expansion and contraction can contribute to cracking, while different minerals may respond differently. Thin outer sheets can become detached in exfoliation. Exfoliation is not restricted to a single cause: unloading and other changes in stress also contribute in some settings. Where a teaching task specifies repeated heating and cooling, describe that thermal sequence. Do not relabel peeling sheets as freeze-thaw unless the resource supplies water and freezing conditions.

Physical weathering increases the number of fragments and can expose a larger total surface area. A large block divided into many smaller pieces has more surface available to water and air, even if its total rock volume remains similar. This can assist chemical weathering by giving reactions more access to minerals. Physical breakdown and chemical change can therefore reinforce one another. The relationship is conditional: dividing a very resistant mineral does not make it react like a more susceptible mineral, and a dry surface may still lack the water needed for a particular reaction.

Chemical weathering includes hydrolysis, carbonation and oxidation. Hydrolysis involves reactions between water and susceptible minerals, for example the alteration of feldspar to clay minerals. Carbonation involves carbon dioxide dissolving in water to make a weak carbonic acid that can dissolve calcium carbonate in limestone. Oxidation involves oxygen reacting with susceptible minerals, such as iron-bearing minerals forming iron oxides. The presence of water can support chemical weathering, but water entering a crack is not itself proof that a chemical reaction has occurred. Evidence of changed mineral products is more discriminating than the observation that a rock is wet.

Chemical alteration can weaken rock and help produce weathered material and soil. In jointed granite, mineral alteration may be strongest where water penetrates and attacks the block from several sides. Corners and edges can be especially exposed, contributing to rounding and the survival of less-weathered cores. In limestone, dissolution along joints can enlarge openings and produce solution features. Do not assume that every rounded stone or hollow has one origin. A rounded fragment might also have been abraded during transport, and a hole might involve another process. The surrounding evidence and material determine which interpretation is justified.

Biological effects include roots entering existing fractures and exerting pressure as they grow. Burrowing animals can loosen material and expose fresh surfaces, while microorganisms and plant-associated chemical activity can alter susceptible minerals. A root should not be imagined drilling cleanly through an unbroken block in the same way as a powered tool. Existing weaknesses often provide access. A root growing within a widening joint supplies stronger evidence for a mechanical biological contribution than a tree merely standing nearby. The link must connect the organism’s action to the change in the rock.

A useful resource contrasts three specimens. Specimen A has smaller fragments with the same mineral composition as the parent rock. B contains clay minerals replacing some original feldspar. C shows a thickening root within an expanding joint. A supports mechanical breakdown; B demonstrates chemical alteration; C supports biological involvement through a mechanical action. C can therefore be described as both biological and physical in the relevant senses. Calling it chemical simply because a plant is present would ignore the stated evidence. Calling B physical because a weakened piece later breaks off would overlook the earlier mineral change.

Weathering products can accumulate below an outcrop as loose angular material or remain within a weathered profile. The material may later be moved by gravity, runoff, rivers or wind. Bare rock, fractured blocks, rounded residual masses and solution features all need a process explanation appropriate to their evidence. A landform records a history that may include more than one process and a long period of change. Its present appearance does not establish that every process is active at the same rate today. Distinguish a description of shape from an explanation of development.

The classification can be tested with a practical comparison. Place equal-sized samples of different rock types under the same stated treatment and compare their responses, while recording the treatment and observation period. A specimen losing material or developing cracks gives a different kind of evidence from a specimen developing new mineral products. Identifying chemical change may require laboratory evidence rather than appearance alone. A fair comparison must hold other relevant conditions steady; otherwise a difference could result from unequal sample size, exposure time or water supply instead of the factor being investigated.

These distinctions link to the study of rivers, wind and coasts, because the size, shape and availability of weathered material influence what agents can remove and transport. They also connect to research skills: a proposed mechanism should be checked against observations that could distinguish it from alternatives. A diagram of a widening water-filled crack is incomplete evidence for hydrolysis unless mineral alteration is also established. A diagram of ice expanding in a joint explains a physical mechanism. The question is what changed, how it changed and which observations support that account.

## 03 Climate and rock controls

Climate influences weathering through temperature, moisture and the pattern of their change. Warm, moist conditions generally favour many chemical reactions because water is available and reactions often proceed faster at higher temperatures. Persistent dryness can restrict reactions requiring water, even where daytime temperatures are high. The statement that hot places have faster weathering therefore needs qualification: it depends on the process, the water available and the rock. A single climatic label cannot establish the dominant mechanism without considering its conditions.

Freeze-thaw weathering needs water in accessible cracks and suitable freezing conditions. Repeated changes around the freezing point can provide more opportunities for cycles than conditions that remain deeply frozen or remain above freezing. A very cold dry site may experience little effective frost weathering because it lacks liquid water entering the cracks. A moist site with temperatures repeatedly moving above and below freezing may offer more favourable conditions. This is a conditional comparison, not a promise that each temperature crossing breaks a measurable piece of rock.

Large changes in the temperature of exposed surfaces can contribute to thermal stresses. Bare rocks may respond differently from shaded or soil-covered rocks. Daily air-temperature range is useful context, but rock-surface temperature can differ from the air reading. The explanation should identify expansion, contraction or differential heating rather than saying that sunshine “melts the rock”. Ordinary daytime surface heating does not melt an outcrop. Where a resource shows detached outer sheets, thermal effects may contribute, but unloading and rock structure should also be considered if the evidence allows them.

Rock characteristics influence access and susceptibility. Mineral composition affects how readily a rock undergoes particular chemical reactions. Limestone contains calcium carbonate that can dissolve in weak carbonic acid; granite contains minerals with different susceptibilities, including feldspar that can alter by hydrolysis. It is misleading to call one rock universally weak and another universally strong without specifying the process. A material resistant to one kind of weathering may be vulnerable under another set of conditions. The relevant comparison is between the actual minerals, structure and environment.

Joints and other openings permit water, oxygen and roots to penetrate. Closely spaced joints can divide rock into smaller blocks and increase the surfaces accessible to weathering. Porosity concerns the proportion of pore space, while permeability concerns how readily fluids pass through connected openings. A rock can have pores that are poorly connected, so high porosity alone does not prove rapid through-flow. Bedding, grain size and the arrangement of minerals can also influence how weathering proceeds. When interpreting a resource, use whichever features are measured rather than supplying a property from the rock’s name alone.

A supplied comparison holds rock type, sample size and exposure time constant. Sample A is kept warm with a regular water supply; sample B is equally warm but dry. If alteration products develop more strongly at A, the evidence is consistent with moisture helping the specified chemical reaction. It does not isolate temperature because temperature was not changed. To test temperature separately, compare otherwise similar moist samples at stated different temperatures. This distinction between a controlled variable and the investigated factor prevents a plausible explanation from becoming an unsupported experimental conclusion.

Now compare two equally exposed blocks of the same rock. One is heavily jointed and the other has few open fractures. Greater alteration along the jointed block’s surfaces would be consistent with increased access of water and air. The observation would not prove that joints changed the rock’s original chemical composition before weathering began. Follow the causal chain: more accessible surfaces or routes allow greater contact with weathering agents, which can increase breakdown or alteration under suitable conditions. Rock controls and climatic controls work together rather than competing as complete explanations.

Climate and rock controls connect this topic to climatic regions and vegetation, river drainage and field research. A weathering investigation needs a stated observation period and a clear measure, such as changes in cracks or identifiable alteration products. Do not infer a long-term rate from one photograph without dates or repeated measurements. Likewise, an association between humid areas and stronger alteration does not rule out differences in rock type or exposure history. A supported conclusion explains the mechanism while recognising what the supplied comparison can and cannot isolate.

A field record can state the number of open joints crossing equal-length lines on comparable rock surfaces. Keeping line length and exposure conditions consistent makes the jointing comparison clearer than describing one surface simply as more cracked.

## 81 Hydrolysis

Hydrolysis is chemical weathering in which water reacts with susceptible minerals and changes them into different substances. Feldspar altering to clay minerals is a useful example. The essential feature is a chemical reaction that changes the mineral, rather than water merely entering a crack. Some products can remain as new solid minerals while other components are carried in solution. The rock may become weaker because the new mineral assemblage has different properties from the original material.

Consider two observations from equal rock specimens. A is wet along a joint but laboratory analysis finds no mineral change. B contains clay replacing some original feldspar after water exposure. B provides stronger evidence of hydrolysis because it establishes alteration of a susceptible mineral. A shows water access, which could allow a reaction, but access is not evidence that the reaction has occurred. Do not identify hydrolysis from the word water alone. A physically split but chemically unchanged fragment also fails to establish the process.

Hydrolysis can contribute to the weathering of granite and the development of soil material. Its effectiveness depends on water, mineral susceptibility and exposure conditions. A simple account can follow the sequence from water contact to mineral reaction and then to altered products and weakening. A complete chemical equation is unnecessary for this distinction. This connects to the broader classification of chemical weathering and to research skills: choose observations that demonstrate the defining change rather than conditions that merely make that change possible. The later removal of altered grains by flowing water is a separate erosional stage.

## 82 Carbonation

Carbonation begins when carbon dioxide dissolves in water, forming a weak carbonic acid. This water can react with calcium carbonate in limestone, producing soluble material that can be carried away in solution. Water entering joints helps the reaction reach the rock interior and can gradually enlarge the openings. This mechanism is chemical weathering because the material is changed through reaction and dissolution, rather than split into chemically unchanged pieces by expanding ice.

A useful sequence diagram shows rainwater acquiring carbon dioxide, the weakly acidic water entering a limestone joint, and dissolved material leaving in the water. Describe each connection: carbon dioxide makes the water weakly acidic; the water reacts with the carbonate mineral; dissolution enlarges the joint over time. Do not put oxygen in place of carbon dioxide or claim that the water must freeze. Carbonation can operate without freezing, provided the relevant water, gas and susceptible material are present. A diagram of a joint becoming wider without those details would leave the mechanism uncertain.

Limestone landscapes may contain solution grooves, enlarged joints, caves and other dissolution features. Formation depends on geological structure, water pathways and time; one shower does not create a large cave. Do not assume that any hole in rock proves carbonation. Identifying the rock, the chemical conditions and the removal of dissolved material makes the explanation stronger. This connects weathering to water movement and drainage. Carbonation describes a chemical process, while rivers and groundwater can transport its dissolved products; the linked sequence should not be reduced to a claim that limestone simply disappears without a mechanism.

## 83 Oxidation

Oxidation occurs when oxygen reacts chemically with susceptible material. In weathering, iron-bearing minerals can form iron oxides, often producing reddish or brownish alteration. Moisture can help the reactions proceed. The change is not simply the rock becoming wet or being covered by an unrelated brown stain: evidence should support a reaction involving the original minerals. Not every rock has the same mineral composition, so its susceptibility varies.

A process sequence can begin with oxygen and water reaching exposed iron-bearing minerals through the surface or cracks. Reaction then produces altered iron compounds. These changes can weaken susceptible material and contribute to further breakdown. Describe the sequence in terms of access, reaction and resulting alteration. A statement that oxygen “pushes the rock apart” would confuse chemical change with a mechanical wedging action. The products and mineral change provide the distinction.

Suppose a supplied resource compares two surfaces of the same specimen: the protected interior retains its original minerals, while the exposed rim contains newly identified iron oxides. The rim’s position supports an access-related pattern; identification of the new minerals supports oxidation. Colour alone is weaker evidence because other materials may also look red or brown. Oxidation connects with hydrolysis and carbonation as a chemical-weathering process, but the reacting substances and mineral products differ. In an explanation, name those distinctions instead of treating chemical weathering as a single unnamed reaction affecting every mineral equally. Subsequent erosion may remove the weakened material without being the chemical reaction itself.

## 84 Freeze-thaw weathering

Freeze-thaw weathering can break rock where water enters accessible cracks and subsequently freezes. Ice-related expansion and pressure can stress the sides of a confined joint. Thawing then permits further water entry, and repeated suitable cycles can enlarge the opening and loosen fragments. The minerals need not change chemically. This is a physical-weathering explanation, with water supply, cracks and freezing conditions as necessary parts of the account.

A sequence diagram should show a water-filled joint, ice within the joint during a freezing stage and a wider joint or loosened fragment after repeated cycles. Arrows for pressure must act against the joint walls rather than lifting an entire uncracked outcrop without explanation. If temperatures are supplied, interpret whether conditions permit freezing and thawing. Air temperatures alone do not measure the exact temperature deep inside the rock, so avoid treating a single brief cold reading as proof that a block has broken. The teaching model represents repeated effective conditions.

A permanently frozen dry surface may offer fewer opportunities for new water entry and cycling than a moist surface experiencing repeated changes around freezing. Therefore, the coldest place is not automatically the place with the fastest freeze-thaw breakdown. This links climatic controls with rock structure and permeability. It also distinguishes freeze-thaw from exfoliation: the former depends on water and freezing in cracks, while a thermal-peeling explanation concerns stresses associated with heating and cooling of the rock. A later movement of detached fragments downslope is separate from their initial loosening through weathering.

## 85 Exfoliation

Exfoliation is the detachment of outer rock sheets or layers, often giving an exposed surface a rounded or peeling appearance. Thermal stresses can contribute where surface heating and cooling create differences in expansion and contraction between outer material and the interior. Repeated stresses may help fractures grow and sheets separate. The account concerns changes in the rock’s stress and shape, rather than chemical conversion of the whole block into a new mineral.

A thermal-exfoliation sequence can show an exposed outer layer warming and expanding, subsequent cooling and contraction, then the growth of surface-parallel cracks and eventual sheet detachment. The diagram should keep the core and outer layer distinct. It should not place ice within a joint unless the intended mechanism is freeze-thaw. The simplified sequence describes a contributing mechanism, not a claim that daily temperature change alone explains every exfoliation surface. Unloading and other stress changes can also contribute in particular landscapes.

USGS research on a monitored granite dome provides evidence that thermal cycling and cumulative surface heating can contribute to exfoliation-related cracking. This supports attention to the mechanism without turning it into a universal single-cause explanation. In a resource task, describe the supplied heating, cooling and peeling evidence and avoid inventing a temperature or rock composition. The process connects weathering to rockfall hazards and to later erosion of loosened material. A rounded outcrop may record a long history of physical and chemical processes; its present shape alone cannot reveal a complete chronological sequence or a precise rate of change.
