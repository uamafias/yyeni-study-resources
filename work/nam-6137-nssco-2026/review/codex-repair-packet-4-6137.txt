BL-6137-2.4.02-01 [6137-2.4-005]
Coal, oil and natural gas remain significant because their stored chemical energy can be transported, held and released when suitable equipment is operated. Their availability depends on deposits, extraction, transport, finance and infrastructure, not simply on their existence underground. Together, coal, oil and natural gas have supplied around four-fifths of the world's energy for decades. Oil dominates transport, while coal and gas generate much of the world's electricity. A country may consume imported fuels without producing them domestically. The source's geological location and the location of its users can therefore differ substantially.

BL-6137-2.4.02-05 [6137-2.4-001]
Oil availability involves global production, shipping, refining, storage, currency and local distribution. Disruption at one stage can affect costs or delivery. International price changes can influence imported fuel costs, but the retail effect also depends on exchange rates and relevant domestic arrangements. A statement that oil becomes more expensive globally does not provide an exact new local price without those additional facts. Namibia has no oil refinery, so all its petrol and diesel are imported, mainly by ship through Walvis Bay.

BL-6137-2.4.02-10 [6137-2.4-005]
Fuelwood provides heat for cooking or other uses where wood can be obtained. It may be locally accessible and usable with relatively simple equipment, but collecting it takes time and sustainable supply depends on recovery. The fuel's cash price may not capture the labour of collection or effects on woodland. In Namibia wood is the most common main cooking fuel: in the 2023 census sample data, 58% of households recorded wood or firewood and 29% electricity. Households may rely on it because other options are unavailable or unaffordable.

BL-6137-2.4.03-02 [6137-2.4-002]
Geothermal energy uses heat from within the Earth. In suitable settings, hot fluids or heated rock can provide usable heat and sometimes electricity generation. For electricity, the system must deliver heat capable of driving the appropriate generating process. Favourable geology, accessible depth, water or fluid arrangements and drilling costs influence feasibility. Iceland, a volcanic island, is a well-known example: in 2003 its government reported that about 88% of homes were heated with geothermal water and that geothermal power stations supplied 18% of its electricity. A hot climate at the surface is not evidence of a geothermal reservoir; solar heating and internal Earth heat are different sources.

BL-6137-2.4.04-02 [6137-2.4-008]
Good HEP sites usually have a large, reliable river flow; a steep drop, waterfall or narrow gorge where a short dam can be built; impermeable rock so water does not seep away; a wide valley upstream to store water; low evaporation; and land of low value with few people who would need to be moved. A run-of-river arrangement needs less storage but depends more directly on flow. The catchment supplies water, so rainfall upstream can matter even where the station itself lies in a dry area. A local rainfall observation alone cannot describe the whole river supply. Transmission routes and distance to users also affect the cost of delivering generated electricity.

BL-6137-2.4.04-03 [6137-2.4-007]
Ruacana on the Kunene River is Namibia's named hydroelectric example. It is a run-of-river station: it has little storage, so its output follows the river's flow. The Kunene rises in Angola, where the rain falls, and it supplies up to 45 per cent of Namibia's electricity. When the river is low in the dry season, Ruacana generates less and Namibia must use other stations or imports.

BL-6137-2.4.05-01 [6137-2.4-006]
Hydroelectric generation converts energy associated with water moving through a height difference into mechanical rotation and then electricity. Water enters an intake and falls through a large pipe called the penstock, gaining speed. The moving water turns the turbine; the turbine's shaft spins the generator, which converts rotation into electrical energy. A transformer raises the voltage and power lines carry the electricity to users.

BL-6137-2.4.06-03 [6137-2.4-003]
Coal generation can provide controllable output when fuel, machinery and network capacity are available. Stockpiles can buffer some delivery variation. However, mining and transport affect land and communities, and ash needs management. Burning coal releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that adds to the enhanced greenhouse effect and global warming, and sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, which form acid rain that damages soils, forests, lakes and fish. Smoke and dust also reduce air quality. Pollution controls can reduce particular emissions without making combustion carbon-free. Continuing fuel and maintenance costs must be included in an affordability judgement alongside initial construction.

BL-6137-2.4.06-05 [6137-2.4-002]
Nuclear disadvantages include high initial capital requirements, long planning or construction periods in many projects, radioactive waste management and decommissioning responsibilities. Severe accidents are rare but serious. On 11 March 2011 an earthquake and tsunami cut the power and cooling at Japan's Fukushima Daiichi station; three reactor cores melted, radioactive material escaped and about 110,000 people were evacuated because of radiation. A comparison should not call nuclear renewable because it has low direct carbon emissions, or call it fossil because its fuel is mined. Replenishment, emissions and risk are separate dimensions.

BL-6137-2.4.06-06 [6137-2.4-004]
Solar photovoltaic generation avoids combustion at the point of generation and can be built at different scales. It needs no fuel, has low running costs and suits sunny places and homes far from the grid. Its disadvantages are a high cost to buy panels and batteries, and small home systems that can run lights and phones but not cooking or large appliances. Its direct output varies with sunlight and is unavailable from new sunlight during night. Storage or another source is needed if the service requires supply beyond that pattern. Manufacturing, land, materials and eventual equipment disposal also belong in a wider environmental assessment.

BL-6137-2.4.06-14 [6137-2.4-004]
Power and energy adequacy need checking separately. A storage device may contain enough energy for several hours but lack the discharge power required to run a large load at once.

BL-6137-2.4.06-15 [6137-2.4-004]
A simple load example uses a two-kilowatt appliance for three hours, requiring six kilowatt-hours. A store able to deliver only four kilowatt-hours cannot supply the full interval without another source, even if it can provide two kilowatts initially.

BL-6137-2.4.06-17 [6137-2.4-004]
Operational flexibility also varies between technologies and designs. Some generators can change output more readily than others, while minimum operating levels, start-up times and maintenance affect practical decisions. A system plan connects resource availability, demand timing and the usable characteristics of each component.

BL-6137-2.4.08-01 [6137-2.4-001]
Energy policy needs to consider environmental effects, resource depletion and household health or safety alongside supply. Under the Environmental Management Act (Act 7 of 2007), listed activities, which include generating and transmitting electricity, may not go ahead without an environmental clearance certificate. The Environmental Commissioner decides each application after an assessment of likely effects and may hold public hearings. The geographical purpose is to identify impacts, alternatives and appropriate management, with attention to affected people and environments. An assessment or clearance record is different from proof that every predicted outcome has occurred.

Namibia's White Paper on Energy Policy (1998) set six goals: effective governance, security of supply, social upliftment, investment and growth, economic competitiveness and efficiency, and sustainability. Security of supply was to come from diversifying sources and using local resources such as sunshine. BL-6137-2.5.01-02 [6137-2.5-001]
Agricultural uses include crop irrigation, livestock drinking and suitable cleaning or processing activities on farms. Crops also use rainwater stored in the soil. Irrigation is deliberate application rather than any water a plant receives. The amount needed depends on crop, weather, soil and management. In Namibia farming is the largest user: in 2008 irrigation took about 40% of national water demand and livestock 26%, compared with about 20% for towns and industry, 6% for tourism, 5% for mining and 3% for rural households.

BL-6137-2.5.02-01 [6137-2.5-001]
Groundwater occupies pores and fractures in rock or sediment below the ground. An aquifer is material capable of storing and transmitting useful quantities of that water. Surface water occurs above ground in rivers, reservoirs, lakes and other visible water bodies. The categories are connected through infiltration, groundwater discharge and river or reservoir interactions. Groundwater is not an underground river in a large cave. Namibia's most productive aquifers are in the Karstveld (karst area), at Stampriet in the east and in Ohangwena in the north; tens of thousands of boreholes and wells tap groundwater across the country.

BL-6137-2.5.02-02 [6137-2.5-001]
Precipitation can infiltrate the soil and, under suitable conditions, move deeper to recharge groundwater. The rate and amount depend on rainfall, soil, geology, vegetation and previous moisture conditions. Not all rain becomes recharge: some evaporates, is used by plants, runs off or remains temporarily in the soil. In Namibia potential evaporation, from about 2,000 mm a year at the coast to over 3,400 mm in the south-east, is far higher than rainfall, so only a small part of the rain recharges aquifers.

BL-6137-2.5.02-04 [6137-2.5-001]
Surface sources include perennial rivers, seasonal channels and impounded reservoirs. A perennial river flows throughout the year under its usual conditions, while an ephemeral channel carries water intermittently. Namibia's five perennial rivers, the Kunene, Okavango, Kwando, Zambezi and Orange, all lie on its borders and are fed by rain in neighbouring countries. Interior rivers are ephemeral: they flow for only hours or days after heavy rain, then sink into their sandy beds. A dry channel can carry a large flow after rain in its catchment; a single dry observation does not establish that it never floods.

BL-6137-2.5.03-02 [6137-2.5-001]
Away from the border rivers, most farms and many towns depend on boreholes, because groundwater is available all year and is not lost to evaporation as dam water is. Along the border, the Kunene alone supplies water to about 35 per cent of Namibia's population. Surface storage may collect episodic inflow for later urban or agricultural use. Both require appropriate extraction, treatment and delivery. A pump failure or damaged pipeline can interrupt access even when the source retains water, so physical scarcity and infrastructure failure should be explained separately.

BL-6137-2.5.04-03 [6137-2.5-003]
Flooding occurs when water inundates land that is normally not submerged. Heavy or prolonged rain can increase channel flow; saturated or low-infiltration surfaces can produce rapid runoff; restricted channels or drainage can worsen local accumulation. The effects depend on the storm, catchment and exposed uses. In March 2009 heavy seasonal rain flooded the Cuvelai basin of northern Namibia; together with floods on the Zambezi and Okavango, about 13,000 people were displaced, 92 died and crops were destroyed.

BL-6137-2.5.04-07 [6137-2.5-003]
Drought concerns a sustained water shortage relative to the setting's conditions or requirements. Rainfall deficits can reduce runoff and recharge, while high evaporative demand can increase losses. Surface stores may decline first or more visibly, and groundwater can respond over different timescales. A dry climate is not the same as a drought. On 22 May 2024 Namibia declared a national state of emergency over its worst drought in about 100 years: dam levels were low, farmers were badly affected and over 331,000 households registered for drought relief.

BL-6137-2.5.05-03 [6137-2.5-002]
Reservoir storage can capture wet-period inflow for later demand. Its usefulness depends on catchment inflow, capacity, losses, quality and operating decisions. Sediment can reduce useful space, evaporation removes water and drought can limit refilling. Namibia's major dams include Hardap, Naute, Neckartal, Von Bach, Swakoppoort and Omatako. Benefits of a dam include a stored supply for dry periods, water for irrigation, flood control, fishing and recreation, and sometimes hydroelectricity. Problems include flooding farmland and homes so that people must move, heavy evaporation losses, less water and silt reaching people downstream, sediment filling the reservoir, and the spread of bilharzia, carried by freshwater snails in still water.

BL-6137-2.5.05-07 [6137-2.5-001]
Coastal desalination can add supply where a saline source and suitable infrastructure are available. Since 2010 a reverse-osmosis plant near Wlotzkasbaken, north of Swakopmund, has supplied water that the national water utility delivers to Swakopmund, nearby uranium mines and other parts of the Erongo Region. A coast's length alone does not establish a feasible national supply to every settlement. Assess intended volume, location, delivery and environmental effects rather than assuming the ocean removes all scarcity.

Visitors from abroad bring in foreign currency, and the lodges and operators they use pay wages and taxes. Their sustainability depends on appropriate ecological information, limits and compliance. BL-6137-2.6.03-01 [6137-2.6-003]
Wild animals influence vegetation, food webs and nutrient movement. Grazing and browsing remove plant material, while predators interact with prey populations. Many effects are positive. Dung returns nutrients to the soil and improves its fertility. Elephants and other animals swallow seeds and drop them far away in their dung, spreading plants to new places. Grazing and browsing remove dry plant material that would otherwise fuel fires, so fires tend to be less frequent and less intense. Predators keep herbivore numbers in check, which helps prevent overgrazing. Effects differ between animals and settings. A single claim that wildlife destroys vegetation or that wildlife has no environmental cost would overlook these varied ecological roles.

BL-6137-2.6.05-01 [6137-2.6-007]
Wildlife management can conflict with agriculture, settlement, infrastructure and resource use where objectives overlap. Elephants can trample crops and damage water pipes and fences; lions, leopards, cheetahs and hyenas can kill cattle and goats; livestock and wildlife compete for the same grazing and water in the dry season; and large animals can injure or kill people, while fences and land conversion can restrict wildlife movement or habitat. A land-use conflict therefore has more than one perspective. People bearing direct costs may not receive the same benefits as a tourism operator or a wider conservation programme.

BL-6137-2.6.06-02 [6137-2.6-001]
Government establishes and implements the relevant conservation framework, manages public responsibilities and supports enforcement or monitoring. Rules can protect species, regulate use and guide management. Their effectiveness depends on suitable ecological information, practical capacity and compliance. A rule on paper does not demonstrate that illegal removal has fallen or that habitat condition improved. Patrol, incident and resource records help assess outcomes while recognising reporting limitations. In Namibia the government passed the Nature Conservation Amendment Act of 1996, which let communal residents gain rights over wildlife and tourism by registering conservancies. It runs national parks such as Etosha, works with conservancies and NGOs on annual game counts and issues hunting and use quotas based on them, and enforces the law against poaching. The results are largely successful but not complete: elephant numbers rose from about 7,600 in 1995 to about 23,600 in 2016, yet since 2012 counts in surveyed areas have fallen because of drought, animal movement and suspected poaching.

BL-6137-2.6.06-07 [6137-2.6-001]
Private enterprises can provide capital, operating skills, employment and payments through tourism or other appropriately regulated wildlife activities. Demand can give an economic reason to maintain habitat or wildlife. The incentive depends on actual returns, obligations and control. An enterprise can also create disturbance, waste or unequal benefits if poorly managed. Profitability and sustainability therefore need separate evidence rather than one being assumed from the other. In 2022 conservancies held 67 joint-venture tourism agreements with private lodge operators and 44 conservation hunting concessions; operators pay fees or a share of income to the conservancy, and hunting income helps pay for anti-poaching work and game guards.

BL-6137-2.6.06-11 [6137-2.6-001]
Communities bring local knowledge, rights, labour and direct experience of wildlife benefits and costs. Communal conservancies provide a Namibian framework for member participation and management. Ministry information describes elected committees and reporting responsibilities. These arrangements create opportunities for accountability, while their actual operation must be assessed through participation, understandable information and delivery. A committee's existence does not establish every member's agreement. Conservancy members also protect wildlife directly: in 2022 conservancies employed 733 community game guards, who monitor animals and report poaching, and members take part in annual game counts and decide how income and game meat are shared.

BL-6137-2.6.06-15 [6137-2.6-006]
Benefits should be measured at the level claimed. Revenue to a conservancy, wages to workers, payments to members and wildlife condition are separate outcomes. The June 2017 evaluation of Namibia’s Conservancy Support Activity, discussed in topic 2.3 Leisure and tourism, illustrates why enterprise or employment gains cannot automatically establish equal household income gains. A stakeholder evaluation should connect each role to the relevant result and retain the limits of the evidence.
