BL-6137-1.4.04-03 [6137-1.4-002]
A star dune has several arms or ridges radiating from a central high area, producing a multi-armed plan form. Star dunes form where winds blow from several directions during the year. Each wind pushes sand back towards the centre, so the dune grows upward instead of moving across the desert, and each arm develops its own slip face. In the Namib Sand Sea, star dunes occur on the eastern side around Sossusvlei, where some are more than 300 m high. It is not identified merely by being high, red or located in a desert. A plan showing three or more arms meeting around a central peak provides more discriminating evidence than colour. The number and length of arms can vary, and their exact angles need not form a perfect geometric star.

BL-6137-1.4.04-06 [6137-1.4-001]
Seif (linear) dunes are found in the dune belt near Swakopmund, and linear dunes dominate inland across much of the Namib Sand Sea. They form where strong winds blow from the south for much of the year and winds from a second direction blow at other times. Each wind piles sand onto one flank, so the ridge does not move forward as a crescent but grows in length along the average (resultant) direction of the two winds, producing long, parallel ridges. The sand comes mainly from the Orange River: it is carried into the Atlantic Ocean, moved north along the coast by waves and currents, and blown inland by southerly winds. Sparse vegetation and dry, loose sand let the wind move it freely.

BL-6137-1.5.04-02 [6137-1.5-001]
Constructive waves are low-energy waves. They are low, with a long wavelength, and break infrequently, so the swash of one wave has time to soak away before the next arrives. They spill gently onto the beach, giving a strong swash and a weak backwash, so sand and pebbles are carried up the beach and left there. Destructive waves are high-energy waves, often driven by strong winds during storms. They are high and steep, with a short wavelength, and break frequently. They plunge down onto the beach, so the swash is weak and the backwash strong: material is dragged down the beach and may be stored offshore in a bar. Beach slope, sediment size and the sequence of events also affect the result.

BL-6137-1.5.04-06 [6137-1.5-001]
A comparison should pair the wave types explicitly: constructive conditions have relatively stronger swash and a tendency towards local accumulation, whereas destructive conditions have relatively stronger backwash and a tendency towards removal from the beach. Saying only that destructive waves are stronger is not enough: state the energy, the wave form and the swash/backwash balance. Likewise, a response that names deposition without locating it can confuse upper-beach accumulation with offshore bar formation. The physical location and observation period belong in the explanation.

BL-6137-1.5.06-07 [6137-1.5-004]
Headlands are projections of land into the sea, while bays are coastal indentations between or beside them. In a simple differential-erosion model, less resistant rock is removed more rapidly, creating bays, while more resistant rock remains as headlands. The model is clearest on a discordant coastline, where bands of more and less resistant rock run at right angles to the coast and hydraulic action and abrasion wear back the less resistant bands faster. The actual pattern also depends on structure and exposure. A bay may have a depositional beach within it, so its existence does not imply that every part is undergoing rapid erosion at the time observed.

BL-6137-1.5.07-01 [6137-1.5-002]
Longshore drift is the net transport of beach material along the coast through oblique swash and returning backwash. Waves are driven by the wind, so they usually approach the shore at the angle of the prevailing wind, and the swash runs diagonally up the beach. Gravity then returns backwash more directly downslope. Repeated movements give sediment a zigzag path with a net alongshore component. The direction depends on wave approach and the orientation of the shore; it is not invariably north, south or to the right of a page. Net drift is therefore usually in the general direction of the prevailing wind along that coast. A labelled diagram shows the prevailing wind, the oblique swash, the backwash running straight down the beach and an arrow for the direction of drift. Longshore currents can also transport sediment within the nearshore system.

BL-6137-1.5.09-02 [6137-1.5-003]
A beach is an accumulation of loose material such as sand, shingle or pebbles along the shore. Waves and currents sort and redistribute the sediment, producing a changing profile. Material is often sorted by size across the beach. Strong swash, especially from storm waves, throws large pebbles to the back of the beach, while the weaker backwash can carry only smaller material back down. Material near the water is moved most often, so attrition makes it smaller and rounder. Large pebbles are therefore often found at the top of the beach and finer material nearer the sea. Beaches can include different zones and seasonal forms. Their material may come from rivers, cliff erosion, offshore deposits or transport from elsewhere along the coast. A beach should not be defined as sand alone or assumed to be permanently the same width. Tidal level and the reference line used for measurement affect the visible exposed width on a particular date.

BL-6137-1.6.02-01 [6137-1.6-002]
A Stevenson screen is an instrument shelter designed to help measure representative air temperature and humidity while reducing direct effects of sunshine, precipitation and nearby heated surfaces. The familiar school model is a raised, white, louvred enclosure. Its slatted sides allow air circulation while shielding instruments. The white exterior reflects much incoming solar radiation, reducing heating of the shelter relative to a darker surface under similar conditions. A roof provides protection from direct radiation and precipitation; designs can include a double roof to reduce heat transfer. The screen is traditionally made of wood, a poor conductor of heat, so the box itself passes little of the sun’s heat to the instruments; modern shelters may use other insulating materials.

BL-6137-1.6.02-03 [6137-1.6-002]
The screen is normally placed above a representative natural surface, often short grass, and stands on legs so that the thermometers are about 1.2 m above the ground in the school model; national weather services use a fixed height between 1.25 m and 2 m. Raising the instruments reduces the direct influence of surface heating while keeping the reading relevant to near-surface air. A screen on a hot paved courtyard can measure a different local environment from one over grass. That difference may be a legitimate object of study, but it should not be hidden within an attempt to compare standard observations.

BL-6137-1.6.03-04 [6137-1.6-001]
A maximum thermometer records the highest temperature reached since its last reset, and a minimum thermometer the lowest. A Six’s thermometer combines both in one U-shaped glass tube. Alcohol in the tube expands and contracts as the temperature changes and pushes a thread of mercury round the bend. The mercury pushes a small steel index along each arm, and each index stays at the furthest point it was pushed to. The maximum is read on the right-hand arm, whose scale reads upwards; the minimum on the left-hand arm, whose scale reads downwards. Read the end of each index nearest the mercury, with your eye level with it. The top of the mercury on either scale gives the current temperature. After recording, reset the thermometer by drawing each index back down onto the mercury with a magnet, or by pressing the release button on newer models. A missed reset changes the period that the next reading covers, so note it in the record.

BL-6137-1.6.03-11 [6137-1.6-005]
A wind vane indicates wind direction. The wind pushes the broad tail away, so the arrow head points into the wind, towards the direction the wind is blowing from; it is read against fixed N, E, S and W arms. A northerly wind comes from the north and moves generally towards the south. A diagram must show the cardinal directions or orientation and enough information to distinguish the vane’s pointing end. Do not assume that an arrow on a decorative drawing has the same meaning as a meteorological wind arrow or barb. Read the key and the stated convention before converting the picture into a direction.

BL-6137-1.6.08-05 [6137-1.6-003]
Altitude commonly influences temperature in the troposphere: higher sites are generally cooler than comparable lower sites. The main reason is that the lower atmosphere is heated mainly from below: sunlight warms the ground, and the ground then heats the air above it, so air farther above the land surface is cooler. Air at height is also thinner (less dense) and holds less heat. On average, temperature falls by about 6.5°C for each 1,000 m of height. A temperature inversion can reverse the usual decrease over a layer, so the relation is a broad tendency rather than an absolute rule for every height interval. A mountain summit and a sheltered valley floor may also have different local exposure and night-time conditions.

BL-6137-1.6.08-08 [6137-1.6-004]
The cold Benguela Current flows northward along Namibia’s coast, from higher latitudes towards the Equator, and upwelling brings cold water to the surface near the shore. Air over this cold water is cooled from below, so it becomes stable: it does not rise to form rain clouds, but its moisture often condenses into fog. This helps explain why the Namib coast is foggy yet very dry; average rainfall within 40 km of the coast is below 20 mm a year. The current does not explain the whole desert on its own, because sinking air in the subtropical high also keeps the air dry.

BL-6137-1.6.08-15 [6137-1.6-004]
Air heated near the Equator rises, cools and loses its moisture as rain, then spreads towards the poles and sinks at about 30° north and south. The sinking air warms and dries, forming belts of subtropical high pressure, so many hot deserts, including the Namib, lie between about 15° and 30° from the Equator. The zone where air converges and rises near the Equator, the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), shifts south during the southern summer. It brings most of Namibia’s rain between about September and April, and the north-east receives on average more than 650 mm a year, far more than the coast. One monthly graph therefore does not represent every place in Namibia.

BL-6137-1.7.01-04 [6137-1.7-001]
Tropical deserts commonly occur in or near subtropical dry belts, often around 20–30 degrees north and south, Many lie on the western sides of continents beside cold ocean currents, such as the Namib beside the Benguela Current and the coastal desert of northern Chile, while others stretch far into continental interiors. Examples include the Sahara and Arabian deserts in the north and the Namib and parts of Australia in the south. The word tropical here concerns the broad climatic setting; it does not mean every desert lies wholly between the two tropics. Aridity depends on limited water availability relative to demand, not simply on the presence of sand dunes. Many desert surfaces are rocky, gravelly or otherwise not dune-covered.

BL-6137-1.7.02-05 [6137-1.7-002]
Savannah commonly has grasses with scattered trees and shrubs, with vegetation responding to a wet season and a dry season. Grasses can grow rapidly when water becomes available and may become dry or dormant during water shortage. Some trees shed leaves in the dry season, reducing transpiring surface. Deep or extensive roots can access water in suitable soils, while small leaves or protective features can reduce water loss or browsing. Two southern African trees show different strategies: the baobab stores water in its thick trunk and drops its leaves in the dry winter, while the camel thorn sends a very deep taproot down towards groundwater and carries paired thorns that deter browsing animals.

BL-6137-1.7.02-09 [6137-1.7-002]
The Namib is a useful named setting because fog and highly variable rainfall create different water opportunities across its coastal and inland zones. Adaptations should therefore be matched to the species or resource evidence rather than described as if every plant relies only on deep groundwater or only on fog. East African savannah provides the contrasting wet–dry seasonal setting, while the Amazon provides a moist layered-forest context. The named example supports a feature–condition link; it is not a substitute for identifying the actual leaf, root, growth form or seasonality being explained. The welwitschia is the best-known Namib plant. It grows only in the Namib of Namibia and southern Angola, has just two long leaves that keep growing from its crown throughout its life, and tends to occur where water lies below the surface of dry river courses.

BL-6137-1.7.03-02 [6137-1.7-001]
Equatorial rainforest climates are commonly warm throughout the year with a relatively small annual range of monthly mean temperature. Many have abundant rainfall spread through much of the year, though the exact seasonal pattern varies. Because the midday sun is always high, monthly means usually change by only about 2°C through the year. The rain is convectional: strong heating of the ground makes warm, moist air rise in the low-pressure belt called the ITCZ, so surface winds are often light. The rising air cools, its water vapour condenses into towering cumulonimbus clouds and heavy showers or thunderstorms follow, often in the early afternoon. Evaporation from wet ground and transpiration from trees keep humidity high. High humidity and cloud are common under suitable conditions, while incoming solar energy remains substantial at low latitudes. Totals still differ between months, places and years.

BL-6137-1.7.03-04 [6137-1.7-001]
Savannah climates commonly have a marked wet season and dry season while remaining warm overall. The contrast follows the overhead sun. In the high-sun (summer) season the ITCZ moves over the region: converging, heated air rises, cools and gives convectional rain, with more cloud and higher humidity. In the low-sun (winter) season the ITCZ moves away and the subtropical high-pressure belt takes its place. Its sinking air stops clouds forming, so skies are clear, humidity is low and rain is rare or absent. Monthly means range from about 18°C to above 25°C. Temperature also varies, but rainfall seasonality is especially important for vegetation and livelihoods. In the Southern Hemisphere, a summer-wet example may peak around the early or late calendar months; northern examples can have different month timing.

BL-6137-1.7.03-06 [6137-1.7-001]
Tropical desert climates are strongly water-limited, commonly with low and irregular rainfall. The main cause is the subtropical high-pressure belt at about 20–30° north and south. Air that rose near the Equator sinks here; sinking air is compressed and warms, so its relative humidity falls and its water vapour cannot condense. Deserts on the sheltered side of mountain ranges also lie in a rain shadow. Clear skies and low humidity often contribute to strong daytime heating and night-time cooling inland, producing a large diurnal range under suitable conditions. This is distinct from annual range, which compares seasonal temperature summaries. Cloud, winds, surface and coastal influence can modify the pattern. Not every tropical desert is extremely hot every night, nor does desert mean literally no precipitation. Short heavy events and long dry intervals can coexist within a dry climate.

BL-6137-1.7.03-07 [6137-1.7-001]
The Namib coast is a useful qualification to an overly simple hot-desert model. Along this coast the cold Benguela Current cools the sea air from below. The cool, moist layer is trapped beneath warmer sinking air of the subtropical high (a temperature inversion) within a few hundred metres of the ground, so it cannot rise high enough to form rain clouds. Its moisture condenses as fog instead, which is common in the evenings and mornings, while rainfall stays very low. Fog moisture and sparse vegetation are compatible with aridity; they do not make the coast an equatorial forest environment. Inland desert sites can have different temperature ranges and moisture patterns. A comparison between a coastal Namib resource and an inland tropical-desert resource should therefore identify current, sea influence and pressure controls rather than claim that one is incorrectly labelled because its temperatures differ.

BL-6137-1.7.05-03 [6137-1.7-003]
Removing vegetation can reduce habitat extent and connectivity, affecting species that depend on the forest structure. Exposed soils may experience increased erosion where rain or runoff acts on an unprotected surface. Without a canopy less rain is intercepted, so water reaches the ground quickly: runoff and flooding increase, soil is washed into rivers and nutrients are leached, and cleared tropical soils can become unable to support crops within a few years. Trees return much rainfall to the air by transpiration, so large clearances make the local climate less humid, drier and hotter. Burning the cleared wood releases its stored carbon as carbon dioxide, adding to global warming. The magnitude depends on what is removed and what replaces it. A newly cleared field does not automatically have the same water and soil behaviour as a mature forest, but the account should identify the mechanism rather than claim that every soil immediately becomes useless.

BL-6137-1.7.06-02 [6137-1.7-005]
Agroforestry combines trees with crops and/or livestock in a managed land-use system. Suitable trees can supply fuelwood, fodder and timber, shelter crops and livestock from wind, and reduce soil erosion, while nitrogen-fixing trees can raise soil fertility, all while farming continues. Because trees on the farm provide fuelwood and fodder, households need to cut less natural forest. The system is more complex than a single crop and needs effort, time, skill and secure land rights. Species choice, spacing and management affect whether trees support or compete with the other uses. A dense planting that shades a light-demanding crop too strongly or consumes scarce water may undermine the intended benefit. The mechanism should be stated: for example, a managed shelterbelt can reduce wind exposure, while useful tree products can diversify supply. Benefits are conditional on the design rather than automatic consequences of any mixed planting.
