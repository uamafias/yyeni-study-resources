BL-6137-1.1.03-02 [6137-1.1-001]
Convection currents move the plates. Where a current rises beneath the lithosphere, it spreads out sideways in two directions. The sideways-moving mantle drags the plate above it, like a slow conveyor belt. Plates therefore move apart above a rising current, as at a mid-ocean ridge, where magma rises into the gap. Where two currents meet and sink, the plates above them are dragged towards each other, as at a destructive margin. Scientists now think that two further forces also help: the cold, dense oceanic plate sinking at a subduction zone pulls the rest of its plate (slab pull), and the raised ridge pushes plates away from it (ridge push).

BL-6137-1.2.02-09 [6137-1.2-001]
Weathering produces several landforms. Scree (talus) is a sloping heap of angular rock fragments at the foot of a steep cliff. Freeze-thaw in the joints of the cliff loosens the fragments, which then fall and roll downslope under gravity. An exfoliation dome is a rounded hill of bare rock, often granite, from which curved outer sheets peel away like the layers of an onion; the loose sheets and broken pieces collect at its foot. A tor is a pile of jointed, rounded blocks on a hilltop, left where weathering attacked the rock most strongly along its joints and the loosened material was later removed. In limestone, carbonation widens joints and bedding planes into grooves and, over a very long time, into caves. A landform can record more than one process, so describe its shape first and then explain how it formed.

BL-6137-1.2.03-04 [6137-1.2-002]
Rock characteristics influence access and susceptibility. Mineral composition affects how readily a rock undergoes particular chemical reactions. Limestone contains calcium carbonate that can dissolve in weak carbonic acid; granite contains minerals with different susceptibilities, including feldspar that can alter by hydrolysis. Resistance matters too: under the same conditions, less resistant rocks generally break down faster than more resistant ones, although a rock that resists one process may be vulnerable to another. Colour and grain size affect heating. Dark rocks absorb more of the sun’s heat and reach higher surface temperatures. In coarse-grained rocks such as granite, neighbouring crystals of different minerals heat and expand by different amounts, which can loosen individual grains (granular disintegration). The relevant comparison is between the actual minerals, structure and environment.

Exfoliation is the detachment of outer rock sheets or layers, often giving an exposed surface a rounded or peeling appearance. Thermal stresses can contribute where surface heating and cooling create differences in expansion and contraction between outer material and the interior. BL-6137-1.3.06-02 [6137-1.3-001]
Velocity is speed of flow, commonly expressed in metres per second. Discharge is volume passing a cross-section per unit time, commonly cubic metres per second. The two are related through cross-sectional area but are not identical. A channel can become wider and deeper as discharge increases, so a change in volume does not determine velocity without considering channel geometry. Likewise, a narrow constriction may have rapid flow even where the river’s overall gradient is gentle. Volume still matters for deposition. When the volume of water falls, the river has less energy and can no longer carry all its load, so it deposits some of it. Volume falls in the dry season and after a flood, when water evaporates in hot, dry areas, when it seeps into a sandy or permeable bed, and when people abstract it. Many Namibian rivers are ephemeral and lose water as they flow. During the floods studied on the Kuiseb River in the Namib, the water shrank downstream and all the transported material was deposited in the lower river; no water or sediment reached the Atlantic Ocean.

BL-6137-1.3.06-06 [6137-1.3-001]
Bedrock and channel form influence roughness, gradient, obstacles and available storage spaces. A boulder or rock step can create local zones with different velocities, allowing sediment to accumulate in sheltered areas while adjacent faster flow continues to erode or transport. Where a river flows over permeable or deeply cracked bedrock, or over thick sand, water seeps into the ground, so the volume falls and load is deposited. Where a bar of resistant rock or a large boulder crosses the channel, water slows in the sheltered area behind it and sediment settles there.

BL-6137-1.3.07-10 [6137-1.3-002]
An inland delta is a branching depositional system that spreads within an interior basin rather than building directly into the sea. Flow paths, evaporation, infiltration and seasonal inundation can be important. Some inland systems terminate in pans or wetlands instead of maintaining one continuous channel to an ocean. The broad term should not erase local distinctions between distributary networks, shallow seasonal channels and terminal depressions. The best-known example in southern Africa is the Okavango Delta in northern Botswana. The Okavango River rises in the highlands of Angola and crosses Namibia before spreading into many channels across the flat Kalahari basin. It has no outlet to the sea: its water is lost by evaporation, transpiration by plants and seepage into the sand, and its sediment is deposited across the swamps. Its yearly flood depends on rain that falls far away in Angola.
