A teaching plan can use identical house symbols in three panels with the same orientation and scale. Panel A places eight houses apart across farm plots; B places eight along one east–west route; C groups eight around a crossroads. Keeping the count equal shows that the difference is arrangement rather than total houses. The panel labels should not announce the pattern names if the learner is asked to identify them.

BL-6137-2.6.04-09
A teaching habitat diagram can compare connected vegetation in an earlier schematic with separate patches after a clearing corridor. It can show a lost route and remaining habitat, but not a measured real-world population decline unless count data are supplied. A separate warming panel can show a changed temperature record and a species' stated tolerance. Those are examples of different evidence, not licence to invent numbers for Etosha or a named conservancy.

BL-6137-3.1.09-07
Suppose region A has 20,000 residents in 1,000 square kilometres and B has 30,000 in 3,000 square kilometres. Densities are twenty and ten people per square kilometre. B has more people but a lower average density. If both contain towns, the average still does not identify each town's concentration. A density answer should report both the arithmetic and the distribution limit.

BL-6137-2.5.04-09
A teaching water-balance diagram can show opening reservoir stock of 100 units, inflow twenty, abstraction thirty and evaporation ten over the same interval, with other changes absent. Closing stock is eighty. The inflow arrow enters the store, while abstraction and evaporation leave it. The diagram supports a twenty-unit decline. It does not by itself prove a meteorological drought, because no rainfall baseline or longer record is supplied.
