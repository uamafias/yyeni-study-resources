BL-6137-1.1.04-03
Subduction can also be associated with volcanoes on the overriding plate. Water released from the descending slab helps surrounding mantle rock melt by lowering its melting temperature. Some of this magma rises and can feed volcanoes. A simple explanation that the whole slab immediately melts through friction is misleading. Rock can remain solid to considerable depths, and the location of volcanoes is usually displaced from the trench onto the overriding side. This gives a useful interpretation test: a diagram with a trench, an inclined slab and volcanoes should show a consistent spatial arrangement.

BL-6137-1.1.04-06
A constructive margin can be recognised from arrows pointing away from a central zone, newly formed crust near that zone and older crust farther from it.

![Annotated cross-section of a constructive margin](../../assets/images/ITEM-6137-1.1-013-INTERP-answer.png)

If a resource supplies ages on both sides of a ridge, a broadly symmetrical increase away from the centre supports spreading. Perfect symmetry is not required in every real setting, but the given pattern must be described accurately. Do not claim that new crust is being produced at a location where the resource shows only a sideways offset. Identify the movement that the evidence actually supports.

BL-6137-1.1.04-07
At a conservative margin, plates slide horizontally past one another. The margin is also called a transform margin. Lithosphere is neither systematically created nor consumed by this sideways interaction. Friction can hold parts of the margin temporarily, while continuing plate motion builds stress. Sudden slip releases that stress as an earthquake. Volcanoes are not a characteristic result of the transform movement itself because that movement does not provide the same melting mechanism as divergence or subduction. A volcano elsewhere in the region would require its own explanation rather than being attributed automatically to the nearby transform fault.

Relative size can help explain the pattern but should not replace the evidence of movement.

BL-6137-1.3.04-02
A waterfall is an abrupt drop in the river’s bed over which water falls. A common school model has a more resistant rock layer overlying less resistant material. Water crosses the resistant cap and falls onto the weaker material below. Hydraulic action and abrasion by the load can excavate a plunge pool at the foot. More rapid removal of the less resistant rock can undercut the cap, leaving an overhang. The overhang can collapse when it is no longer adequately supported. Repetition moves the waterfall upstream and can leave a steep-sided gorge downstream of the retreating fall.

![Waterfall, plunge pool, undercut cap and gorge in section, plan and cross-section](../../assets/images/ITEM-6137-1.3-018-INTERP-answer.png)

Freeze-thaw weathering can break rock where water enters accessible cracks and subsequently freezes. Ice-related expansion and pressure can stress the sides of a confined joint. Thawing then permits further water entry, and repeated suitable cycles can enlarge the opening and loosen fragments. The minerals need not change chemically. This is a physical-weathering explanation, with water supply, cracks and freezing conditions as necessary parts of the account.
