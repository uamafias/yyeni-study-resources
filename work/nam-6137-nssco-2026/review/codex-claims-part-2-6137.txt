CL-6137-1.7.02-R1
Named southern African examples: the welwitschia, found only in the Namib of Namibia and southern Angola, has just two leaves that keep growing from its crown and tends to occur near ephemeral water sources; the baobab stores water in its trunk and drops its leaves in the dry winter; the camel thorn has paired spines and a very deep taproot that can reach groundwater.

CL-6137-1.7.05-01-R1
After tropical forest clearance, flooding and erosion rates rise and soils can become unable to support crops within a few years; loss of transpiring trees makes the local climate drier and hotter, and burning cleared wood returns stored carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.
NASA Earth Observatory, Tropical Deforestation: https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/tropical-deforestation, accessed 2026-10-30

CL-6137-1.7.06-01-R1
Agroforestry can improve soil fertility (especially with nitrogen-fixing trees), shelter crops and livestock from wind, reduce soil erosion and supply fuelwood, fodder and timber, but it is more complex than a single crop and needs effort, time, skill and secure land rights.
FAO Sustainable Forest Management Toolbox, Agroforestry basic knowledge: https://www.fao.org/sustainable-forest-management/toolbox/modules/agroforestry/basic-knowledge/en/, accessed 2026-10-30

CL-6137-2.1.03-R1
The cold Benguela Current flows northwards along Namibia, driven by the south-east trade winds; near the coast these winds move surface water offshore so that cold, nutrient-rich water rises and sustains a highly productive ecosystem and good fishing grounds.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benguela_Current and NOAA Ocean Service https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/upwelling.html, accessed 2026-10-30

CL-6137-2.1.03-04-R1
Namibia has a coastline of about 1,500 km between the Kunene and Orange rivers and, since independence in 1990, a 200-nautical-mile Exclusive Economic Zone that is among the most productive fishing grounds in the world; most landings are at Walvis Bay, in the middle of the fishing grounds, with Lüderitz the other main port.
FAO Fishery Country Profile Namibia (FID/CP/NAM, March 2007): https://www.fao.org/fishery/docs/DOCUMENT/fcp/en/FI_CP_NA.pdf, accessed 2026-10-30

CL-6137-2.1.05-R1
Namibian fisheries management (as described in 2007) combines the 200-nautical-mile EEZ, fishing rights limiting entry, vessel licences, annual TACs set from scientific advice and shared as quotas, closed seasons, minimum mesh sizes, an observer on every commercial vessel, sea, air and shore patrols, and inspection of landings at Walvis Bay and Lüderitz.
FAO Fishery Country Profile Namibia (FID/CP/NAM, March 2007): https://www.fao.org/fishery/docs/DOCUMENT/fcp/en/FI_CP_NA.pdf, accessed 2026-10-30

CL-6137-2.1.05-04-R1
Before independence in 1990 foreign distant-water fleets overexploited Namibian fishing grounds under poor or absent management; too many vessels (overcapacity) leads to overfishing; pilchard stocks were seriously reduced by the 1993-1995 Benguela Niño and earlier overfishing, and the pilchard quota was set at zero for 2002.
FAO Fishery Country Profile Namibia (FID/CP/NAM, March 2007): https://www.fao.org/fishery/docs/DOCUMENT/fcp/en/FI_CP_NA.pdf, accessed 2026-10-30

CL-6137-2.1.05-02-R1
Off Namibia, bacteria decomposing dead plankton on the seabed release hydrogen sulphide, which can erupt to the surface, poisoning fish and removing oxygen (for example around Walvis Bay in July 2007); about once a decade a Benguela Niño brings warm, nutrient-poor water into the northern Benguela.
NASA Earth Observatory, Hydrogen Sulfide Eruption off Namibia: https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/NaturalHazards/view.php?id=18791; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benguela_Current; accessed 2026-10-30

CL-6137-2.1.10-R1
Namibian fisheries policy aims to rebuild stocks through conservative TACs, to develop a national catching and processing industry, and to Namibianise the industry by favouring Namibian-owned right holders; onshore processing is promoted (in 2007 60% of the hake TAC was a wet-fish quota landed for processing on shore and about 85% of landed fish was processed in Namibia before export), and quota fees earn government revenue.
FAO Fishery Country Profile Namibia (FID/CP/NAM, March 2007): https://www.fao.org/fishery/docs/DOCUMENT/fcp/en/FI_CP_NA.pdf, accessed 2026-10-30

CL-6137-2.2.05-R1
In the colonial period the centre and south became freehold commercial farmland, mostly owned by white settlers, while black farmers were largely confined to communal land, mainly in the north, where households grow mahangu, sorghum and maize for their own use and keep cattle mainly as wealth and security.
Wikipedia, Red Line (Namibia) ("North of the line ... all farm land is communal and operated mostly by black farmers"), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Line_(Namibia); Wikipedia, Land reform in Namibia (state buys farms from white owners for resettlement), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform_in_Namibia; Atlas of Namibia (online), Chapter 8 Farming, https://atlasofnamibia.online/chapter-8/farming (homesteads grow mahangu/maize/sorghum for domestic consumption; northern cattle kept as an asset for security or savings); accessed 2026-10-30.

CL-6137-2.2.05-R2
The veterinary cordon fence (Red Line), a colonial boundary from 1896-1907 built as a physical fence in the early 1960s, protects livestock to its south from foot-and-mouth disease and lung sickness; meat from the disease-free south can be exported, while northern livestock cannot be sold to overseas markets and needs quarantine to reach other markets, raising marketing costs.
Wikipedia, Red Line (Namibia), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Line_(Namibia); Atlas of Namibia (online), Chapter 8 Farming, https://atlasofnamibia.online/chapter-8/farming (fence protects livestock to its south from foot-and-mouth and lung sickness; southern meat exportable; quarantine facilities in the north); accessed 2026-10-30.

CL-6137-2.2.05-R3
After independence, the Agricultural (Commercial) Land Reform Act of 1995 allowed the state to buy commercial farms offered for sale ("willing buyer, willing seller") to resettle previously disadvantaged Namibians; progress was slower than hoped.
Wikipedia, Land reform in Namibia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reform_in_Namibia; World Hunger Notes, "Land Reform in Namibia: Slow Pace, Debatable Benefits", https://www.worldhunger.org/namibialandreform/; accessed 2026-10-30.

CL-6137-2.2.05-R4
Namibia’s Green Scheme develops irrigation along perennial rivers and at dams so that small-scale farmers can grow crops for sale alongside commercial operators; at Etunda near Ruacana in the Omusati Region, small-scale farmers each irrigate about 3 ha, growing crops such as maize, cabbage, tomatoes and onions; further projects lie along the Okavango River in the Kavango regions.
Agricultural Bank of Namibia, Green Scheme page (aims ~22,000 ha along perennial rivers; Etunda at Ruacana, Omusati; ~3 ha small-scale plots; crops), https://agribank.com.na/page/green-scheme/; Republikein, 18 Jan 2018, "Govt to continue irrigation scheme implementation" (Kavango projects Sikondo, Musese, Shadikongoro, Ndonga-Linena, Mashare), https://republikein.com.na/nuus/govt-to-continue-irrigation-scheme-implementation2018-01-18; accessed 2026-10-30.

CL-6137-2.2.04-03-R1
Large-scale irrigated commercial farms grow table grapes for export beside the Orange River at Aussenkehr, where rainfall is under 50 mm a year; Naute Dam near Keetmanshoop irrigates grapes and dates; southern karakul sheep farming for pelts collapsed in the 1980s and the south now keeps mainly sheep and goats.

CL-6137-2.2.12-R1
In Namibia’s northern communal areas, boreholes allowed livestock numbers to rise, adding grazing pressure around water points, and large areas of woodland in Kavango West and Kavango East have been cleared for crops; with bush encroachment on rangeland, these practices contribute to land degradation and desertification.
Halle and Bruzon, Country Environmental Profile of Namibia, final report, 2007 (boreholes in northern regions increased livestock numbers and overgrazing; bush encroachment), https://catalog.ihsn.org/citations/84817; Atlas of Namibia (online), Chapter 8 Farming, https://atlasofnamibia.online/chapter-8/farming (woodland cleared for crops in Kavango West and East); accessed 2026-10-30.

CL-6137-2.2.13-R1
Conservation agriculture, promoted on northern Namibian maize and mahangu fields, replaces ploughing with ripping narrow lines, keeps crop residues as soil cover and rotates cereals with legumes; ripping breaks the hard layer below the topsoil so water and roots go deeper, and trials in the Zambezi Region (2016/17-2019/20) found maize yields similar to ploughed fields.
Shiimi, T. (2025) Comparing conventional and conservation agriculture for multi-seasonal maize production in the Zambezi Region, Namibia. Namibian Journal of Environment 10A: 30-39, https://doi.org/10.64640/2b7j9a6d; New Era, "From ripping to reaping: milestones of NCAP", https://neweralive.na/?p=64448; accessed 2026-10-30.

Named Namibian tourist activities include dune climbing and photography at Sossusvlei in the Namib-Naukluft National Park, hiking the Fish River Canyon trail in southern Namibia during the cooler months (about May to mid-September), and adventure activities such as quad biking and skydiving at the coastal resort of Swakopmund.

CL-6137-2.3.03-R2
Etosha National Park, first proclaimed in 1907 and covering about 22,000 km2, is dominated by a large, usually dry salt pan; visitors take game drives, bird-watch and photograph wildlife at waterholes, including floodlit waterholes at the Okaukuejo, Halali and Namutoni rest camps.

Tourism development in Namibia is constrained by long distances between attractions and from overseas markets, a road network that is mostly gravel or earth rather than tar, and limited air links after the national airline stopped flying in February 2021, ending its direct Windhoek–Frankfurt service.

Ruacana on the Kunene River is a run-of-river hydroelectric station; the Kunene supplies up to 45 per cent of Namibia's electricity, and run-of-river output depends on river flow, which is lower in the dry season.
