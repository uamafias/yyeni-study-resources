## The private firm as producer and employer — 5.1

### Profit as an objective

Profit is total revenue minus total cost over a stated period. Profit maximisation means producing the output that gives the highest possible profit (total revenue minus total cost). Marginal revenue (MR) is the extra revenue from selling one more unit; marginal cost (MC) is the extra cost of producing one more unit. While MR is greater than MC, each extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so profit rises. Once MC is greater than MR, an extra unit reduces profit. In the usual model, profit is highest where MC = MR and extra units change from adding to profit (MR > MC) to reducing it (MC > MR). Equality alone is not enough if the change goes the other way. It differs from maximising output or sales revenue. A firm can sell more while adding costs that exceed the extra receipts. It can also reduce output and improve profit if the forgone sales would have cost more to supply than they earned. The comparison requires both sides of the calculation and a consistent period.

Suppose a cabinet maker has two complete monthly plans. Plan A gives revenue N$30,000 and total cost N$24,000, leaving N$6,000 profit. Plan B gives revenue N$35,000 and total cost N$30,000, leaving N$5,000. A yields N$1,000 more profit despite lower revenue. These figures describe complete stated plans, so the comparison is supported. If a case instead lists only material cost, do not subtract it and call the remainder profit: wages, premises or other costs may be missing. Use the information actually supplied and label a partial comparison accordingly.

### Decisions and time

A profit-seeking firm may seek higher receipts, lower costs or a more suitable combination of price and output. Whether an action works depends on buyers’ responses and production conditions. Raising a price can reduce sales, so the revenue effect requires demand evidence. Cutting maintenance may save money now but create breakdowns, delays or lost customers later. The time horizon matters: an immediate saving is not necessarily the greatest longer-term profit. A decision should identify which period the comparison concerns and what assumptions the expected result relies on.

Profit maximisation is an objective, not proof that every business decision achieves it. Firms face uncertainty and limited information about demand and future costs. Besides profit maximisation, firms may aim for survival (especially when new or during a recession), growth in size, a larger market share, maximum sales revenue, good customer service, or social responsibility such as protecting the environment and supporting the community. In this topic the analytical question is how a stated profit goal affects choices. Do not assume that an ambitious output target or a manager’s claim is evidence of a calculated optimum. Compare feasible alternatives and the relevant receipts and costs before interpreting the goal.

### Production includes services

Production is the creation of goods and services to satisfy needs and wants using productive resources. A bakery creates bread, while a repairer provides a service that restores the usefulness of a product. Both involve production. A service need not create a new physical object to count. Land, labour, capital and enterprise contribute in different ways, and a firm combines the resources suited to its task. The categories introduced in topic 1.2 help identify these inputs without treating money itself as a machine or skill.

Production and productivity answer different questions. If a workshop produces 60 chairs rather than 50 per week, output has increased. To decide whether productivity improved, compare output with the relevant input. If labour hours rise from 100 to 150, chairs per labour hour fall from 0.5 to 0.4 despite higher production. If hours stayed at 100, the new output would be 0.6 per hour instead. The input condition changes the interpretation. Productivity is developed in topic 5.2; here the distinction prevents assuming that increased production alone proves more efficient resource use.

Production takes place in stages. The primary sector extracts natural resources (farming, fishing, mining). The secondary sector processes or manufactures them (a sawmill turning logs into planks, a meat-processing plant). The tertiary sector provides services (transport, retail, banking, tourism). Value added is the value of a firm’s output minus the cost of the materials and services it buys from other firms. A carpenter who buys N$2,000 of timber and sells a table for N$5,000 has added N$3,000 of value. Value added is used to measure GDP (topic 7.3).

### Why firms demand factors

Factor demand is derived demand: firms want resources because those resources help produce output buyers want. Rising orders can encourage a laundry to employ more staff or use more equipment, provided the expansion is worthwhile and feasible. Falling demand for the service can reduce that need. A factor is not demanded simply because its price is low; it must contribute to production in a way the firm can use. Productive contribution and the value of output therefore matter alongside resource cost.

Relative factor prices can affect the combination chosen. If manual work becomes more expensive relative to suitable machinery, a firm may consider substituting equipment. Technical feasibility matters: some tasks require judgement or personal interaction that the available equipment cannot replace satisfactorily. Other inputs can be complements. A machine may require an operator, maintenance and reliable power, so purchasing it can increase demand for some labour or other resources even while reducing manual tasks. A sound explanation identifies the output, the resource contribution and the condition allowing the proposed change. It does not treat all capital as interchangeable with all labour.

A firm will employ an extra worker if the value of what that worker adds, the marginal revenue product (extra output multiplied by marginal revenue), is at least equal to the wage.

### Substitution and its consequences

Factor substitution replaces some use of one input with another. Production is labour-intensive when it uses a high proportion of labour relative to capital (for example hand-made crafts), and capital-intensive when it uses a high proportion of machinery relative to labour (for example a bottling plant). Substituting machines for workers makes production more capital-intensive. A packing firm might use a sealing machine for work previously done by hand. Potential benefits include consistency, faster processing or lower cost per satisfactory package. Against these gains, equipment requires finance, maintenance and training, and may be unsuitable when orders are small or designs change frequently. Compare complete relevant costs and the quality of output rather than the machine price alone.

Labour effects are also specific. Some manual sealing tasks may disappear while operation, inspection or maintenance tasks expand. Whether employment falls overall depends on the new labour requirements, output response and time period. Workers may need retraining and adjustment can be difficult even if total output grows. For a justified decision, establish that the technology performs the required task, likely demand supports its use and expected savings exceed the relevant costs over a suitable period. This links substitution to occupational skills in 4.2, productivity in 5.2 and costs in 5.3. A technical possibility is the start of an evaluation, not a conclusion that customers, workers and owners gain equally.

A complete like-for-like comparison can clarify the cost argument. Suppose manual and machine methods each deliver 1,000 satisfactory packages in a month and each produces N$9,000 sales revenue. Total relevant cost for that month is N$4,800 manually and N$4,300 with equipment, including the equipment-related charge, operation, training and maintenance stated for this illustration. Profit is N$4,200 manually and N$4,700 with equipment. The N$500 advantage follows because output quality and revenue are held equal and the cost figures are complete for the stated period. If setup cost or downtime were omitted, the same conclusion would require further information.

The example does not establish the best choice at another volume. A method efficient for 1,000 packages might be underused at 200, while changing design requirements might create extra setup work. Nor does the financial difference show how benefits are distributed: owners may gain while particular workers need retraining or lose tasks. Customers benefit only if the change improves their price, quality or access. Keep these perspectives distinct when evaluating substitution. Identify the affected task, check the complete comparison and then consider output, employment and service consequences. This turns a vague statement that machinery is cheaper into a judgement whose assumptions and limits can be checked. Evidence about the proposed method should therefore be matched to the actual task and production volume before inferring a general advantage.
