## Price elasticity

### Responsiveness is different from a shift

Price elasticity measures the responsiveness of a quantity to a change in the product’s own price using percentage changes. Demand elasticity concerns quantity demanded; supply elasticity concerns quantity supplied. Each asks how strongly quantity responds, rather than merely identifying whether the curve slopes up or down. Two demand relationships can both slope down while showing different responses to a similar price change.

Keep responsiveness separate from a non-price determinant moving a curve. A change in income or preferences can shift demand, while a price-elasticity comparison normally holds other demand conditions unchanged. Likewise, an improvement in production technology can shift supply, whereas price elasticity of supply describes the quantity-supplied response along a given relationship over the stated period. If several conditions change together, the observed price and quantity differences need not identify a pure response to own price.

The percentage basis makes comparisons more meaningful than raw quantities alone. Ten extra units can be a large response for a producer initially offering five units and a small response for one initially offering ten thousand. State which quantity responds, the price change and the bases used. That discipline prevents a large-looking absolute number from being mistaken automatically for high elasticity.

### Price elasticity of demand

Price elasticity of demand is the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a price change, measured by percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price. For conventional downward-sloping demand, quantity and price move in opposite directions, giving a negative signed coefficient. The magnitude, without that negative sign, is commonly used to classify demand. State the convention rather than confusing a negative sign with low responsiveness.

A magnitude greater than one indicates elastic demand: the quantity response is proportionately larger than the price change. A magnitude between zero and one indicates inelastic demand: quantity changes, but by a smaller proportion. A magnitude of one is unit elastic in the percentage comparison. Perfectly inelastic demand is the special case of zero response. On a price-vertical, quantity-horizontal construction it is represented by a vertical demand line at the fixed quantity.

Inelastic does not mean that buyers ignore price completely. For example, a 5% reduction in quantity after a 10% price increase is inelastic but not perfectly inelastic. The distinction is useful when explaining revenue and when comparing markets. A curve’s apparent steepness on a page is not enough to establish a numerical coefficient without its scales, quantities and prices; use the percentage relationship rather than judging from the picture alone.

### Price elasticity of supply

Price elasticity of supply is the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a price change, measured by percentage change in quantity supplied divided by percentage change in price. In the conventional upward-sloping relationship, both changes have the same sign, so the coefficient is positive. A coefficient above one is elastic supply, a coefficient between zero and one inelastic supply, and zero indicates no quantity-supplied response over the stated comparison.

Supply responsiveness depends on what producers can do during the period. An existing stock of suitable goods may be offered quickly, while producing additional units can require materials, working time or new capacity. The availability of those options is part of the explanation. A higher price does not physically create equipment or skilled workers without time or resources.

Do not infer PES from a situation where a separate supply shock changes the relationship itself. If input costs rise while quality or demand also changes, comparing the resulting price and quantity can mix several causes. A clean teaching calculation supplies an own-price change with other production conditions held unchanged over the stated period. The coefficient then describes that response rather than proving a universal rule for every later season or product.

### Calculate percentages before the ratio

With original values as bases, percentage price change is (new price − original price) ÷ original price × 100. Apply the same method to the relevant quantity. PED divides the percentage change in quantity demanded by the percentage price change; PES uses quantity supplied instead. Keep the numerator and denominator in that order. Using a raw quantity change divided by a money change gives a slope-like figure, not the stated percentage elasticity.

For a price change from N$10 to N$11, the increase is N$1 and the original base is N$10, giving 10%. If quantity demanded changes from 100 to 95, the change is −5 on a base of 100, giving −5%. Dividing −5% by 10% gives −0.5, or a magnitude of 0.5. The percentage signs cancel in the ratio. Do not report the final coefficient as N$0.5 or 0.5%.

Use the base convention specified in a question. A different convention can produce a different finite-change estimate, so do not silently mix original bases with new bases. The present examples deliberately use original values and small clean changes. Rounding should not turn a coefficient just below one into a confident classification above it; retain enough working to support the conclusion.

### A complete demand calculation and interpretation

Use invented data for a Namibian notebook seller, with other demand conditions unchanged: price rises from N$10 to N$11 per notebook and weekly quantity demanded falls from 100 to 95. Price changes by 10%; quantity demanded changes by −5%. PED is −5 ÷ 10 = −0.5 using the original bases. Its magnitude is 0.5, so demand is inelastic over this stated comparison.

Interpret the economic meaning as well as naming the category. The quantity-demanded response is proportionately smaller than the price rise; buyers reduce purchases, but not by as large a percentage. It is not perfectly inelastic because the quantity actually changes. The comparison alone does not establish which individual buyers leave or which substitute they use.

Check revenue with the actual figures: the original N$10 × 100 gives N$1,000 per week, while N$11 × 95 gives N$1,045. Here receipts rise even though fewer notebooks are sold. That result is consistent with inelastic demand in this example, but it is not yet a profit calculation. Printing, distribution and other relevant costs must still be considered. Keep the calculation, the response classification and the business judgement as separate steps rather than letting the word inelastic stand in for all three.

### A complete supply calculation and interpretation

Use invented data for a small producer, with the relevant supply relationship unchanged: a price rise from N$20 to N$22 accompanies an increase in weekly quantity supplied from 50 to 60. Price rises by 2 ÷ 20 × 100 = 10%. Quantity supplied rises by 10 ÷ 50 × 100 = 20%. PES is 20 ÷ 10 = 2, so supply is elastic over this comparison.

The coefficient means the percentage quantity-supplied response is larger than the percentage price change. It does not mean supply rose by two units or that every price rise produces exactly twice as many physical units. The original bases and units explain the result. If the period or available capacity changes, a later coefficient can differ.

Relate the result to feasible production. A producer with spare equipment time and accessible materials may be able to expand output promptly. A producer already operating at full capacity may have fewer short-run options. The numerical coefficient describes the supplied evidence; an explanation of the cause needs facts about capacity, stocks, labour and time. A strong response distinguishes what the figures establish from what remains a possible explanation rather than inventing an unstated spare machine or unlimited materials.

### Apply demand elasticity to a price decision

Total revenue is price multiplied by quantity sold. With other demand conditions unchanged, a small price increase on locally inelastic demand can raise total revenue because the proportionate reduction in quantity is relatively small. On locally elastic demand, the larger quantity response tends to outweigh the higher price per unit, reducing revenue. A price reduction reverses the revenue comparison under the corresponding conditions.

Use a second invented notebook case: the same price increase from N$10 to N$11 is followed by quantity falling from 100 to 80. The 20% quantity reduction relative to the 10% price increase gives PED magnitude 2. Revenue falls from N$1,000 to N$880. That differs from the magnitude-0.5 case, where the supplied figures give N$1,045. The examples make the response conditions concrete rather than claiming that any price rise increases receipts.

For a sizeable finite change, calculate price times quantity from the supplied endpoints as a check instead of treating an estimated coefficient as a guarantee. Demand might also change during the period because preferences, income or competitors change. A pricing decision therefore needs a relevant estimate and clear assumptions. Costs still separate revenue from profit: attracting more purchases can require more production, while lost sales may alter costs in ways the revenue figures alone do not establish.

### Apply supply elasticity to capacity and time

Available stocks can make output offered for sale more responsive to a higher price, provided the stock is suitable and can be released. Spare production capacity can allow a firm to use equipment for more hours or increase the use of existing workers and materials. If those inputs are accessible, a price incentive can translate into more quantity supplied relatively quickly.

Time can widen adjustment options. Over a very short period, a grower cannot instantly complete another crop cycle, while over a longer period planting decisions and resources can be adjusted. A manufacturer may be able to vary working hours sooner than build a new factory. These comparisons need the actual production circumstances: longer time permits more options but does not guarantee a particular coefficient for every industry.

The concept is useful when predicting the effect of a demand increase. More responsive supply can accommodate more of the increased quantity demand with less price pressure than a strongly constrained supply relationship, other relevant conditions comparable. A firm considering expansion should therefore check stocks, spare capacity, materials and skills rather than assume a higher selling price is enough. The distinction remains between a response along the given relationship and a separate technology or cost change shifting it.

### Use elasticity as evidence, not as a complete decision

An elasticity estimate helps a seller assess the likely quantity response to a proposed price. It does not identify every objective or cost. If the goal is revenue, the comparison of price and quantity is directly relevant. If the goal is profit, costs and capacity also matter. If maintaining access or a long-term customer relationship matters, an immediate revenue gain may not settle the choice.

Evaluate a price increase by developing both sides. The case for can be a plausible revenue gain under inelastic demand and unchanged other conditions. The case against can include lost customers, uncertainty in the estimate or higher costs. The decisive condition is whether the response estimate is relevant and whether the revenue change, after costs and other objectives are considered, supports the decision. An elastic response strengthens the case against a revenue-seeking price increase, but the conclusion should still use the supplied evidence.

Supply conditions can constrain a price reduction intended to attract more buyers. If additional output cannot be supplied in the relevant period, the predicted demand response does not automatically become additional sales. A justified judgement therefore combines the buyer response with feasible output rather than confusing PED and PES or treating one coefficient as a complete business plan.

### Factors behind different responses

Close substitutes can make buyers more willing to switch after a price rise. A product taking a large share of a household’s budget can make a price change more significant to its choices. Time can allow buyers to find alternatives or adjust habits. Necessity may reduce some scope to respond, but it is not a guarantee of perfectly inelastic demand: actual options and circumstances still matter.

On the supply side, stocks, spare capacity, factor availability and the time needed for production affect adjustment. Explain the relevant side separately. Buyers having substitutes is not the same mechanism as a producer having a spare machine. These distinctions help prevent a correctly calculated coefficient from being paired with an explanation belonging to the other curve.

### The starting values affect the percentage comparison

Compare two invented movements along a demand schedule. In the first, price changes from N$10 to N$11 and quantity from 100 to 90. In the second, price changes from N$15 to N$16 and quantity from 50 to 40. The absolute changes are the same: price rises by N$1 and quantity falls by ten units. Their percentage responses differ because the bases differ.

In the first comparison, quantity falls by 10% and price rises by 10%, giving PED magnitude 1. In the second, the quantity fall is 20%, while the price change is one fifteenth of the initial price. Dividing the quantity proportion 0.2 by the price proportion 1/15 gives magnitude 3. Using proportions instead of percentages in both parts gives the same ratio because the factors of 100 cancel. The example shows why an absolute slope or the visual angle of a curve cannot substitute for the percentage calculation. It does not require a new formula or a judgement about profit.

### Check that the comparison matches the question

Suppose an invented seller observes both a higher price and more quantity purchased after a product becomes fashionable. That evidence can reflect a demand shift, not a movement along the old demand curve. Dividing the two observed changes and calling the result PED would ignore the changed preference. The calculation needs the relevant other conditions held unchanged, or a clearly stated model isolating the own-price response.

The same caution applies to a comparison using a different product quality or a different time basis. A weekly quantity cannot be compared directly with an annual quantity without conversion, and a larger product may not be the same unit as the original. Establish the product, period, units and assumed unchanged conditions before interpreting the coefficient. Arithmetic can be correct while the economic meaning assigned to it is wrong.
