## Population — 8.1

### Development and different starting conditions

Economic development means improvement in economic and social conditions, including productive capacity, access to services and living conditions. It is broader than a larger output total. Developed and developing are broad descriptions of conditions, not a rule that residents share one income or that countries follow an identical path. Comparisons should identify evidence about productivity, income, education, health, infrastructure and opportunities rather than infer people’s abilities or preferences from a national label.

Capital resources, skills, institutions, geography, access to markets and historical conditions can influence development. Reliable transport can make trade and service access easier; suitable education can improve productive skills when opportunities exist. Limited finance or infrastructure can constrain investment, while weak coordination can reduce the benefit of available resources. These mechanisms interact and require evidence. A country rich in a particular resource does not automatically provide high living standards for each resident, since management, distribution and wider costs matter. When comparing Namibia with another country, use the relevant period, definition and indicators. A national average does not describe every household. A useful comparison asks what constrains a particular activity. Missing transport may raise market-access costs, while unavailable skills may limit use of equipment. Different constraints require different responses, and a single broad development label does not identify them. Evidence about services and opportunities also helps distinguish real improvements from a larger nominal income total caused by prices.

Developing economies usually have low real GDP per head, a large share of workers in the primary sector (farming, fishing, mining), high birth rates and often high death rates, a young population with a high dependency ratio, lower literacy and life expectancy, and weaker infrastructure. Developed economies usually have high real GDP per head, most workers in the tertiary (services) sector, low birth and death rates, an older population, high literacy and life expectancy, and good infrastructure. As a country develops, the share of output and employment in the primary sector tends to fall, the secondary (manufacturing) sector grows, and later the tertiary sector becomes the largest. Reasons for different stages of development include differences in capital, education and skills, infrastructure, natural resources, political stability, access to markets and rates of population growth.

### Adjustment as development proceeds

Development can provide employment, infrastructure and access to goods and services, while changing how and where people live and work. Urbanisation is an increasing proportion of the population living in urban areas. It can bring people closer to employers, education and services, but requires housing, transport, water and other capacity. If settlement grows faster than infrastructure, congestion or poor access can offset some benefits. The process is not automatically either a problem or a success; compare the resources and opportunities available.

An expanding town gains processing and repair work. Workers and firms may benefit from proximity and shared services, while authorities face pressure to plan transport and housing. Some existing activities may contract or need new skills. Gains can be distributed unevenly, so a higher town output does not prove each family benefits. Environmental effects and the opportunity cost of infrastructure should also be considered. A supported explanation identifies the initial economic change, the adjustment it requires and the possible consequence for a specified group. Policy needs to fit those constraints instead of simply urging everyone to move. Development is a change in productive and living conditions, whose quality depends on access, distribution and sustainable use of resources. The pace of adjustment matters to both costs and gains. Infrastructure takes time to plan and provide, while household needs occur immediately. A short-term bottleneck can therefore coexist with a longer-term opportunity. State the time horizon and the affected groups rather than treat development as one instant change with equal effects everywhere.

Problems as a country develops include rapid rural-urban migration and the growth of informal settlements, a shortage of jobs when job creation lags behind the growing labour force, pollution and environmental damage from industry, depletion of natural resources, widening income inequality between regions and groups, loss of traditional skills and culture, and government borrowing for infrastructure that leads to debt. Developed countries face different problems, such as an ageing population, high pension and health-care costs, labour shortages and congestion.

### Population accounting and reasons for change

Natural population change is births minus deaths in a period. Total change adds immigration and subtracts emigration. All components need the same geographical boundary and period. In a district with 10,000 initial residents, 250 births, 100 deaths, 200 arrivals and 150 departures, natural increase is 150 and net migration 50. Total increase is 200, giving 10,200 residents if no other statistical adjustments are assumed. The 200 arrivals alone are not net migration, and the 150 natural increase alone is not total change.

Birth rates tend to be high where children are seen as extra labour or support in old age, where family planning is less available or less used, where people marry young, and where cultural or religious values favour large families. Birth rates tend to fall as women’s education and employment increase and as the cost of raising children rises. Death rates fall with better health care, clean water, sanitation, nutrition and vaccination, and rise with disease such as HIV and AIDS or with famine. Net migration rises when a country offers more jobs, higher wages or greater safety than its neighbours. Population grows quickly when birth rates stay high while death rates fall. Age composition, education and household circumstances also matter. For Namibia or another country, distinguish a reasoned mechanism from a verified current trend. A population with many people entering reproductive ages can have substantial births even when a fertility measure falls. Changes in mortality can affect natural increase, while movement towards work or services can alter local populations. It is not enough to say a place develops and therefore its population must grow at one fixed rate. Interpret the specific components and circumstances. Births, deaths, fertility and migration are defined carefully so that a count, a per-thousand rate and a per-woman measure are not treated as interchangeable. A percentage rate should use the population basis requested. In the district illustration, 200 additional residents relative to the initial 10,000 gives 2 percent total growth. That is not the same measure as the birth rate alone or the net-migration count. Keep additions and rates separate, and do not add percentages with incompatible denominators as though they were counts.

### Size, structure and the economic response

Population size is the total number, while structure describes its composition, including age. A larger child population can increase demand for schooling and family services. More older residents can change care, accessibility and income-support needs. A larger working-age group can increase potential labour supply and demand for products. The actual effect depends on health, skills, productive resources and opportunities, not simply a person crossing an age threshold.

Suppose a population is 1.8 million and 62 percent belongs to the age group specified in a task. The number is 0.62 times 1,800,000 =1,116,000 people, or 1.116 million. Labelling it 1,116 million would create a thousandfold error. An age-group percentage also does not tell us how many actually work or depend on others. Some working-age people are studying or lack jobs; some older people work. Ratios using age bands are indicators with assumptions rather than exact records of dependency.

A larger market can support firms and more specialisation, but it also requires housing, infrastructure and investment. If real output fails to keep pace, output per person can fall. A policy judgement should identify which needs and resources change, the response time and how gains or burdens are distributed. This links population to employment in 7.2, output in 7.3 and living standards in 8.2. Do not equate more people with economic growth or assume one age structure guarantees prosperity. Population structure can also change without a large total change. A place may have similar resident numbers but a larger older share, altering the type of service demand. Another may attract working-age arrivals and need housing near jobs. Planning therefore needs composition as well as size. The economic mechanism follows the specific needs and resources, not the total alone. Movement between regions redistributes service demand even when national population is unchanged. A supported response therefore connects the level of the decision—local or national—to the boundary of the demographic evidence and the resources that can actually be redirected.

The age-dependency ratio compares people in dependent age bands (often under 15 and 65 or over) with the working-age population (15–64): age-dependency ratio = (population in the dependent age bands ÷ working-age population) × 100. A high ratio means more people in those age bands per 100 working-age people, which can put pressure on education, health and pensions. It does not count actual dependants per employed worker: some working-age people do not work and some older people do. An ageing population raises spending on pensions and health care and can cause labour shortages. Rapid population growth can strain government aims: more people may be without work if jobs grow more slowly than the labour force, GDP per head falls if output grows more slowly than population, housing, schools, clinics, water and food come under pressure, and resources are used up faster. This is why a government may try to slow high population growth through education, family-planning services and incentives.

## Population — 8.1.3

### Birth rate and birth numbers

The crude birth rate is live births during a year per thousand people in the relevant population. Calculate births divided by population, multiplied by 1,000, using a consistent population basis when the exercise supplies it. In an area with 300 births and 20,000 people, the rate is 15 births per thousand. It is neither 15 percent nor 300 births per thousand. Units and denominator distinguish the rate from the number of births.

The same rate can produce different counts. At 15 per thousand, 40,000 people correspond to 600 births in the simplified calculation. Comparing 300 births in one place with 600 in another therefore does not establish a higher birth rate without population information. Age structure also matters because the crude rate uses the entire population as denominator rather than only people in reproductive ages. A change in the number and age distribution of potential parents can affect total births separately from individual childbearing patterns. This helps explain why births need not fall immediately when a fertility measure declines. For a supplied Namibia or international comparison, read the year, population and measure rather than inventing a current trend. The calculation gives a defined summary, while explaining the summary needs relevant demographic and economic evidence. Births contribute to natural change, but deaths and migration are still needed for a complete population-accounting conclusion. If a problem supplies a population in millions, convert consistently before obtaining a count. A rate per thousand multiplied by a population count must also be divided by a thousand. Writing both steps protects against a large scale error. The rate’s meaning is unchanged by the display unit, while the arithmetic must use compatible numbers.

### Death rate and population composition

The crude death rate is deaths during a year per thousand people in the relevant population. It uses the total population denominator, so its interpretation depends partly on age composition. An area with 160 deaths and 20,000 people has a rate of 8 per thousand. Subtracting this from a birth rate 15 per thousand gives natural increase 7 per thousand on the same basis, before migration. The unit is per thousand, not an unlabelled population percentage.

A population containing many older residents can have a higher crude death rate than a younger one even if its death rate at each age is the same or lower. The crude number alone therefore does not establish that healthcare is worse. Other evidence about ages and circumstances is needed for that comparison. Changes in survival and living conditions can affect deaths, but the mechanism should be stated without using an aggregate to diagnose individual health. For population accounting, a fall in deaths with births unchanged increases natural change. That does not by itself reveal the final population if migration is also occurring. This links death rates to the population-change identity and prevents replacing a component with the whole. Use the same area and period for births, deaths and population, and keep a count distinct from the per-thousand summary when calculating a result. A count can decline while the crude rate rises if the population denominator shrinks sufficiently. This is another reason to inspect the numerator and denominator rather than interpret either separately. The same ratio principle applies to birth rates and migration rates, while each has a different event being counted. Describe the actual comparison before assigning a cause.

### Fertility and its measurement basis

Fertility concerns childbearing and needs a defined measure. Total fertility rate summarises how many children a hypothetical woman would have through reproductive ages if she experienced the period’s age-specific fertility rates. It is commonly expressed as children per woman. It is not the annual number of births divided by the entire population, and it is not a promise about each individual’s completed family size.

An illustrative total fertility measure of 2.5 children per woman does not mean each woman has exactly 2.5 children or that the national population grows 2.5 percent that year. It summarises a pattern of age-specific rates under the definition’s conditions. Other fertility measures can use births relative to women in specified ages, so read the exact label and units rather than silently treating them as identical. The important distinction is between childbearing intensity, crude births per thousand total residents and total births.

Education, opportunities, family circumstances and access to relevant services can influence childbearing patterns, but a national label alone is not evidence of one family’s choices. Demographic structure affects how a pattern translates into births. Population change then additionally depends on deaths and migration. Keep these stages separate when explaining how a fertility change might influence future size or structure, and avoid a precise population forecast without the necessary component data. The total-fertility summary represents a pattern under the stated period’s rates, not a direct measurement of all future births. A population projection would also need age structure, survival and migration assumptions. A comparison can explain likely channels without producing a precise forecast from one fertility number. State that limitation clearly.

### Net migration and boundaries

Net migration is immigration minus emigration over a stated period. A count of arrivals alone is not net migration. If 600 people enter a country and 450 leave, net migration is positive 150. If the same totals are reversed, it is minus 150. The sign tells the direction of the migration contribution, while a rate additionally requires a stated population denominator and multiplier. Do not call 150 a percentage or a per-thousand rate without that basis.

The boundary matters. Moving from one Namibian town to another can change both local populations but does not add to the national total through international migration. For a district account, arrivals and departures across its boundary matter; for a national account, international movements are the relevant component. Work, family, education or safety can influence movement, while finance and access constrain choices. A migrant is not automatically unemployed, highly paid or in one skill group; effects on labour and services depend on the people and opportunities involved.

This links back to total population change. With births 1,000, deaths 700 and net migration minus 100, natural increase is 300 but total increase is 200. The population can grow despite net outward movement. Conversely, positive inward movement can offset natural decline. The accounting identity clarifies the components, while evaluating economic effects requires the changing age mix, skills, resources and service needs rather than a judgement from migration’s sign alone. A positive net figure can conceal large flows in both directions. Immigration 1,000 and emigration 900 give the same net 100 as immigration 150 and emigration 50, but the gross movements differ. Their implications for services and adjustment may therefore differ even though the net addition matches. Net is a useful summary whose limits should remain clear when evaluating the underlying changes.
