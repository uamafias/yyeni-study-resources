## Advertising — 3.4

### Advertising and its intended effect

Advertising is communication intended to inform or persuade an audience about a product, service or organisation. It can make people aware that something exists, explain relevant features or encourage them to choose one offer rather than another. The audience is the group the advertiser wants to reach. A message should therefore be understood in relation to both its content and the people expected to receive it. A notice for a local repair service and a national product campaign may both advertise, despite their different scale.

Advertising is an attempt to influence behaviour, rather than proof that behaviour changes. Someone may hear a message but have no need for the product, find it unaffordable or prefer an alternative. Seeing an advertisement does not establish that the viewer purchased because of it. Equally, sales following a campaign could be affected by other changes, such as the season or price. Distinguishing communication, attention, enquiry and purchase makes the mechanism clearer. More attention can be useful, but its value depends on whether the intended audience receives relevant information and can act on it.

### Purpose and method are different choices

An advertising purpose answers why the message is being sent. Its method answers how it reaches people. An invented shoe-repair business might want residents to know its new opening hours and choose a radio announcement to communicate them. Informing customers is the purpose; radio is the method. The same radio channel could carry a message persuading listeners that repair is preferable to replacement. Conversely, the same information about opening hours could appear on a poster or digital notice. Do not classify a message as persuasive merely because it is electronic or as informative merely because it is printed.

The method should fit the audience and the information. Residents within walking distance might notice a nearby poster. Distant customers might need another channel. A radio message can explain the service and repeat contact details, but cannot show the finish of a repaired shoe. Photographs can show appearance but may omit conditions or fail to reach people without suitable access. These are reasons to compare channels using the specific task, because suitability depends on the audience and circumstances. A firm also needs to consider how much communication it can afford and whether it can fulfil the enquiries it generates.

### Evaluating a campaign

Suppose the repairer receives more enquiries after a message but completes no additional jobs. Advertising may have raised awareness without yet increasing purchases. It should examine whether the message reached suitable customers, explained the offer clearly and supplied workable contact details. This links to digital and physical market access in topic 3.1 and buyer demand in 3.2. Advertising can affect information or preferences; it does not automatically shift demand by a known amount. The evaluation should compare the intended result with relevant evidence and the cost of obtaining it. The comparison should state what the advertiser wants to achieve and which people the message is expected to reach. A channel choice without those details has little basis for assessing suitability or interpreting later results.

## Advertising — 3.4.2

### Informing, persuading and reminding

An advertising purpose is the intended effect on the audience. Informing supplies useful information, such as a product's availability, price, location, features or conditions. Persuading tries to influence preference and encourage a choice. Reminding keeps an existing product or organisation in people's awareness. These purposes can overlap. A message giving a new service's location may also use a clear image or claim to encourage customers to prefer it. Identify the relevant parts of the message instead of assuming the whole communication has a single possible role.

An invented refill shop might publish its opening hours and explain which containers customers can bring. That reduces uncertainty about how to use the service. If it also emphasises convenience to encourage a switch from another shopping habit, it has a persuasive element. A repeated short reminder before the weekend may aim to keep the shop in mind. Different purposes call for different evidence of success. Awareness might be checked through whether people know the location, while a purchase objective requires evidence about customer action. A large number of views alone does not prove a sales objective was met. The intended effect and the observed result should therefore be stated separately.

### Methods and audience fit

An advertising method is the communication channel used to reach an audience. Common methods include printed notices or newspapers, radio, outdoor signs and digital media. Print can carry detailed information that a reader can revisit, but requires access and attention. Radio can reach listeners over an area and use spoken explanation, but a brief message may be missed or forgotten. Outdoor signs can reach people passing a particular location; their usefulness depends on where they are displayed and whether the message can be read in time. Digital methods can combine images, text and contact opportunities, while depending on suitable access.

For an invented local bicycle-maintenance service, a large audience outside its service area may be less useful than a smaller relevant audience nearby. A detailed digital notice could explain repair options, while a sign near the workshop could help residents find it. Repeating an unclear message does not resolve missing information. The business should match the channel's format to what customers need to know and compare cost with the likely relevant reach. Do not assume that a free posting has no opportunity cost: preparation and responding to enquiries use time, even where no placement fee is charged. Nor does paying for a large campaign guarantee that people will notice or believe it.

### A justified method choice

Choose a method by linking an audience characteristic to a channel feature and then to the intended effect. If customers need to see dimensions or appearance, a visual format may help. If connectivity is unreliable, digital communication alone may leave some people uninformed. These explanations connect the method to the purpose rather than listing media without reasons. A combination can be appropriate if each channel serves a distinct audience or information need at an acceptable cost. A radio announcement can suit a spoken location reminder, while a leaflet can retain detailed dimensions for later reading. State which information the audience needs before comparing these methods; neither example establishes a universal ranking.
