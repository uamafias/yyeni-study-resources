## Financial institutions — 2.3

### Why people exchange

Specialisation allows people to concentrate on particular activities, but it leaves them needing products made by others. A person repairing bicycles needs food, clothing and housing without producing all of these personally. Exchange links specialised producers and consumers. It allows a producer to sell output and obtain other products from the proceeds. This connects financial arrangements to the division of labour in topic 1.3: producing efficiently is useful when people can also obtain what they need from one another.

Barter is the direct exchange of goods or services for other goods or services. Imagine a potter offering a bowl to a vegetable grower. A trade is possible if the grower wants the bowl and the potter accepts the vegetables offered. If the grower instead wants a repaired roof, the potter may have difficulty completing the trade. This matching problem is often called the double coincidence of wants. It concerns both parties wanting what the other offers on acceptable terms. Different values, indivisible goods and the difficulty of storing some products add further obstacles. Money can reduce these obstacles when both parties accept it, allowing the potter to sell to one person and buy vegetables from another.

### What money does and what makes it useful

Money is an asset generally accepted as a means of payment and used to express value. Do not confuse money with income or wealth. Income is a flow received over a period; a money balance is held at a point in time. Wealth can include buildings and equipment that are valuable but are not generally used as everyday payment. A workshop may own valuable machinery while lacking a bank balance sufficient to pay a bill today.

Three questions organise the topic. A function asks what money does, such as expressing a price or paying for goods. A quality asks what helps money do its work, such as portability or divisibility. A type identifies the form in which monetary value is held, such as cash or a bank deposit. Electronic payment methods also require attention because they can transfer those balances. A shopper paying N$75 by debit card uses a payment method to access money held in an account; the plastic card does not create an additional N$75. These distinctions prevent the same transaction being counted as several separate amounts of money. The sections below develop each function, quality and form with its own example.

### Calculating an exchange

An exchange rate is the value of one currency expressed in terms of another currency. Read the direction of the quotation before calculating. In an illustrative exercise, suppose one foreign currency unit costs N$18. A foreign bill of 40 units costs 40 × N$18 = N$720 before charges. To find how many foreign units N$900 purchases, divide N$900 by N$18 per foreign unit: the result is 50 foreign units. Multiplying and dividing serve different directions of conversion. Attach currency labels so that the units show what the answer means.

If the quotation changes to N$20 per foreign unit, the same 40-unit bill costs N$800. More Namibian dollars are now required for the same foreign amount, so the Namibian dollar is weaker against that currency. A larger numerical quotation does not necessarily mean a stronger domestic currency. If instead the rate is quoted as foreign units per N$1, the direction reverses. These are example rates. Real transactions may include charges and different buying and selling rates; include them when they are stated.

An independent check reverses the conversion. The N$720 bill at N$18 per foreign unit converts back to 40 foreign units because 720 ÷ 18 = 40. At the later rate, N$800 converts to the same 40 foreign units because 800 ÷ 20 = 40. The purchase size is constant; the domestic-currency cost changes. A common error is to multiply when converting domestic funds into foreign units even though the quotation is domestic currency per foreign unit. Writing the units exposes the mistake: N$ divided by N$ per foreign unit gives foreign units. The relationship can be understood with ordinary multiplication and division; no complicated formula is needed.

### Effects of currency fluctuations

Depreciation is a fall in a currency’s value relative to other currencies under market determination. Appreciation is the corresponding rise. If the domestic currency depreciates and a supplier keeps the foreign price unchanged, an importer needs more domestic currency for the same goods. Imported materials can then raise a firm’s costs, potentially adding to domestic prices. Appreciation has the reverse direct effect on that unchanged foreign-price import bill. Whether consumers receive the full price change depends on sellers’ costs and pricing decisions.

Export effects require equal care. An exporter keeping a domestic-currency price unchanged may become cheaper to foreign buyers following depreciation. Export demand may rise, but buyers must respond and producers must be able to supply more. Imported inputs may partly offset the gain. An exporter instead invoicing in a fixed foreign amount receives more domestic currency when converting the receipts, without necessarily reducing the price paid by its customers. Thus a weaker currency does not automatically improve the current account: trade demand must be sufficiently responsive and supply can respond; production capacity and the time available also matter. Namibia’s [central bank monetary-policy explanation](https://www.bon.com.na/Bank/Monetary-Policy.aspx) describes one-to-one parity with the South African rand. That arrangement does not mean exchange rates against other currencies are fixed. The numerical examples here concern an illustrative foreign currency, not a freely fluctuating N$/rand rate.

For the economy as a whole, a depreciation tends to make exports cheaper abroad, so export and tourism firms may sell more and employ more workers; imports become dearer, so imported inflation may rise (fuel, machinery, food) and living costs increase; repaying debts owed in foreign currency costs more Namibia dollars; and frequent swings create uncertainty, which makes traders and investors cautious. An appreciation tends to have the opposite effects: cheaper imports and lower inflation, but exporters lose competitiveness and jobs may be lost. Because the Namibia dollar is pegged one-to-one to the rand, it rises or falls against the US dollar, the euro and other currencies whenever the rand does.

### Financial institutions in an economy

Commercial banks help customers hold deposits, make payments and obtain loans. Central banks perform monetary and banking-system functions. Stock exchanges organise trading in securities, which represent financial claims such as ownership shares. These institutions serve different needs. A customer paying a supplier through a bank account is not trading shares on an exchange. A bank providing a workshop loan is not carrying out the same function as the central bank issuing currency or supporting the payment system.

Their contribution should be evaluated rather than assumed. Convenient payments reduce the difficulties of exchange. Bank lending can help fund productive activity when borrowers can service the debt. Securities markets can support company finance and give investors a route to sell holdings. Yet charges, distance, digital access, credit risk and uncertain investment returns limit benefits. A loan that funds suitable equipment can support production, while an unaffordable repayment schedule can strain the same firm. A shareholding can gain or lose value. The appropriate institution therefore depends on the task, the amount, the time horizon and the risk the user can bear. This connects to saving and spending in topic 4.3, firms’ production decisions in theme 5 and economic policy in theme 6. Financial services facilitate decisions; they do not remove scarcity or make every project worthwhile.

Compare two financing cases. An existing shareholder selling a holding receives the buyer’s money; the company’s available cash does not necessarily change. A company issuing new shares receives funds from the new investors under that issue. A bank loan is different again: the borrower receives funds while taking on a repayment obligation. Each can be described as finance in a broad discussion, but the recipient and obligation differ. State which transaction occurs before explaining who benefits. The distinction helps a learner avoid recommending a stock exchange as if it were a lender offering an ordinary overdraft. It also prevents confusing a business’s rising share price with additional cash available in its bank account.

## Financial institutions — 2.3.2

### Functions as a connected system

The functions of money are the economic jobs it performs. Four central functions are medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value and standard of deferred payment. They concern payment now, expressing value, keeping purchasing power for later and stating future obligations. Naming them is a starting point; understanding requires identifying the relevant action in a situation. A shop’s price label, a customer’s payment, an unspent balance and a loan repayment agreement illustrate different functions even when each uses Namibian dollars.

The functions can occur together. A sewing business quotes a repair at N$90, receives payment and retains the proceeds for next week’s cloth purchase. The quote uses a unit of account; payment uses a medium of exchange; retention uses a store of value. If the customer is permitted to pay N$90 next month, the agreement also uses money as a standard of deferred payment. These are roles of the same monetary unit, not four different forms of money. Cash and a transferable bank deposit may perform similar roles through different payment arrangements. A description should state what is happening to value rather than choose a function merely because a bank or a price appears somewhere in the story.

A useful way to classify an example is to identify its verb. Quoting describes measurement; paying describes exchange; retaining funds introduces time; promising a later monetary payment introduces an obligation. Read the entire situation because the same case can demonstrate several functions in sequence.

### Qualities support functions

Qualities are characteristics that make money useful in carrying out its functions. Durability concerns surviving use, portability concerns moving value, divisibility concerns smaller amounts, general acceptability concerns willingness to take payment, scarcity concerns controlled supply and homogeneity concerns uniform units of the same denomination. Each addresses a different practical obstacle. A bulky object might be durable but difficult to carry; a convenient token might still be unsuitable if sellers refuse it. No single quality is a complete explanation of why a monetary arrangement works.

Qualities and functions should be linked through a reason. Divisibility supports exchange by allowing a buyer to pay a precise small amount. Acceptability supports exchange by making recipients confident they can use the payment elsewhere. Durability helps physical money survive repeated transactions. Homogeneity means recipients do not need to negotiate a different value for each genuine unit of the same denomination. This differs from protection against counterfeiting, which concerns authenticity. A durable counterfeit object would not thereby become valid money. Likewise, physical durability does not prove that purchasing power remains unchanged over time. The properties of the object and the value of the balance are separate matters. The following explanations distinguish each quality through a practical contrast.

Two qualities may support the same function through different mechanisms. Portability helps a buyer reach a seller with usable value, while divisibility helps settle the exact amount. Explaining both requires separate reasons; calling each convenient without showing how misses the distinction.

### Forms and payment arrangements

Types of money distinguish forms in which monetary value is held. Cash is physical notes and coins. Bank deposits are balances recorded in bank accounts. Electronic means of payment include methods for giving instructions to transfer monetary value, such as a debit-card transaction or an electronic transfer. Some books call cards and transfers types of money, but the card only gives access to the balance. A device, card or instruction is not automatically an extra monetary asset in addition to the underlying balance.

Suppose a learner has N$120 in cash and N$180 in a transaction account. The stated balances total N$300. Possessing a debit card for the account does not raise that total to N$480. If N$50 is withdrawn from the account, the bank balance becomes N$130 and cash becomes N$170, before any fee. The total remains N$300: the form has changed, rather than new wealth appearing. If a fee is stated, subtract it separately. Payment choice can affect convenience, security, access and cost, so two balances with the same face value need not be equally convenient for every purchase. These practical differences explain why people may hold more than one form.

Payment records also differ from the balances they describe. A receipt showing N$60 confirms a stated transaction, but the receipt is not another N$60 held by the customer. Identify ownership of the funds after the transaction when reconciling totals.

Money is also classified into standard types. Commodity money is a good with its own value used as money, such as cattle or salt. Token or fiat money is notes and coins worth more as money than the material they are made of, accepted because the government declares them legal tender. Legal tender is money that must by law be accepted in payment; in Namibia both the Namibia dollar and the South African rand are legal tender. Bank money is deposits transferred by electronic transfer or debit order. Cheques appear in older textbook descriptions, but cheque payments have been phased out in Namibia. Plastic or electronic money (debit and credit cards, mobile transfers) moves bank balances.

### Medium of exchange

A medium of exchange is something accepted in payment for goods and services. This allows a seller to receive money instead of needing the exact product the buyer produces. A gardener can be paid in money and then purchase shoes from a different producer. The shoe seller does not need to want gardening services. Money separates the act of selling from the later choice of what to buy, reducing the matching difficulties of barter.

The relevant evidence is that payment passes from buyer to seller in return for a product. A price tag alone does not demonstrate this function, because the good has not yet been paid for. Nor is a bank balance simply sitting unspent best identified as a current exchange transaction. Imagine N$35 changing hands for a packet of seeds. In this example money acts as the medium through which the seeds are bought. If the seed seller later buys a bus ticket, a second transaction occurs. Payment methods can differ while the function remains: paying electronically can still use money as a medium of exchange. Acceptance remains necessary in the specific setting; a seller without electronic facilities may require another payment method. The function explains the role money plays, while the available facilities help explain which form can perform it locally.

The gardener does not need the final buyer’s product, because accepted money can be used in a later transaction with someone else. This is the specific obstacle money helps overcome; merely saying it makes trade easier leaves the matching problem unexplained.

### Unit of account

A unit of account is a common measure in which prices and values are expressed. Quoting prices in Namibian dollars allows a buyer to compare unlike products. At a market, a bag costs N$60 and a basket N$90. The basket’s price is N$30 higher, and it is one and a half times the bag’s price. A buyer can make that comparison without first exchanging the products against one another. Under barter, many pairs of goods would require their own relative terms of exchange.

This function concerns measurement, rather than the physical handing over of payment. A repair estimate written in N$ uses money as a unit of account even if the customer declines the service. A business can express the value of its tools in N$ without those tools becoming money. It can also compare costs and expected receipts using the same unit. A record of N$200 does not by itself show a store of value: it might be a price, a debt or an account balance. Read what the number represents. When a learner sees two listed prices, the comparison role points to unit of account. When the story says funds are kept for later use, the time element points towards store of value. These contrasting clues keep two frequently confused functions apart.

A business comparing two quotes of N$400 and N$520 can identify a N$120 difference before it buys anything. The common unit allows that comparison. It does not establish which product offers better quality, so price comparison alone is not a complete purchasing decision.

### Store of value

A store of value holds purchasing power for use in the future. A person who receives money today can keep some rather than spend it immediately. This permits separation between the time income is received and the time purchases are made. A craft worker may retain N$240 from this week’s sales to help buy materials next week. The unspent balance provides a way to carry spending power forward without storing the sold craft products themselves.

Money is not a perfect store of real value. If prices rise, an unchanged amount buys fewer goods and services. Suppose an illustrative basket costs N$100 initially and N$110 later. A N$110 balance could initially buy 1.1 baskets but later buys one basket. The nominal amount held is unchanged, yet purchasing power falls. Interest on a deposit can add to the monetary balance, but its ability to preserve purchasing power depends on the rate of price change as well as the interest received. Durability is also different: a note may remain physically intact while the goods it buys become more expensive. A clear explanation identifies value being held across time and then states the condition that affects how well that purchasing power is preserved.

Keeping value for later use also has an opportunity cost: funds kept available today are not used for a current purchase. That decision may still be sensible when a future expense matters more. The function describes the option to postpone spending rather than a claim that saving is preferable in every circumstance.

### Standard of deferred payment

A standard of deferred payment is an agreed unit in which debts or other future payments are stated and settled. Deferred means postponed to a later time. When a buyer agrees to pay N$600 in three months, money gives the parties a clear unit for the future obligation. This differs from immediate payment, where money acts as the medium of exchange at that moment. The debt agreement concerns the future settlement even before the payment occurs.

In a furniture workshop, a customer receives a desk and agrees to pay N$300 now and N$300 next month, with no interest or charges in this example. The initial payment uses money as a medium of exchange, and the agreement fixing the later N$300 uses it as a standard of deferred payment. If prices change before next month, the purchasing power of the later payment can differ from what was expected. A fixed nominal debt does not automatically mean a fixed real burden. The actual burden also depends on the borrower’s income. Do not infer that borrowing is beneficial just because general prices rise. The borrower still needs the resources to make the payment. Money provides the unit for stating the promise; it does not itself establish that the promise is affordable or that the creditor faces no risk.

A wage agreement can also specify a future monetary payment. The service and payment may occur at different times, but a stated monetary unit helps both sides understand the obligation. The existence of an agreed unit does not remove uncertainty about performance or settlement.

### Durability

Durability is the ability of money to withstand repeated use without quickly deteriorating. Physical notes and coins circulate through many transactions. Materials that tear, rot or lose their identifiable form easily would make payment difficult, requiring frequent replacement or causing disputes about whether damaged units retain value. Durability reduces these practical costs. The relevant comparison is about surviving handling and storage, rather than how large a payment can be made.

Consider a community experimenting with perishable fruit as a payment object. Fruit can spoil before the recipient spends it, so its physical condition makes it inconvenient to hold and pass on. A durable payment object avoids that particular problem. This example illustrates a quality, not a claim that modern money derives value merely from the material it contains. A durable metal object is not money simply because it survives use; it would still need acceptance and a monetary role. Equally, a note remaining in good physical condition says nothing certain about its purchasing power. If local prices rise, that intact note buys less. Link the quality to the practical mechanism: resistance to deterioration permits repeated use, helping payment function smoothly. Keep this physical explanation distinct from store of value, which concerns carrying purchasing power across time.

Durability should be tested against deterioration, not against changes in fashion or the price of goods. A monetary object can survive physical use even if a particular product becomes more expensive. Stating the relevant comparison keeps a physical property separate from a price movement.

### Portability

Portability is the ease of carrying or transferring monetary value for transactions. An object that requires a heavy load to make an ordinary purchase creates transport and handling costs. Money with substantial value relative to its weight reduces this difficulty. Buyers can move between sellers without carrying every product they produced. Transferring a bank balance can also move value without transporting physical notes, although access to the required payment facilities matters.

Imagine a seller who would need to carry several sacks of produce to barter for tools in another town. Receiving accepted money for the produce first can make the journey and purchase easier. The saving concerns the movement of value; it does not prove that the tools are cheaper or that transport is free. A person may still face the risk of losing cash or have difficulty using an electronic service where connectivity fails. These limitations explain why portability should be assessed in context. A small physical object can be portable yet unacceptable to sellers, while a large bank balance may be easy to transfer only when the user has functioning access. Distinguish the quality from divisibility: being easy to move does not necessarily mean being easy to split into the exact payment amount required.

A long-distance transfer can improve practical portability, but it may still require an account, connectivity and an accepted receiving method. Explain what the arrangement saves and what it requires. Avoid assuming that the absence of physical weight means the absence of transaction costs.

### Divisibility

Divisibility is the ability to use monetary value in smaller units without losing its proportionate value. A buyer needs to pay for purchases of different sizes. Denominations such as dollars and cents allow a precise amount to be stated and settled. Electronic balances can also record and transfer small amounts subject to the system’s rules. A divisible payment unit reduces the difficulty of agreeing how to exchange a valuable large item for a small purchase.

In an illustrative market transaction a shopper uses N$100 cash to pay N$37 and receives N$63 change. The combined value of payment and retained money remains N$100 before any other transaction. Change uses smaller denominations to transfer the appropriate value. Compare a large indivisible animal offered in barter for a small packet of seeds: dividing the animal might destroy much of the value the owner intended to preserve. The money example avoids that particular obstacle. Divisibility does not mean creating value by splitting it. Ten genuine N$10 notes total the same N$100 as one genuine N$100 note. A seller may still lack the right denominations to provide change at a particular moment. The quality makes precise payment possible in principle; practical availability of the required denominations affects a specific transaction.

Precision matters when discussing divisibility: smaller units preserve proportionate value rather than increasing it. Receiving change rearranges the denominations held by buyer and seller. It is not an extra reward paid to the buyer for choosing a larger note.

### General acceptability

General acceptability means people are broadly willing to accept money in payment. A recipient takes it partly because they expect others to accept it when they later make purchases. This shared confidence allows money to link transactions among people with different products and wants. If a payment object were accepted by one trader but rejected by almost everyone else, a seller would be less willing to receive it and might have to search for a suitable outlet.

A festival token accepted at two stalls may help exchange within that small setting, but it should not be assumed to have general acceptance across the whole town. The limited network changes how useful it is to someone who needs to buy products elsewhere. This comparison explains the quality without asserting that every payment form must be accepted in every transaction. Cash, cards and transfers have different practical arrangements; a seller might lack a card terminal or a buyer might lack an account. General acceptability concerns a broad willingness to receive the monetary unit. Availability of equipment and the rules for a particular method are separate practical considerations. Confidence also depends on authenticity: a recipient who believes a supposed note is counterfeit may refuse it. That illustrates a trust issue, rather than a definition of homogeneity.

The recipient’s expectation of later acceptance creates a connection between transactions. One person’s willingness to receive money is supported by the wider network of people who use it. The example explains a social property of exchange, rather than a physical characteristic of a note.

### Scarcity and controlled supply

Scarcity as a quality of money means its supply is limited and controlled rather than something anyone can reproduce without constraint. If people could manufacture accepted monetary units at no cost and in unlimited amounts, confidence in their value would be undermined. Controlled issuance helps maintain trust that monetary units are not being created arbitrarily by each user. It is one part of a functioning monetary system, alongside acceptance, the payment arrangements and the institutions involved.

This meaning should not be confused with making money so rare that exchange becomes difficult. The economy needs means of payment to support transactions. Nor does the word scarcity imply that a particular household must be poor or that every bank must lack funds. It concerns control over the supply of the monetary units, not a personal shortage alone. A rare collector’s item is not necessarily money: it may be scarce yet unsuitable for routine payment and not generally accepted as such. Similarly, ordinary paper being easy to obtain does not make an authorised banknote freely reproducible as valid money. The monetary status, authenticity and controlled issuance are the relevant distinctions. Explaining this quality requires the supply constraint and its connection to confidence, rather than merely saying money is valuable because people would like more of it.

Controlled scarcity and general acceptability complement one another. Limited supply without acceptance does not produce useful money, while widespread confidence can be damaged by uncontrolled reproduction. An explanation should connect the supply condition with confidence rather than state that rarity alone is enough.

### Homogeneity

Homogeneity means that units of money have a standardised appearance within a denomination, so people can readily recognise and accept them. Notes of the same denomination and series share a design, size and colour. Different approved series can circulate together; homogeneity does not mean every genuine note has exactly the same design. Their serial numbers or small differences from handling do not matter. This standardisation removes the need to assess each payment unit in the way a trader might examine the quality of different animals or crops offered in barter.

Homogeneity is about appearance within a denomination. A genuine N$20 note and a genuine N$100 note look different because they are different denominations; homogeneity does not say they are worth the same. It also differs from anti-counterfeit protection: security features help establish whether a note is genuine, while homogeneity means genuine notes have recognisable standard characteristics within a denomination. Consider two sacks of produce of different quality: a buyer may value them differently despite equal weight. Standardised notes and coins reduce that variation in the payment object.

### Cash

Cash consists of physical notes and coins used for payment. A buyer can transfer cash directly to a seller without instructing an account-to-account transfer at the time of purchase. This can be convenient where electronic facilities are unavailable. Small denominations also make cash useful for modest purchases, provided the right notes or coins are available. Cash represents a monetary balance held physically; it should not be added a second time merely because it has previously been withdrawn from a bank.

Suppose a stallholder starts with N$200 in cash, receives N$85 from sales and pays N$40 cash for supplies. The remaining cash is N$245. This describes movements in a physical money balance, not profit, because costs other than the stated cash payment may exist. A cash box can be lost or stolen, and counting or transporting money uses time. Electronic payment can avoid some handling but introduces different requirements and risks. Neither method is automatically best in every setting. Cash should also be distinguished from a valuable object such as jewellery: an object may be sold for cash without itself being generally used as money. The defining point here is notes and coins functioning as payment, while the evaluation concerns their practical suitability for a particular user and transaction.

A seller may prefer cash for one transaction and a transfer for another. Size, distance and available facilities change the practical balance. An explanation of cash should therefore start with its physical form and then use case evidence for any preference.

### Bank deposits

Bank deposits are balances held in accounts at banks. An account records the amount the customer holds under its terms. Some balances are transferable for payments, while access to others may depend on notice periods or withdrawal rules. A person paying through a transfer can move monetary value without handing physical cash to the seller. The record is not itself proof that the customer possesses the same amount of banknotes at home. Cash and a bank balance are different forms of holding monetary value.

For a repair shop, customers may transfer payments to a transaction account and the owner may use it to pay suppliers. Records can help track receipts and spending. Bank services may involve fees, and a loan is a separate obligation that must be repaid under its terms. Depositing a day’s takings changes where funds are held; it does not create an additional day’s sales. If N$500 moves from a cash box into the shop’s account with no charge, cash falls by N$500 while the account balance rises by N$500. The combined stated balance is unchanged. This simple reconciliation prevents double counting. Assessing whether to use an account also requires considering accessibility and the relevant terms, rather than assuming any account can serve every payment or saving purpose equally well.

A loan entering an account increases the recorded funds available but also creates a debt. The increase in the balance should not be treated as an increase in net wealth equal to the borrowed amount without recognising the obligation. Payment capacity and ownership of wealth are different questions.

### Electronic means of payment

Electronic means of payment are methods using electronic instructions to move monetary value. Examples include a debit-card transaction accessing a deposit balance and an electronic bank transfer. The account balance is the money held; the card, device or instruction is a way of arranging a payment. A credit-card purchase instead creates a borrowing obligation under the relevant terms. These distinctions matter because the presence of a card does not establish how much money the holder has or whether a payment is made from savings or credit.

Electronic arrangements can make distance payments convenient and provide records. They depend on access, functioning systems and careful authentication. A customer sending funds to the wrong account may encounter difficulty recovering them; convenience does not remove the need to check details. A seller considering a payment facility should weigh charges and reliability against customers’ needs. For a delivery business with distant customers, transfers can reduce the difficulty of carrying cash over long distances. At a temporary stall with unreliable connectivity, a backup method may be useful. This links money’s forms to portability and acceptability without treating them as identical concepts. When classifying a payment, name the underlying balance where known, then explain how the instruction moves it. Avoid describing a debit card, its linked bank balance and the transfer as three separate amounts of money.

An electronic confirmation should be checked against the actual transfer details and the relevant account record. A displayed message is evidence to examine, rather than an economic definition of money. The underlying balance and completed movement of funds determine what the transaction has changed.

## Financial institutions — 2.3.5

### Central banks and system functions

A central bank is an institution responsible for currency, monetary arrangements and banking-system functions such as acting as banker to government and banks. In Namibia the Bank of Namibia is the central bank. Its official [explanation of its functions](https://www.bon.com.na/About-Us/Our-Functions.aspx) describes responsibilities for monetary and financial stability, payment arrangements and services to government. These are system functions, rather than the ordinary task of opening household retail accounts.

Central banks commonly issue currency, maintain accounts for government and banks, manage reserves and support monetary and payment arrangements. Currency issuance does not mean the institution can remove resource scarcity by printing notes. Payments and credit still operate within a real economy with limited productive capacity. Support for a sound banking system benefits households and firms by helping exchange function reliably, but it is not a promise that every borrower will receive a loan or every investment will succeed. For a learner comparing institutions, ask whether the example concerns the monetary system and banking relationships or a retail service to a particular customer. A shop seeking an ordinary equipment loan would normally approach a commercial lender rather than assume a direct central-bank loan is available.

The Bank of Namibia’s main functions are: it issues Namibia dollar notes and coins; it is banker to the government, holding its accounts and advising it; it is banker to the commercial banks, holding their deposits and settling payments between them; it is lender of last resort, lending to a commercial bank that runs short of cash so that depositors do not lose confidence; it supervises and regulates banks so that they stay sound; it manages the country’s foreign-exchange reserves; and it carries out monetary policy. When household borrowing or inflation is rising too fast, it can raise the repo rate (the rate at which it lends to commercial banks). Commercial banks then raise their lending rates, borrowing becomes more expensive, and credit and spending slow down.

Currency provides a recognised common means of payment without barter. Government accounts handle public receipts and payments. Last-resort lending can meet a solvent bank’s temporary cash shortage; it does not promise to rescue every failing bank.

### Stock exchanges

A stock exchange is an organised market for trading shares and other financial securities. A share represents an ownership interest in a company. An investor buying an existing share obtains that interest from its previous holder; the payment normally goes to the seller, not to the company whose share is traded. Issuing new shares can instead raise funds for the company. Both activities relate to securities, but their immediate flow of finance differs. Do not claim that each transaction in existing shares directly finances a new machine for the issuing firm.

A market where investors can sell holdings can make owning shares more attractive, supporting the wider process of raising company finance. Prices also communicate information about buyers’ and sellers’ valuations, though they can fluctuate and do not prove that an investment is suitable. A shareholder may receive income or a capital gain, but may also receive less than expected or lose value. Small businesses may find a public share issue unsuitable or costly compared with other finance. In a processing company, the appropriate choice depends on its legal form, scale, financing need and owners’ willingness to share control. A stock exchange is therefore neither a savings account nor a source of assured returns. It provides a securities-trading arrangement whose usefulness and risks must be evaluated.

If a share is bought for N$50 and later sold for N$44, the investor receives N$6 less per share before fees. If instead it sells for N$58, the gain is N$8 before fees. Neither result follows merely from the existence of the exchange. Prices depend on what buyers and sellers are willing to trade at. A possible route to sell an asset can improve liquidity, but does not ensure that the price at the time of sale matches the investor’s needs. That distinction helps evaluate the market’s benefit without turning liquidity into a claim of safety.

Namibia’s exchange is the Namibia Securities Exchange (NSX), formerly called the Namibian Stock Exchange, in Windhoek. Its functions are: it provides a market for buying and selling existing (second-hand) shares, so investors can turn shares back into cash; it helps companies and the government raise new capital by issuing shares and bonds; it protects investors through listing rules and the information listed companies must publish; its share prices act as an indicator of business confidence; and it encourages saving and investment, including by foreign investors. A company lists its shares to raise large amounts of capital, to make its shares easier to sell (which attracts investors) and to raise its public profile.

### Commercial banks and customer services

A commercial bank provides deposit, payment and lending services to customers. Households may use accounts to receive income, make payments or save. Firms may use payment services, loans or an agreed overdraft. A loan provides borrowed funds with repayment obligations, while an overdraft permits borrowing through an account up to agreed terms. The bank considers risk, repayment capacity and its own lending conditions. An application, even one supported by a business plan, is not proof that finance has been approved.

A refrigeration repair firm may seek a loan for tools expected to be used over several years. It should compare repayments and charges with realistic cash receipts, rather than assume the bank’s willingness to lend proves the project profitable. A short seasonal cash gap may require a different arrangement. Deposit and payment services also matter independently of borrowing: a firm can need reliable ways to receive customer funds even when it has no expansion plan. Access, cost and reliability affect the benefit. This connects the institution to spending decisions in topic 4.3 and firms’ production in theme 5. Central banks oversee monetary and system functions, commercial banks serve customers’ banking needs, and stock exchanges organise securities trading. Clear distinctions allow the learner to choose the institution relevant to the stated problem.

Other services include current accounts for everyday payments and savings or fixed-deposit accounts that pay interest; debit orders and electronic transfers; buying and selling foreign currency for travellers and importers; safekeeping of documents and valuables; and financial advice to customers.
