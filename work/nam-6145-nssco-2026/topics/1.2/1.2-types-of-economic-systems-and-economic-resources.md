## Types of economic systems and economic resources — 1.2

### Resources and allocation are different questions

The factors of production describe the contributions that make production possible. Land supplies natural resources, labour supplies human effort, capital supplies produced productive assets and entrepreneurship organises the venture while bearing business risk. An invented vegetable grower near Otjiwarongo uses soil and water, workers, irrigation equipment and decisions about what to grow and how to market it. These categories answer a different question from whether the business is privately or publicly owned. A government-owned enterprise can use the same four factors as a privately owned producer. A factory may also be described by its manufacturing activity without that description identifying its owner. Classifying the contribution carefully prevents money, machines, natural resources and management effort from being treated as interchangeable. Several contributions can be supplied by the same person or organisation.

### Ask what, how and for whom

An economy has to coordinate what goods and services are produced, how resources are combined and who receives the resulting output. A market system gives prices and decisions by buyers and sellers the main coordinating role. If customers are willing and able to buy a product, the prospect of revenue can encourage suppliers to produce it. A planned system gives government decisions the main role in setting priorities and directing resources. A mixed system uses markets alongside public provision, ownership or regulation. These descriptions identify different organising arrangements; they do not prove that an outcome is good merely because it came through a market or a government decision. Start with the relevant problem, then ask whether the arrangement can solve it using the resources and information available.

### Follow a decision through the system

Consider an original example of additional transport services for an isolated community. Under market allocation, a private operator compares expected paying demand with its costs. A profitable route may attract an entrant, while a route with strong social need but little purchasing power may remain underserved. Under planned provision, the authority can prioritise access even when fares do not cover costs. It must still obtain vehicles, workers and funds, and decide which other services will receive fewer resources. A mixed arrangement could use private operation with a publicly supported service obligation. Calling the arrangement mixed is not enough: identify who decides the route, who pays, who operates the vehicles and how performance is checked. The important comparison is the ability of the actual arrangement to meet the stated access objective.

### Advantages need conditions

Competition can encourage a business to control costs and respond to customers, because dissatisfied buyers may choose another supplier. This incentive is weaker where customers lack alternatives or information. Market allocation can also leave benefits to third parties outside the buyer's and seller's calculations. Government action may improve access or take wider effects into account, but an authority can make mistakes if it lacks information or if performance is poorly monitored. A useful evaluation therefore compares arrangements against criteria such as efficiency, access, consumer choice and responsiveness. It explains why a benefit could occur and what could prevent it. Do not treat equal access as costless or assume that profit-seeking prevents a business from providing a useful service. The organisation of incentives and resources matters.

### Distinguish a supervisor from the entrepreneur

A supervisor employed for a salary contributes planning and coordination as part of labour. The entrepreneur's role concerns organising the business venture and bearing its business risk. Someone who starts a workshop may also repair products and supervise staff, so that individual supplies more than one type of contribution. Classify the role described in the particular situation rather than assuming that every person who gives instructions is the entrepreneur. Similarly, a payment into a bank account is not itself a new machine. It may provide finance for later productive investment, but the distinction between financing a resource and adding the productive resource remains important. Compare a deposit transfer with buying additional production equipment when testing this boundary.

### Build a judgement from the problem

The choice between arrangements depends on the economic problem and the available means of addressing it. Ask whether users can pay, whether alternative suppliers exist, whether costs and quality can be observed and how wider effects will be handled. A conclusion might favour market provision for a service with informed customers and active competition, but support a public role where essential access is otherwise missing. Explain the condition rather than labelling an entire system superior for every purpose. Topic 2.1 develops ownership forms; topic 3.2 develops market prices; topic 6.3 develops intervention. Here, preserve the distinction between the productive resources used and the system that coordinates their use. Financing and ownership can also differ: an authority might fund a privately operated service without owning the operator. Separate the payment arrangement from the decisions about production when identifying the system.

## Types of economic systems and economic resources — 1.2.1

### Land: the natural contribution

In economics, land comprises natural resources used in production. It includes more than the ground occupied by a building. Water, fish stocks, mineral deposits, forest resources and the productive qualities of a site can all contribute. In an original crop-growing example, the soil and water are land, whereas an installed pump is capital. The distinction is about origin and function: the pump is a produced asset that helps use the natural resource. The payment for using land is conventionally called rent. Do not infer that every natural resource can be obtained without a cost because nature originally supplied it. Access, extraction and competing uses can make it scarce and valuable. A business choosing one site over another compares qualities such as access to water, soil suitability and distance from its customers.

### Using natural resources responsibly

A shoreline can support fishing, tourism and conservation interests. Allocating it to one use may restrict another. The quantity of a natural resource is not the same as the amount that can be used sustainably each year. Better knowledge or equipment can make some resources easier to use, while depletion or damage can reduce what remains available. An invented campsite may benefit from scenery without owning the surrounding mountains. Its activity still depends on a natural contribution. When identifying land in a case, point to the natural resource rather than naming the business's cash account. Keep an improvement such as a constructed access road separate from the underlying site. These distinctions become useful when analysing how an activity combines natural resources with equipment and human effort, and when comparing current use with future availability.

### Labour: physical and mental effort

Labour is human physical and mental effort used in production. A mechanic diagnosing a fault, an office worker organising orders and a farm worker harvesting crops all supply labour. The contribution is not limited to manual work. Wages and salaries reward employed labour, while the terms of employment and the nature of the task can vary. Distinguish the quantity of labour available from its quality. The number of people and their working hours affect quantity. Skills, experience, education and health can affect how effectively that time is used. A trained worker may diagnose a fault more accurately, reducing the time and materials needed for reworking. This does not mean a change in training instantly improves every task; relevance, practice and the equipment available also matter.

### Labour quality and labour availability

Suppose an invented furniture workshop has four employees, each available for the same hours as last month. A relevant training programme may improve their ability to read plans without increasing the number of workers. This is a possible change in labour quality, rather than an automatic increase in the labour force. Conversely, extending opening hours may increase the labour time needed without making the employees more skilled. The two changes require different explanations. Health problems, absence or a mismatch between skills and tasks can restrict effective labour even when the headcount is unchanged. When a case describes a salaried supervisor directing staff, that person's mental effort is labour. Look for responsibility for organising and risking the venture before identifying a separate entrepreneurial contribution. Topic 4.2 develops the rewards and differences in earnings associated with labour.

### Capital: produced means of production

Capital means produced resources used to make other goods and services. Tools, machinery, delivery equipment and productive buildings are examples. A tailoring business can use sewing machines and a cutting table to make clothing; these assets provide productive services rather than being goods purchased for direct personal satisfaction. The same physical object can have a different economic role in another setting. A vehicle used to carry business deliveries is capital for that activity, while a household's vehicle used for personal journeys is a consumer durable. Explain the use in the case instead of classifying an item by its appearance alone. More capital equipment can help workers produce, but its usefulness depends on suitability, maintenance, skills and demand for the output. Buying an unsuitable machine can tie up resources without creating the expected gain.

### Capital goods and financial resources

Money can finance a purchase of capital equipment, but money is not the productive machine itself. In this productive sense, investment adds to the stock of capital goods. An invented mill purchasing an additional grain grinder makes productive investment. Moving existing money from one account to another is a financial transaction and does not by itself add a grinder. Financial arrangements can support later investment, so the distinction does not imply that banking is unimportant. It identifies which step has actually occurred. Capital goods can wear out or become outdated, meaning some investment replaces existing assets rather than increasing the net stock. When explaining an example, state whether the business is financing equipment, acquiring equipment or using equipment to produce output. Topic 2.3 explains financial institutions, while topic 5.2 explains how the combination of equipment and labour can affect productivity.

### Entrepreneurship: organising and bearing risk

Entrepreneurship organises productive resources and bears the risk of business decisions. An entrepreneur may recognise an opportunity, decide what service to offer, obtain equipment, arrange finance and combine the resources needed to operate. The venture can succeed or fail; its receipts and costs are uncertain when the commitment is made. Profit is conventionally associated with the entrepreneurial contribution, but a loss is possible. Organising a venture therefore does not imply that the owner receives a secure return. An original example is a person starting a mobile bicycle-repair service who commits funds to tools and decides where to operate before knowing how many customers will buy repairs. Those tools are capital, the person's repair work is labour, and the decision to organise and risk the venture is entrepreneurship.

### One person can supply several contributions

The four-factor classification separates roles; it does not require four different people. The owner of the mobile repair business may own its tools, perform the repairs and decide how the venture is run. A salaried workshop manager can also organise daily tasks without bearing the owner's business risk. Distinguish those roles using the facts provided. Risk-taking does not mean choosing a reckless project: gathering information and planning can reduce some uncertainty, while demand and other conditions can still change. The entrepreneur compares expected returns with the resources committed and the next-best alternative use. Good coordination may improve the combination of inputs, but its results depend on the venture's circumstances. Link back to opportunity cost in topic 1.1 rather than assuming that being an entrepreneur makes the chosen activity the best use of resources.

## Types of economic systems and economic resources — 1.2.2

### Market allocation through decisions and prices

A market economic system coordinates production mainly through prices, private ownership and decisions by buyers and sellers. Households make consumption and work choices; producers compare expected revenues with costs. A change in customers' willingness and ability to buy can alter the incentive to supply a product. In an original example, increased paying demand for bicycle repairs could encourage a workshop to extend its service or a rival to enter. The response depends on available workers, equipment and finance; the prospect of revenue does not instantly create those resources. Competition can give customers alternatives and encourage cost control, while private ownership allows owners to decide how their assets are used within the rules that apply. The model describes the main coordinating role of markets, not the absence of every legal or public institution.

### Purchasing power is not identical to need

A market responds to demand backed by purchasing power. A low-income community can have a strong need for a service but limited ability to pay enough to cover its costs. This can leave an access problem even where producers are behaving consistently with their financial incentives. Some wider costs or benefits may also fall on people who are not parties to the transaction. Competition, information and the ability to enter a market affect the outcome. When evaluating market allocation, state the relevant condition instead of assuming that private ownership guarantees low prices or good quality. Compare the incentives with the users' needs and the resources available. Topic 3.5 develops the difference between competitive and monopoly conditions, while topic 6.3 examines reasons for government involvement.

### Planned allocation through public priorities

A planned economic system gives government planning the main role in directing resource allocation and production targets. Public authorities decide priorities, arrange the use of productive resources and can influence prices or the distribution of output. A plan can give weight to goals such as access to essential services or development of an underserved area, including needs that would not generate enough paying demand for private provision. Scarcity still exists. Choosing more resources for one service can reduce the resources available elsewhere. Planning therefore requires a comparison of alternatives rather than merely a statement of desirable goals. In an original example, allocating additional vehicles to a remote service requires funding, workers and equipment that could have served another route. Public ownership does not remove the underlying resource trade-off.

### Information and incentives affect a plan

An authority needs reliable information about demand, costs and available resources. A fixed production target can become unsuitable when needs or supply conditions change. If information travels slowly or managers have little incentive to respond, shortages, unwanted output or avoidable costs may result. These are risks to explain, not proof that every public activity performs poorly. Monitoring, local knowledge and accountability can improve implementation. Evaluate whether the particular planning arrangement can obtain the needed information and respond to it. Compare its ability to deliver access or coordination with the resources used and alternatives forgone. A judgement about a transport route, for example, should consider service coverage, operating constraints and performance, rather than substituting a general slogan about government control.

### Mixed allocation combines arrangements

A mixed economic system combines private market decisions with government provision, ownership or regulation. Privately operated shops may coexist with public services, while rules influence how private businesses behave. The label does not mean that government and private activity must each account for half of production. The balance can vary across countries and industries. A mixed arrangement can retain incentives for customer responsiveness while using public action to address gaps or wider effects. For an invented town's waste collection, private operation might be combined with service standards and public funding for households unable to afford a full fee. Identify the actual responsibilities: who owns the equipment, chooses the service area, pays for it and checks the result. The mixture's design matters more than the label alone.

### Judge the combination rather than the name

A mixed arrangement can face problems from either weak market incentives or weak public implementation. A poorly designed contract may give an operator little reason to maintain quality; unclear rules can make accountability difficult. Conversely, a well-specified service obligation with credible monitoring may improve coverage. Compare efficiency, access and responsiveness in the circumstances described. Explain how each expected benefit could arise and a limitation that might prevent it. A supported conclusion identifies which combination is appropriate for the stated objective and resources. It need not claim that one system is best for every activity. This connects the classification of systems to the later study of government policies, while retaining the scarcity and opportunity-cost reasoning introduced in topic 1.1. A change in one policy does not establish that the whole economy has changed system; identify the coordinating arrangements across the activities being considered.
