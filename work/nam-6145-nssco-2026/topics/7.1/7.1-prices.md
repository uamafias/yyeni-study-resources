## Prices

### Measure a basket against its base

A retail price index summarises changes in the cost of a representative consumer basket compared with a base period. A basket contains specified quantities of goods and services. Holding quantities fixed in a simple example separates price changes from changes in what is purchased. The base index is set to 100; it is a reference number rather than a money amount.

Use an invented Namibian household basket costing N$500 in the base year, N$550 in Year 1 and N$577.50 in Year 2. The simple index is current basket cost / base basket cost × 100. Year 1 gives 550 / 500 × 100 = 110; Year 2 gives 577.50 / 500 × 100 = 115.5. An index of 115.5 means the same basket costs 15.5% more than in the base year.

The annual change from Year 1 is not 15.5%. The index rises 5.5 points from 110 to 115.5. Dividing 5.5 by the earlier 110 gives 0.05, equivalent to 5%. This distinguishes a change in index points from a percentage change. Similarly, the base-to-Year-1 rise is 10%, so the annual inflation rate slows from 10% to 5% even though the basket keeps becoming more expensive.

A representative basket is an average. A household buying more of the fastest-rising items can experience a larger increase in its own cost of living. Basket selection, quality changes and changing consumption patterns can affect the usefulness of the measure. In a simple calculation use the supplied comparable basket rather than introducing unprovided quantities or treating the index as a precise experience shared by every household.

Basket costs can be built from individual prices and quantities. An invented basket containing ten units of item A and five of item B costs N$200 if A is N$10 and B N$20. If A rises to N$12 while B stays N$20, the same quantities cost N$220, giving an index of 110. Simply averaging the two percentage price changes would give a different result because their contributions to expenditure need not be equal. Cost the specified quantities first.

### Distinguish the price trend from its cause

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. A single product becoming dearer does not establish that general pattern; other prices could be unchanged or falling. Demand-pull inflation begins with demand growing beyond what firms can readily supply. Buyers compete for limited available output, putting upward pressure on prices. The capacity condition matters: stronger demand can instead raise real output where resources are idle and supply responds.

Cost-push inflation begins with increased production costs, such as a broad rise in imported input prices. If firms pass those costs into selling prices, the general level can rise even without stronger consumer demand. The cost increase must spread sufficiently to support a general-price explanation; one workshop’s isolated bill is not the whole economy. The two causes can operate together, so identify the initiating change and the evidence rather than assuming a single cause.

Slower positive inflation is disinflation, not deflation. In the invented basket, prices still rise in Year 2 despite the slower rate. Deflation would mean a sustained decline in the general price level. These distinctions affect a household’s interpretation: a lower inflation announcement does not promise restoration of the previous year’s prices. Topic 6.2 considers policy instruments; this topic owns the measurement, causes and consequences.

### Consequences depend on incomes, contracts and expectations

If an invented pension stays N$3,000 while the pensioner’s basket prices rise 10%, the same money buys less. To buy the old bundle would require N$3,300. The purchasing-power loss follows from income failing to adjust, not from the pension’s money amount falling. A household whose income rises with relevant prices may avoid that particular loss, although its personal basket and timing still matter.

Inflation can affect savers and lenders where returns fail to compensate for higher prices. A fixed money balance can buy less later. A debtor with a fixed nominal sum can owe less in real purchasing-power terms after unexpected inflation. Yet the nominal debt remains due, and the debtor’s repayment burden relative to income improves only if income rises or other relevant conditions change. A person with fixed income and rising essentials can find repayment harder despite lower real debt value.

Uncertain inflation can complicate budgets, pricing and investment decisions. Businesses may struggle to distinguish a general price rise from demand for their own product. Competitiveness can weaken if domestic costs and export prices rise faster than competitors’ without offsetting productivity or exchange-rate changes. These are conditional effects, not proof that each firm’s profit falls.

Anticipated low stable inflation can be easier to plan for than an unexpected rapid rise. Contracts may adjust, and some workers may negotiate higher pay. The judgement should compare the affected groups and their ability to adapt. Fixed-income households face a strong direct purchasing-power risk; debtors can gain in real terms under a fixed nominal contract, but income, prices and financing conditions decide practical affordability. No group label alone settles the outcome.

Relative price changes can alter the effect further. A business selling a product whose price is rising may still face input costs increasing faster, squeezing its margin. Another can pass costs on more readily. Inflation is a general measure, so an individual profit conclusion needs both receipts and costs rather than the general rate alone.
