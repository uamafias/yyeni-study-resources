## Prices — 7.1

### Measuring a representative basket

A retail price index measures changes in the cost of a representative consumer basket relative to a base period. The basket gives a consistent set of goods and services for comparison. A base index of 100 is a reference, not a money amount or a claim that every product costs N$100. Prices can be combined using their importance in the basket rather than assuming a small purchase and a large household expense have equal weight. A fixed-quantity basket provides a simple way to incorporate those weights through quantities. The usual method gives each item a weight showing its share of household spending. Example: food has weight 6 and price index 110, transport weight 4 and index 120. The weighted index is (6 × 110 + 4 × 120) ÷ 10 = (660 + 480) ÷ 10 = 114, so the price level has risen 14% since the base year (index 100). Namibia’s official measure is the consumer price index published monthly by the Namibia Statistics Agency.

In a basket, four food packs cost N$25 each and two transport services N$50 each in the base period, giving N$200 total. In the later period the same quantities cost N$30 and N$60, giving N$240. The index is 240 divided by 200 times 100 = 120. The basket is 20 percent more expensive than in the base period. The quantities are held constant so the comparison measures prices rather than more consumption. These are example items and weights. A representative index can be useful while individual households experience different price changes because their spending patterns differ.

### Index levels and inflation rates

Inflation is a sustained (persistent) and appreciable increase in the general price level over time, which reduces the purchasing power (value) of money.

An index level and an inflation rate are different measures. Index 120 means a 20 percent increase relative to base 100. If the next index is 126, inflation from 120 to 126 is six divided by 120 times 100 = 5 percent, not 6 percent and not 26 percent. Six is the index-point rise, 5 percent the change relative to the preceding period, and 26 percent the cumulative change from base 100. State which comparison is requested before choosing a denominator.

A falling inflation rate does not necessarily mean prices fall. A move from 10 percent annual inflation to 5 percent means the general level is still rising, but more slowly. A falling general level is a different situation. Nor does one product’s price rise establish the general trend. A basket combines changes across items, some of which may fall while others rise. Keep the observation, measure and time period clear. A comparison of two index numbers can quantify the change, but it does not identify whether demand, cost or monetary conditions caused it. Those mechanisms require separate evidence from the case.

The base-period comparison can be checked by rebuilding the basket total from its components before dividing. If a component price is omitted, the index no longer describes the stated basket. If the quantity changes, the comparison mixes spending and prices. These checks protect the meaning of the result rather than merely repeat a percentage calculation. The learner should be able to explain why the basket costs differ and which features were deliberately kept constant.

### Consequences for purchasing power

Real income is income adjusted for the price level. An unchanged monetary income buys less when the relevant basket becomes more expensive. A household receiving N$5,000 with an index moving from 100 to 125 has later real income of N$5,000 divided by 125 times 100 = N$4,000 at base prices. The money amount is unchanged, while purchasing power is 20 percent below its original N$5,000 base-price value. A 25 percent price increase does not imply an exact 25 percent purchasing-power fall; the index ratio gives the correct relationship.

If money income also changes, deflate the new amount before comparing. Disposable income is different: taxes and deductions determine the funds available, while the price adjustment determines their buying power. Inflation may also reduce the real value of unchanged balances unless interest or other changes offset it. Do not assume a household’s own basket exactly matches the representative index. The supplied exercise defines the comparison basis. Explain the result as purchasing power for that basis rather than inventing an identical loss for each resident.

### Uneven effects and uncertainty

Inflation can affect groups differently. A borrower with a fixed nominal debt may benefit from an unexpected fall in its real burden, while a lender may receive repayment with less purchasing power. The actual effect depends on interest, income changes and whether inflation was expected when terms were agreed. The borrower still needs the cash to make payments; a smaller real debt is not proof of easier monthly affordability. Firms can face uncertain costs and selling conditions, making planning harder, while the effect on profit depends on both receipts and complete costs.

An explanation should therefore name the affected person, the contract or income condition and the mechanism. Avoid saying inflation helps all borrowers or harms all firms identically. The cause also matters to the policy response: demand growing beyond capacity differs from an input-cost shock even if both raise the general level. This links the measurement to the causes of inflation and economic policy in 6.2. A sustained trend, a one-off relative-price change and an individual experience should be kept distinct so the learner can interpret the data without supplying their own unsupported facts.

Consequences of inflation: people on fixed incomes, such as pensioners, lose purchasing power; savings lose value if interest is below inflation; borrowers on fixed nominal debts may gain while lenders lose if inflation exceeds what their interest terms allowed for; workers demand higher wages, which may cause strikes; uncertainty can reduce business investment; and if Namibian prices rise faster than other countries’, exports become less competitive and imports more attractive, worsening the balance of payments. Mild, steady inflation can have benefits: firms’ profits may rise, encouraging investment and employment, and the real burden of debts, including government debt, falls.

## Prices — 7.1.2

### Demand-pull inflation

Demand-pull inflation is sustained general price pressure when aggregate demand grows beyond the economy’s ability to expand real output sufficiently. Demand concerns total spending pressure, not just popularity of one shop’s product. If many buyers seek more output while capacity and inputs are limited, firms may be unable to meet the extra demand mainly by producing more. Prices can then respond strongly. Where resources are unused and available to the relevant activities, additional demand may instead support a larger real output response with less price pressure.

An economy experiencing broad growth in consumption, investment and public purchases near capacity illustrates the mechanism. The explanation should identify spending pressure and constrained supply rather than merely quote rising prices. A policy restraining demand may reduce that pressure but can also affect output and jobs, so evaluation considers unused resources and priorities. The presence of a capacity bottleneck does not mean every sector is equally constrained. Some workers or equipment can be unused while a particular input is scarce. State the relevant evidence before claiming an economy-wide result. Demand-pull is a causal account for the general trend, not a label assigned automatically to each individual price rise. To assess a supplied case, ask whether the information describes broad spending growth relative to feasible real production and whether the pressure persists, rather than an isolated surge in one product’s demand. A useful contrast holds supply response clear: extra spending when suitable capacity is available can increase output, whereas the same spending pressure with a binding bottleneck may produce more price adjustment. This is a conditional mechanism, not a precise forecast of the inflation rate from a spending figure alone.

### Cost-push inflation

Cost-push inflation is sustained general price pressure associated with rising production costs or adverse supply conditions. Higher important input costs can affect many firms, making the same output more costly to produce. Firms may seek higher prices, reduce output or accept lower margins, depending on demand and circumstances. An isolated cost increase at a small producer does not establish a sustained general rise. Explain how the shock is broad enough and transmitted through the economy rather than assume a single expense determines the whole price index.

Imported fuel or materials can be a source of pressure in a case. If a foreign-currency input price is unchanged but the domestic currency weakens against that currency, more domestic money is needed for the same purchase. That increases the importer’s domestic cost, rather than lowering it. How much reaches selling prices depends on competition, contracts and buyers’ responses. Similarly, a general disruption to essential supply can raise costs and constrain output, while a demand-only explanation would miss the initial cause. Policy should address the actual pressure where feasible, while recognising that using resources elsewhere has an opportunity cost. This mechanism can overlap with demand conditions: strong spending may make pass-through easier, while weak demand can limit it. The labels identify channels rather than mutually exclusive boxes into which every event must fit. Distinguish a change in the cost level from a sustained process. A single input shock may raise some prices once, while continuing shocks or wider adjustments can sustain the general trend. The supplied period and evidence determine what can be claimed. A single price rise is not on its own evidence of sustained inflation.

Common causes of cost-push inflation are wage rises greater than productivity (which can start a wage-price spiral as workers then demand more pay), higher prices of imported fuel and raw materials (imported inflation, worse when the Namibia dollar weakens), and higher indirect taxes. Inflation is also described by its speed: creeping inflation is mild (a few per cent a year), galloping inflation is rapid (double figures), and hyperinflation is extremely rapid, when money loses its value very quickly.

### Monetary inflation and linked mechanisms

Monetary inflation refers to price pressure associated with monetary expansion that supports nominal spending faster than real output can respond. The mechanism links money and credit conditions to spending and then to prices. It is not simply the existence of a larger money number. People may choose to hold more balances, spending behaviour may change and unused capacity may permit more real output. Those conditions affect whether expansion translates into immediate general price pressure.

In a case with expanding money and credit, rising broad spending and constrained production, the evidence can support a monetary contribution. The explanation should carry each stage rather than infer the result from the first fact alone. It can also overlap with demand-pull: the increased financing may support the demand pressure. Cost shocks can operate at the same time, so a careful answer identifies the information supporting each channel. A policy response depends on the actual cause, institutions and trade-offs rather than a slogan that money growth inevitably causes the same inflation everywhere. This links the type to monetary policy in 6.2 and banking in 2.3. For a comparison question, distinguish the initial change—spending pressure, cost/supply pressure or monetary conditions—while recognising how the channels interact. The shared outcome is a sustained rise in the general level, whose magnitude and consequences need further evidence. The three accounts should therefore be compared through the initial change and transmission mechanism. They are not three different measures of the price index. Measurement tells us what happened to the basket; causal analysis explains why, using separate supporting facts.
