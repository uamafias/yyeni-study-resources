## Government as producer and employer — 6.1

### Public operations and scarce resources

The public sector comprises activities owned or controlled by government. Government can act as a producer of goods and services and as an employer of labour. These roles are connected but not identical. Producing output means combining resources to provide something useful; employing people means directly hiring them to perform work. Public employees can provide the labour needed for a service, while buildings, equipment and organisation also matter. A government paying an independent contractor for a task does not thereby directly employ the contractor’s whole workforce.

An invented publicly operated community facility may hire staff to maintain grounds and provide an information service. The facility provides output and employs workers. Its contribution should be assessed through the useful service and resources used, rather than merely the number hired. A service offered without a user fee still requires labour, equipment or other scarce inputs. Its opportunity cost is the alternative use of those resources. Funding it collectively changes how payment is arranged, rather than making production costless.

A public good is a specific economic category: non-rival and non-excludable. One person’s use does not reduce availability to others, and excluding non-payers is difficult. Not every government-provided service has these properties. A crowded classroom or an individual treatment can use scarce capacity. Nor does zero price make a service a free good; a free good has no opportunity cost in the relevant circumstances. This distinction links to scarcity in 1.1 and government policy in 6.2. Classify by rivalry, exclusion and resource cost rather than by who pays or owns the provider alone. The funding arrangement and the economic properties of the output answer different questions and should be explained separately.

## Government as producer and employer — 6.1.1

### Government as producer

Government acts as producer when public operations combine resources to provide goods or services. The output may serve access, safety or other social objectives that are not captured by sales receipts alone. Producing a service still means using productive inputs: staff, equipment, premises and management must be available and organised. A financial loss does not by itself show a socially valuable service should cease, but a social aim does not remove the need to examine cost and effectiveness.

Consider an invented public information centre serving a remote community. It provides assistance that residents might otherwise have difficulty obtaining. The benefit concerns the actual service and access, not the centre’s government label. If funding is limited, spending on the centre has an opportunity cost in other possible services. Evaluate whether the output meets the intended need and whether its resources are used well. Paying no fee at the counter does not make staff time or equipment abundant. It also does not prove the output is a public good: access and capacity characteristics must be considered separately.

Provision, funding and production can differ. Government may fund a service produced by an independent supplier, or produce it through its own operations. Read the case before assigning the producer role. A public purchase shows demand for an output, whereas direct public production means the government operation itself combines the relevant inputs. This distinction makes later policy analysis clearer by identifying which activity and responsibility the evidence actually describes.

### Government as employer

Government acts as employer when it directly hires people for paid work in public operations. The employees provide labour services and receive remuneration under the relevant arrangements. Public work can involve many occupations, including administration, maintenance and specialised services. The ownership sector does not determine whether the job is manual, professional or clerical; those are separate occupational classifications.

Hiring staff can expand the ability to deliver a useful service when the right skills, equipment and organisation are available. If an invented public facility hires technicians but has no working equipment, employment numbers alone do not establish greater service output. Training, support and effective scheduling can affect how much satisfactory work is completed. Equally, an employee’s contribution cannot be judged from a government label without examining the task and resources. The economic comparison concerns the service benefit and the scarce resources needed.

A contractor’s staff and a public employee have different direct employers even when both work towards the same public project. This matters when describing the government as an employer rather than a purchaser. It also connects to earnings comparisons in 4.2: varied roles and conditions need a fair basis before comparing public and private rewards. Government employment can provide household income and support public output, while budget commitments and opportunity costs remain. A supported judgement identifies what work is needed and whether the arrangement delivers it effectively, rather than assuming either that public jobs are inherently unproductive or that more jobs necessarily mean a better service. A useful comparison also specifies the period. Hiring today can require induction before additional service is available, while an existing tool shortage may need attention sooner. Timing affects the feasible contribution of each option. Evidence should guide timing.
