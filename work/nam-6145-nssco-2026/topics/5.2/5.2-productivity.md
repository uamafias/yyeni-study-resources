## Productivity — 5.2

### Productivity is a ratio

Productivity is the output of goods or services per unit of input in a given period. Labour productivity is usually measured as total output divided by the number of workers, or by the number of labour hours worked. Production tells us how much output is created, while productivity relates it to the resources used. More output can be useful without proving more efficient use of an input. If a workshop raises weekly output from 100 to 120 items while increasing labour hours from 50 to 80, output rises but items per labour hour fall from two to 1.5. The denominator changes the interpretation. A comparison omitting input is incomplete.

Name both sides of the ratio. Items per worker per week and items per labour hour are different measures. A worker count can hide differences in hours, so labour hours can give a more suitable basis where shifts differ. Quality also matters: producing more defective items is not necessarily a gain in satisfactory output. If the data specify completed acceptable units, use those consistently. Productivity is not a synonym for the speed of an individual worker or a measure of personal worth. Equipment, materials, organisation and working conditions can all influence the output achieved from labour.

Choose the denominator that matches the question. Comparing teams with equal headcounts can be useful when their hours are also equal, while a change in shift length requires more detail. If one measure is items per worker per month and another items per hour, they cannot be ranked directly without conversion data. The input unit is part of the result, rather than a label added after the calculation. A number such as four without packs per labour hour would leave its meaning unclear.

### Measuring and interpreting

For a lunch-packing team, 480 satisfactory packs are produced in 120 labour hours. Labour productivity is 480 divided by 120, or four packs per labour hour. After a change, 540 packs are produced in the same hours, giving 4.5 per labour hour. The increase is 0.5 pack per hour, or 12.5 percent relative to the initial four. This figure describes output per labour hour; it is not a claim that total cost fell by exactly 12.5 percent or that profit rose by that amount.

Suppose instead the second output required 150 labour hours. Productivity would be 3.6 packs per hour, lower than the initial four despite greater production. This counterexample shows why total output is insufficient. An independent check multiplies the ratio by input: 3.6 packs per hour times 150 hours returns 540 packs. That confirms the arithmetic, while interpretation explains why a larger denominator changed the comparison. When drawing conclusions, hold the product and quality definition consistent and identify whether a change in staffing, equipment or organisation is also present. The ratio records an outcome; it does not by itself isolate which influence caused it.

Product mix can affect a count as well. A simple job and a complex repair may each count as one, despite requiring different resources. If the mix changes, a jobs-per-hour comparison may reflect the changed tasks. Use the quality and output definitions specified in the exercise and explain this limitation when relevant. The numerical examples deliberately hold satisfactory pack quality constant, allowing a clear comparison. A percentage ratio change then compares the new productivity with the old productivity, rather than dividing by total output.

Why productivity matters: higher labour productivity means each unit needs less labour, so the cost per unit (average cost) falls if wages are unchanged. At the same price this raises profit, which links productivity to the profit-maximising goal in 5.1. Alternatively the firm can cut prices and compete better, including against imports. Higher productivity can also support higher wages and, across the economy, higher output and economic growth. Comparing productivity over time or between firms therefore shows how efficiently resources are being used.

### Ways to improve productivity

A productivity improvement increases satisfactory output for a given input or reduces input for a given satisfactory output. Training can reduce mistakes and time spent repeating work. Suitable equipment can assist tasks that are difficult or slow manually. Maintenance can reduce breakdowns. Better layout and scheduling can reduce waiting or unnecessary movement. Motivation and clear communication may improve cooperation or retention. Each explanation needs a mechanism connecting the change to resources used per acceptable output, rather than a vague statement that workers become better.

At a frame workshop, moving frequently used tools nearer the work area can reduce searching time. If the saved time is used to complete more satisfactory frames with the same labour hours, measured labour productivity rises. Buying equipment without using it effectively would not establish that result. Training and reorganisation also use resources during implementation, so immediate output may be disrupted before later gains appear. Compare a suitable period and avoid assuming a possible gain is already achieved. Improvements can involve complementary changes: a new tool may need training and reliable materials to work well. A firm should identify the obstacle first, then choose a response suited to the task and check the actual ratio afterwards.

A maintenance example illustrates the difference between a mechanism and a result. Servicing a machine may reduce lost production time. If workers spend fewer hours waiting and complete more acceptable units in the same paid hours, labour productivity improves. If the time saved is offset by another bottleneck, the measured gain may be smaller or absent. Record what actually changes, including implementation time, before claiming the intervention achieved its aim. This makes the explanation testable using the relevant process and ratio data.

Other methods include incentive schemes such as piece rates and bonuses, better working conditions, specialisation and division of labour (topic 1.3), and improved management and organisation.

### Information technology and the work process

Information technology uses electronic systems to process, store or communicate information. Its productivity effect depends on what problem it solves. A delivery service using a shared booking record may reduce repeated data entry and conflicting instructions. Staff spend less time correcting mistakes, allowing more completed deliveries for the same administrative hours. A stock record may help a workshop identify missing inputs earlier, reducing idle time. The mechanism is better information flow, rather than the presence of a computer by itself.

There are costs and limits. Staff need skills, accurate input data and reliable access. A poorly designed system may create duplicate tasks rather than remove them. Downtime can interrupt operations and a mismatch between the software and the job can waste resources. The effect should therefore be evaluated using completed satisfactory work and the relevant inputs over a fair period. A shorter booking time does not prove total delivery productivity increased if the same bottleneck remains elsewhere. Do not count a technical feature as an economic gain without explaining its consequence. This links to factor substitution in 5.1: electronic systems can change tasks and skill requirements rather than simply remove all need for people.

Compare an intended improvement with an observed one. A system proposal may promise fewer errors, but adoption alone does not establish that promise was met. Evidence could include time spent correcting orders, completed satisfactory jobs and the input hours used across comparable periods. An economic evaluation also compares the resources spent on implementation with the resulting benefits, while keeping that financial question distinct from the narrower productivity ratio. Both can matter without being identical measures of success.

Examples: computers and accounting software process data faster and with fewer errors; email, the internet and cellphones speed up communication with suppliers and customers; computer-aided design and computer-controlled machines (robots) raise output per worker; online ordering and payment reduce paperwork. Drawbacks: the high cost of equipment and software, training costs, breakdowns and computer viruses, and some workers losing their jobs or needing retraining.

### Health, continuity and support

HIV and AIDS can affect productivity. HIV and AIDS are not interchangeable terms: HIV affects the immune system and AIDS describes an advanced stage of the infection. Effective treatment and care can support health, so a person’s HIV status alone does not establish low productivity or inability to work. The economic analysis concerns health-related disruption and the conditions that can reduce it, rather than assumptions about individuals.

Where illness causes absence or reduces the time someone can work, production can be interrupted. A firm may need cover, recruitment or training, and experienced knowledge can be difficult to replace. Caring responsibilities may also affect available hours. These mechanisms concern changes in labour availability and continuity. Lower total output is not enough to prove lower output per actual labour hour: the input may also have fallen. State which measure is being discussed and avoid silently switching between production and productivity.

Supportive arrangements, access to care and retaining trained staff can help maintain continuity. Their effects depend on actual circumstances and implementation; no treatment outcome or individual work capacity should be inferred from a group label. In a firm where employees can attend care appointments and tasks are covered through clear scheduling, fewer avoidable interruptions may protect output and skills. This connects to labour supply and skills in topic 4.2 and employment in 7.2. A complete explanation identifies the initial disruption, the input or process affected and the possible consequence, while recognising that prevention, treatment and workplace support can change the outcome.

Where HIV-related illness is untreated, it can reduce productivity through absenteeism (workers who are ill, attending a clinic, caring for sick relatives or attending funerals); the loss of skilled and experienced workers through illness or death; the cost of recruiting and training replacements; higher sick-leave and medical-aid costs; and lower morale. Output per worker falls and average costs rise, so profits fall. Firms can reduce these effects through awareness and prevention programmes, workplace wellness programmes and support for access to antiretroviral treatment, which allows people living with HIV to keep working productively. Judge each worker by the work done, not by HIV status.
