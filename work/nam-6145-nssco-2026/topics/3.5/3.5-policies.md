## Policies — 3.5

### Comparing price and output choices

Pricing and output policies concern the price a firm charges and the amount it chooses to offer. Market conditions affect its discretion. A firm facing many sellers of an identical product must consider buyers’ ability to obtain the same item elsewhere. In the perfect-competition model the individual firm takes the market price. A monopoly faces less direct rivalry within its defined market and can choose a price-output combination, but still faces demand and costs. A higher asking price is not proof that customers will buy the same quantity.

For consumers, competitive pressure can encourage lower costs, choice and attention to price or service. Monopoly power can weaken that pressure, allowing higher prices or less responsive service. However, a large sole supplier may spread costs over a large output or fund investment that smaller firms would find difficult. The consumer outcome depends on whether these potential savings or improvements are realised and passed on.

Pricing policies. Price competition means attracting buyers with a lower price, for example a price cut or a special offer. Non-price competition means attracting buyers without changing the price: advertising, branding, better quality, after-sales service, loyalty schemes, free delivery or convenient opening hours. A firm with market power may use price discrimination, charging different groups different prices for the same product. It charges a higher price to the group whose demand is price inelastic (for example business travellers who must travel on a set day) and a lower price to the group whose demand is price elastic (for example leisure travellers who can choose another day or seller). This can raise total revenue. It works only if the firm can keep the groups apart, so that buyers paying the lower price cannot resell to the others.

### A conditional judgement

A sole supplier’s large equipment may lower unit costs without justifying a higher price. Examine output, demand, quality and how savings are used. Several small sellers may face high costs, so their number does not prove prices must be low. This links to market forces (3.2), demand responsiveness (3.3) and economies of scale (5.4).

## Policies — 3.5.1

### Perfect competition and the individual firm

Perfect competition is a model with many small buyers and sellers, identical products, perfect knowledge of prices and freedom of entry and exit. The individual seller is too small to set the market price. It is described as a price taker because buyers can obtain the same product from alternative suppliers at the going price. This is a model with demanding assumptions, not a description automatically attached to a market just because several stalls exist. Different product quality, access or information can change the actual conditions.

A price taker still makes decisions. It chooses how much to offer given the market price, its resources and production costs. Taking price as given does not mean receiving revenue regardless of output or having no reason to control costs. If a seller of identical standard sacks asks more than the market price while buyers have the stated alternatives and information, customers can switch. Offering a lower price would also need justification because the seller could receive the going price under the model’s assumptions. The analysis concerns the individual seller: the market price can change when total demand or supply changes. Price taking by a small firm does not mean prices are fixed permanently across the whole market.

### Monopoly and the demand constraint

A monopoly has a single supplier in a defined market and barriers preventing effective entry. Defining the market matters: being the sole seller at one corner does not establish monopoly if buyers can readily obtain an equivalent service nearby. Barriers might arise from control over an essential resource or the expense of establishing a competing supply system. The relevant issue is the lack of effective entry and rivalry in that market, rather than the business being large or famous.

A monopolist has greater discretion over price, but demand constrains its options. If it chooses a high price, fewer customers may be willing and able to buy. If it wants to sell more, it may need to accept a lower price. It cannot assume an unlimited number of buyers at any price. Costs also matter: revenue from selling output must be considered alongside the resources used. High revenue is not proof of high profit. The key comparison is therefore price taker versus price maker.

For a sole local equipment-hire service, specialised machinery might allow useful capacity and cost savings, but customers could suffer if weak rivalry permits poor maintenance or high charges. Evaluate the outcome by service, affordability, cost efficiency and the alternatives available. A possible gain from scale is distinct from evidence that consumers receive it. In both market types, market power changes incentives and choices, while neither the label monopoly nor the label competition supplies a complete welfare judgement by itself. A market definition should specify the product and the area in which effective alternatives are available. A seller’s local prominence alone does not establish the absence of competition, while distant nominal alternatives may be difficult for customers to use.

A monopoly is a market with a single seller of a product that has no close substitutes. The monopolist is a price maker: it can set the price, but buyers then decide how much to buy, so it cannot set both the price and the quantity sold. It can restrict output to push the price up. Its position is protected by barriers to entry, such as patents or copyrights, government licences or legal rights, very high start-up costs, control of an essential raw material, and the economies of scale an established large firm already enjoys. In a perfectly competitive market there are no such barriers, so firms are price takers. If firms in perfect competition earn abnormal profit, new firms enter, supply rises and price falls until only normal profit remains; a monopoly’s barriers can let it keep abnormal profit in the long run.
