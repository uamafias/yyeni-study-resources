## Policies — 3.5

### Comparing price and output choices

Pricing and output policies concern the price a firm charges and the amount it chooses to offer. Market conditions affect its discretion. A firm facing many sellers of an identical product must consider buyers’ ability to obtain the same item elsewhere. In the perfect-competition model the individual firm takes the market price. A monopoly faces less direct rivalry within its defined market and can choose a price-output combination, but still faces demand and costs. A higher asking price is not proof that customers will buy the same quantity.

For consumers, competitive pressure can encourage lower costs, choice and attention to price or service. Monopoly power can weaken that pressure, allowing higher prices or less responsive service. However, a large sole supplier may spread costs over a large output or fund investment that smaller firms would find difficult. The consumer outcome depends on whether these potential savings or improvements are realised and passed on. Ownership size alone does not settle the judgement.

### A conditional judgement

Suppose an invented sole supplier uses large equipment that reduces unit costs but proposes a higher price. The cost-saving possibility is a case for large-scale production; it does not itself justify charging more. Examine service quality, output, demand and how the savings are used. Equally, several small sellers may face high costs, so counting them does not prove prices must be low. Compare incentives and evidence under the stated conditions. This links to market forces in topic 3.2, demand responsiveness in 3.3 and economies of scale in 5.4. These are simple economic models for explaining choices, rather than a claim that a real business perfectly matches their assumptions.

## Policies — 3.5.1

### Perfect competition and the individual firm

Perfect competition is a model with many small buyers and sellers, identical products, relevant information and freedom of entry and exit. The individual seller is too small to set the market price. It is described as a price taker because buyers can obtain the same product from alternative suppliers at the going price. This is a model with demanding assumptions, not a description automatically attached to a market just because several stalls exist. Different product quality, access or information can change the actual conditions.

A price taker still makes decisions. It chooses how much to offer given the market price, its resources and production costs. Taking price as given does not mean receiving revenue regardless of output or having no reason to control costs. If an invented seller of identical standard sacks asks more than the market price while buyers have the stated alternatives and information, customers can switch. Offering a lower price would also need justification because the seller could receive the going price under the model’s assumptions. The analysis concerns the individual seller: the market price can change when total demand or supply changes. Price taking by a small firm does not mean prices are fixed permanently across the whole market.

### Monopoly and the demand constraint

A monopoly has a single supplier in a defined market and barriers preventing effective entry. Defining the market matters: being the sole seller at one corner does not establish monopoly if buyers can readily obtain an equivalent service nearby. Barriers might arise from control over an essential resource or the expense of establishing a competing supply system. The relevant issue is the lack of effective entry and rivalry in that market, rather than the business being large or famous.

A monopolist has greater discretion over price, but demand constrains its options. If it chooses a high price, fewer customers may be willing and able to buy. If it wants to sell more, it may need to accept a lower price. It cannot assume an unlimited number of buyers at any price. Costs also matter: revenue from selling output must be considered alongside the resources used. High revenue is not proof of high profit. At this level the key comparison is therefore price taking versus constrained price-setting power, without introducing advanced optimisation rules.

For an invented sole local equipment-hire service, specialised machinery might allow useful capacity and cost savings, but customers could suffer if weak rivalry permits poor maintenance or high charges. Evaluate the outcome by service, affordability, cost efficiency and the alternatives available. A possible gain from scale is distinct from evidence that consumers receive it. This returns to the parent comparison: market power changes incentives and choices, while neither the label monopoly nor the label competition supplies a complete welfare judgement by itself. A market definition should specify the product and the area in which effective alternatives are available. A seller’s local prominence alone does not establish the absence of competition, while distant nominal alternatives may be difficult for customers to use.
