## Policies

### Compare models using their assumptions

Perfect competition and monopoly describe conditions affecting a firm’s pricing and output decisions. They are not descriptions of private versus government ownership. Compare the number of suppliers, the product, entry conditions and the firm’s relationship with demand. A statement about one feature does not settle every outcome. Cost, demand, quality and the objective used to judge performance remain important.

Use invented alternative teaching cases for the same basic product. One has many suppliers of an identical product, adequate information and free entry. The other has one supplier protected by an entry barrier. These assumptions isolate the market structures; they are not claims that a named Namibian industry exactly matches either model. An actual judgement would need evidence about the relevant market rather than assume that a large firm is automatically its only supplier.

### Perfect competition and the price-taking firm

Perfect competition is a model with many price-taking firms selling an identical product, with free entry and exit and adequate information. An individual firm is too small relative to the market to determine its price. Asking more than the market price for an identical product gives informed buyers an opportunity to purchase from other suppliers. The firm therefore takes the market price and chooses an appropriate output in relation to its costs.

At that price, an additional unit adds the price to total revenue, so the firm’s marginal revenue equals price in the model. Producing additional units is worthwhile for a profit objective while the extra revenue exceeds the extra cost. For an interior smooth profit maximum, marginal revenue equals marginal cost. Immediately below that output, extra revenue should exceed extra cost; immediately above it, extra cost should exceed extra revenue. This condition describes a maximum rather than any equality. Also compare feasible alternatives and check whether the firm is making a loss. With discrete units, compare total profit and whether the next unit adds more cost than revenue.

Abnormal profit means a return above economic costs including normal profit. Normal profit is the minimum reward needed to retain entrepreneurship in its current use, treated as part of economic cost. Where entry is genuinely free, abnormal profit can attract additional suppliers, expanding market supply and putting pressure on price and profit. This is a model mechanism, not a guarantee that every real firm immediately earns an identical return.

### Monopoly and the demand constraint

Monopoly is a market structure with a single supplier in the defined market and barriers that restrict entry. A monopolist faces the market demand relationship rather than the same price-taking position as a small competitive firm. If it wants to sell additional output along a downward-sloping demand curve, it generally needs a lower price. It cannot independently choose any high price and any high quantity without considering buyers’ response.

Marginal revenue is the extra revenue from another unit, distinct from average revenue, which is revenue per unit. When selling more requires lowering the uniform price on the output sold, the extra revenue can be less than the new price because receipts on earlier units are also affected. This helps explain why the monopolist considers both demand and costs when choosing output. Use the profit-maximising comparison of additional revenue and cost under the stated conditions, then read the price buyers will pay for that output from demand.

Monopoly does not guarantee profit. Demand can be weak or costs high, and a barrier to entry does not remove those constraints. A larger operation may obtain economies of scale, reducing long-run average cost. That potential efficiency gain must be distinguished from what consumers receive. A lower cost can increase the firm’s margin without necessarily leading to a lower selling price.

### Separate firm efficiency from consumer outcomes

Competitive pressure can limit an individual firm’s pricing power and encourage attention to cost. Against this, a collection of small firms may lack a particular scale advantage available to one larger supplier. Monopoly can therefore have a cost advantage in a suitable case, while restricted entry can weaken pressure to pass savings to buyers. Neither structure is established as best from its label alone.

Use invented figures: competitive suppliers have average cost N$9 and sell at N$10, while a monopolist’s scale advantage gives average cost N$6 but its price is N$12. The monopolist uses fewer stated cost resources per unit, yet consumers pay more in this comparison. The data do not support calling its lower cost a consumer price saving. If it instead passed sufficient savings through a lower price while maintaining the required quality, the consumer comparison could change. A justified conclusion identifies the beneficiary, the cost conditions and the actual price/output policy.
