## Business organisations

### Ownership and productive activity are different questions

Private-sector economic activity is owned and controlled by private individuals or organisations. Public-sector economic activity is owned or controlled by government. The distinction concerns ownership and control, rather than the physical product. A privately owned bakery and a government-owned bakery would both manufacture bread, but belong to different ownership sectors. A privately operated service is not public-sector activity merely because many members of the public buy it.

Primary, secondary and tertiary activity answer a different question. They describe extraction or harvesting, processing or manufacturing, and services. A government-owned manufacturer can therefore be public-sector and secondary-sector at the same time. A privately owned transport operator can be private-sector and tertiary-sector. These are two axes of classification, so choosing one does not replace the other. In an explanation, identify the evidence for ownership separately from the evidence for the activity performed.

Ownership can influence objectives and accountability. Private owners may emphasise profit, independence or other goals; a public enterprise can be directed towards service access or a wider public objective. These are possible priorities, not guarantees of performance. Private businesses can care about service quality, while public enterprises face resource and cost constraints. Classify the ownership from the supplied facts, then examine the actual objective rather than assuming that the sector label alone settles the question.

### Compare forms using consistent criteria

Business forms organise ownership, control and responsibility in different ways. Begin with who owns the enterprise and who makes its decisions. Then ask whether the business has a legal identity separate from its owners, how ownership finance can be obtained, and what happens if an owner leaves. These criteria help explain economic consequences without assuming that every business with a particular name has the same size or management quality.

Limited liability means that owners’ financial liability for business debts is limited by the legal form. It does not mean the enterprise cannot fail or that creditors face no risk. Owners can lose funds invested, and separate commitments such as a personal guarantee can create additional exposure. Distinguish the company’s obligation to pay a debt from the ordinary shareholder’s limited liability for it. Do not infer that a lender is certain to lend merely because an enterprise is incorporated.

The forms in this topic also classify different dimensions. Sole proprietorship, partnership and incorporation concern ownership/legal organisation. Multinational activity concerns operating across countries. A co-operative concerns member ownership and shared interests. A large company can also be multinational, so the labels are not necessarily mutually exclusive. Explain the relevant dimension in each case. Current registration procedures, statutory owner limits and tax rules require verified jurisdiction-specific information and are not assumptions to add to these conceptual comparisons.

### Sole proprietor: direct control and concentrated responsibility

A sole proprietor is an individual who owns and operates an unincorporated business. The owner makes decisions and receives the business’s residual result after costs. Direct control can make a small operation responsive: a repairer can change opening hours or the services offered without seeking agreement from other owners. The owner may employ workers; sole ownership does not imply doing every task alone.

Consider the invented Copper Acacia Repairs in Namibia, started by one person using personal savings and repair skills. Remaining a sole proprietor allows that person to preserve control and keep the organisation relatively straightforward. Against this, finance depends on sources available to that owner, such as savings, retained earnings or credit on acceptable terms. Company shares cannot be issued while the enterprise remains an unincorporated sole proprietorship.

There is no separate incorporated identity protecting the owner in the same way as ordinary limited-liability share ownership. Business obligations can therefore place the owner’s personal resources at risk: this is commonly called unlimited liability, with the applicable legal rules determining enforcement. This matters when debts and contractual commitments grow. The business can also depend heavily on the owner’s time, health and expertise. Evaluate those disadvantages in context: a modest local service with manageable commitments may suit direct ownership, while a large expansion may require finance and management capacity beyond one person’s resources.

### Partnership: combine resources and agree responsibilities

A partnership is a business owned by people who agree to operate it together and share its results. Combining partners can bring more funds, skills and contacts than one owner possesses. One person might understand equipment maintenance while another manages customer orders and records. The economic advantage comes from resources and expertise being combined, rather than from simply increasing the number of names attached to a business.

Shared ownership also creates a need for agreement. Partners should understand how decisions are made, how work and rewards are divided, and how a disagreement or departure is handled. Different priorities can slow a decision that a sole owner could make immediately. Sharing the result can also be a disadvantage for someone who would otherwise retain it alone, although a better-resourced business may generate a larger total result.

Avoid treating every arrangement between several people as an identical legal form. Liability and the effect of a partner leaving depend on the type of partnership and its governing rules. A comparison should use the legal and contractual facts supplied, rather than inventing a current national owner limit or promising automatic limited liability. In a school-level decision, the useful reasoning is that shared funds and expertise must be weighed against shared control, potential disagreement and the responsibilities accepted by the participants.

### Private company: separate identity and restricted public access to shares

A private company is an incorporated business whose shares are not offered freely to the general public. Shareholders own interests in the company, while the company has a legal identity separate from those individuals. This distinction allows the enterprise’s contracts and property to be associated with the company rather than simply with one owner. It does not mean shareholders stop caring about business risks or that management is automatically capable.

Ownership finance can be raised from suitable private shareholders on agreed terms. An existing owner may use this route to bring funds and expertise into an expanding enterprise while avoiding a general public share offer. The new investors ordinarily want rights and an expected return appropriate to their investment, so bringing them in can reduce the original owner’s share of control. Whether investors participate depends on their judgement of the proposal; the legal form does not guarantee finance.

For ordinary share ownership, limited liability restricts the shareholder’s exposure to company debts under the legal form, while the amount invested can still be lost. Additional undertakings, such as personal guarantees, must be considered separately. Incorporation also brings record-keeping and governance responsibilities. A private company can therefore suit an enterprise needing a continuing identity and additional ownership finance, provided the owners can manage those responsibilities and accept how control will be shared. The word private does not mean the business must be small or hidden from customers.

### Public limited company: public share offers are not state ownership

A public limited company is an incorporated company that can offer shares to the public; public in its name does not establish government ownership. The term concerns access to share ownership. A company of this form can belong to the private sector when private shareholders own and control it. It differs from a public corporation, whose defining connection is establishment or ownership by the state.

Access to a wider set of potential investors can help finance substantial equipment, premises or other expansion. Investors can contribute amounts without each needing to manage daily production. That possibility does not establish that every offer succeeds or that all shares are automatically traded on a stock exchange. Raising funds depends on the proposal, investor confidence and the arrangements through which shares are offered or transferred.

Wider ownership can separate the people providing ownership finance from managers making operational decisions. This creates a need for oversight, information and accountability. An original owner may lose the ability to decide alone, and administrative responsibilities can be substantial. These costs make a public share offer less attractive for a small enterprise whose financing needs can be met more simply. Judge the form against the scale of finance required and the value placed on control, rather than assuming that public in the title means a public service or an automatic improvement over a private company.

### Multinational business: control operations across countries

A multinational business controls production or operations in more than one country. Its defining feature is the international organisation of its operations, not merely the location of its customers. A Namibian workshop that exports a consignment to an overseas buyer has international sales, but that fact alone does not establish controlled operations abroad. A business controlling a workshop in one country and a distribution operation in another fits the multinational idea more closely.

Use an invented example: Sand Lark Footwear controls production in a hypothetical southern African country and a distribution subsidiary in another. It can coordinate activity across those locations, potentially obtaining access to markets, skills or inputs unavailable from a single site. Managing those operations requires communication and decisions across different working environments. Distance and unfamiliar conditions can make coordination more difficult, while changes in demand or trading conditions can affect several locations.

Multinational status is compatible with other business classifications. The business might be incorporated, have private shareholders and undertake manufacturing as well as services. Do not replace those descriptions with the multinational label as though they answer the same question. For this topic, identify where control of operations exists and distinguish that evidence from a shipment sent abroad. Wider effects of trade, exchange rates and government policy are developed in their own topics rather than assumed from the label.

### Co-operative: member ownership for shared interests

A co-operative is a business owned and controlled by members to serve their shared interests. The members might be producers seeking shared purchasing or marketing services, workers organising their enterprise, or consumers seeking access to goods. The common feature is the member relationship and shared purpose, rather than a claim that the enterprise has no costs or never needs a financial surplus.

Imagine the invented Kalahari Seed Members’ Co-operative. Small producers pool orders to obtain seeds and arrange a shared delivery. Combining demand may make delivery practical when separate individual orders would be costly to serve. Members receive a service connected with their needs and participate in control through the co-operative’s agreed arrangements. The precise decision rules should be stated rather than assumed from a universal legal formula.

Collective ownership can also create difficulties. Members may disagree about which service receives priority or how contributions and benefits are shared. Decision-making can be slow when interests differ, and the enterprise still needs suitable management and finance. The advantage of pooling resources must therefore be weighed against coordination and participation costs. A co-operative should not be confused with a charity simply because it has a shared purpose, nor with an ordinary privately controlled company merely because both may buy, sell and employ workers. Identify the owners, the control arrangements and the members’ economic interest.

### Close corporation: member interests and incorporated identity

A close corporation is an incorporated business owned by members, with a legal identity separate from them. The membership structure distinguishes it from the share-based descriptions used for companies in this topic. It can provide an organisation for an enterprise whose participants want a continuing business identity while remaining closely involved in its ownership and operation. The label does not establish that the business is informal or that it has no administrative duties.

Consider an invented repair enterprise whose founders hold member interests in a close corporation. The corporation undertakes business activity in its own identity, while the members organise their involvement according to its governing arrangements. Economic comparison can focus on shared control, contributions, continuity and management responsibilities. An incorporated structure is different from one individual simply trading in an unincorporated personal capacity.

Liability, registration and eligibility are governed by the relevant legal framework. Do not invent a current Namibian membership cap or describe the form as freedom from every debt risk. The separate identity and ordinary liability treatment do not prevent a member losing invested resources or accepting a separate personal undertaking. For an appropriate school-level answer, identify the member-based incorporated form and explain a consequence using facts actually supplied. A named statutory requirement would need current verification before being taught as a fact, rather than recalled from a different country’s rules.

### Public corporation: state connection and accountability

A public corporation is an enterprise established or owned by the state to undertake economic activity. Its public connection concerns the state, unlike the public share-offer meaning in public limited company. An invented publicly owned water enterprise illustrates the idea without requiring a claim about a real organisation. Its activity may include supplying a service, employing staff and using produced equipment, but the sector classification rests on ownership or establishment.

State ownership can support objectives such as access, coordination or continuity of an important service. This may matter where private paying demand would not cover the full cost of provision. However, providing the service still uses scarce labour, equipment and natural resources. Weak maintenance, poor management or unclear accountability can reduce performance, so ownership alone is not evidence that the public objective has been met.

The enterprise may charge users, receive public support or combine sources of income, depending on the arrangements stated. Charging a price does not by itself turn it into a private-sector organisation. Equally, private owners selling an essential service do not become government owners merely because the service is socially useful. Keep classification separate from evaluation: first establish the ownership facts, then examine costs, access and the quality of delivery. ### Formal activity, informality and misleading labels

Formal and informal activity should be distinguished using the registration, recording or regulatory criteria relevant to the example. Size alone is not a reliable test. A single-person repair enterprise can be registered and keep official records, while a larger activity may operate outside a stated recording arrangement. Private ownership answers a separate question: both formal and informal producers can be privately owned.

Informal is also not a synonym for illegal. The term can describe how activity is organised or recorded, while legality depends on the particular activity and applicable rules. Do not infer a legal conclusion from an informal label without the necessary facts. In an original teaching scenario, state what is registered or recorded and ask the learner to apply that criterion. This tests the boundary without inventing a general rule for current licensing law.

Similar care is needed with public. A public limited company can be privately owned; a public corporation is defined by its state connection. Customers being members of the public establish neither form. To distinguish them, check the ownership and the way participation in ownership is organised. A company’s public share offer and a government’s ownership of an enterprise are different pieces of evidence and should lead to different explanations.

### Choose a form using the firm’s actual constraints

The most appropriate form depends on the decision-maker’s aims and the resources the business needs. A sole proprietor values direct control and may have manageable finance requirements. A growing enterprise may need new ownership finance, specialist management and continuity beyond one individual. Incorporation can help organise those relationships, but introduces responsibilities and can require control to be shared. A recommendation should compare what the proposed form solves with what it costs the existing owners.

Use invented figures: Copper Acacia Repairs has N$30,000 available for expansion but estimates that suitable equipment and working resources require N$120,000. Its owner prefers control but is willing to negotiate with a private investor who may offer funds after assessing the proposal. A private company could accommodate new ownership finance and a separate identity. Against this, the original owner may surrender part of the decision-making power and must manage company responsibilities. The investor’s participation is a possibility, not an assured result.

The conclusion depends on whether suitable finance can be obtained on acceptable terms and whether the expected expansion benefits justify shared control and administration. If the business instead needed only a small affordable improvement and the owner placed overriding value on independence, remaining a sole proprietor could be preferable. This changed condition explains a different judgement; it is not inconsistent reasoning. Compare like objectives, distinguish legal form from management quality, and identify the evidence that determines the final choice.
