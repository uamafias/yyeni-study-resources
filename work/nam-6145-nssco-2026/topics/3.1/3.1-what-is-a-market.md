## What is a market? — 3.1

### Markets connect buyers and sellers

A market is an arrangement bringing buyers and sellers into contact to exchange a product or resource. Buyers seek something they want and sellers offer it on terms they are willing to consider. Contact allows information about the product, quantity and payment to pass between them.

A market is therefore broader than a building or a row of stalls. A vegetable stall is one familiar example, but a repair service booked remotely can also participate in a market. Markets can concern goods, services or resources such as labour. In each case identify what is being exchanged and who occupies each side. A person looking for paid work offers labour services, while an employer seeks those services. Saying that someone is a buyer in one transaction does not mean they cannot be a seller in another. The same household may buy groceries and supply labour.

A market’s role goes beyond bringing buyers and sellers together. The interaction of demand and supply in a market sets the price. Prices then act as signals: a rising price encourages producers to supply more and buyers to buy less, while a falling price does the reverse. In this way markets help decide what is produced, how and for whom, and allocate scarce resources (see topics 1.2 and 3.2).

### Physical and digital contact

Physical and digital describe ways of bringing buyers and sellers together. In a physical arrangement, customers can visit a location and discuss a product in person. In a digital arrangement, electronic communication can let customers view offers and place orders at a distance. These channels can overlap. A tailor might take measurements at a shop, show available fabrics through an online catalogue and confirm collection electronically. That combination does not create three separate products or require three separate sellers. It shows different stages of one trading arrangement.

To classify a case, inspect how contact and ordering occur rather than the payment method alone. A customer using a bank card at a physical stall has not thereby turned the stall into a digital marketplace. Conversely, an online order collected and paid for in cash still used digital contact to arrange the sale. Payment, communication and delivery are related parts of a transaction, but they answer different questions. A successful channel must make exchange workable for the relevant people: attractive pictures are insufficient if customers cannot obtain accurate information or receive the goods.

Markets can be classified in several ways. By area: local, national and international (world) markets. By what is traded: consumer goods markets, such as food and clothing; factor markets, such as the labour market where workers sell their labour; financial markets, such as the stock exchange for shares and the foreign-exchange market for currencies; and commodity markets for raw materials such as minerals or grain. By form: physical markets, such as an open market or a shop, and online markets. Markets can also be formal (registered and taxed) or informal, such as unregistered street trading, and wholesale (selling in bulk to retailers) or retail (selling to final consumers).

### A choice of access

A seller of handmade bags could reach local passers-by at a stall and distant customers through electronic orders. Direct inspection may matter when customers want to check stitching. Distance ordering may matter when travelling to the stall is difficult. Neither channel is automatically cheaper or better: travel, operating hours, access, delivery and confidence affect the comparison. This links to exchange in topic 2.3 and introduces the buyer and seller decisions analysed in market forces in topic 3.2.

Buying and selling online is called e-commerce. Its advantages are that it is open 24 hours a day, reaches customers far away, lets buyers compare prices easily, and can lower the seller’s costs because no shop is needed. Its disadvantages are that buyers cannot inspect goods before paying, delivery takes time and costs money, internet access is unreliable in some areas, and there is a risk of fraud.

## What is a market? — 3.1.2

### Physical markets

A physical market involves buyers and sellers meeting at a physical location to arrange exchange. The location may contain several traders or a single seller. The defining point is direct physical contact through the trading arrangement, rather than whether the place is indoors, formal or large. A furniture showroom can be a physical trading location, just as a small produce stall can. The product need not be paid for immediately to make the arrangement relevant: a buyer can inspect, compare and agree a later collection.

Direct inspection can reduce uncertainty about qualities such as size, finish or freshness. Face-to-face discussion may help a buyer describe a repair problem or ask questions. These benefits have costs and limits. Travelling requires time and possibly money. Trading may be limited to opening hours, and the seller reaches people able to attend. Seeing a product does not remove every uncertainty about its later performance. A sensible explanation links the case fact to the benefit: inspecting a chair can help a buyer judge its dimensions, while inspecting a sealed package may reveal much less about what is inside. Do not assume that a physical location assures quality or honesty.

### Digital markets

A digital market uses electronic communication to bring buyers and sellers into contact and arrange exchange. A customer may read a product description, compare offers and place an order without visiting the seller first. The product itself can still be physical. For a pottery business, digital orders might connect customers in another town with bowls made in its local workshop. Describing the contact as digital does not imply that the bowls travel electronically or that transport is unnecessary.

Distance contact can extend reach and reduce a buyer's need to travel before ordering. It depends on customers having suitable access and being able to judge the information supplied. Photographs may not show exact colour or scale, so dimensions and clear descriptions matter. Delivery, collection and payment must also be workable. A failed connection or unclear collection arrangement can prevent a potential contact from becoming a completed exchange. These practical requirements make comparison conditional. Digital contact may suit customers who already know the product, while first-time buyers may prefer direct inspection. Neither preference establishes that the other channel has no market role.

### Using both channels

A seller can combine digital enquiries with physical inspection or collection. The economic question is whether the arrangement connects the relevant buyers and sellers effectively. Compare what each group needs to know, what access it has and how exchange can be completed. This links the two market types back to the general definition: a building is one possible meeting point, while communication at a distance can provide another arrangement for contact. A contact channel can be useful even when a particular buyer chooses not to purchase. Availability for exchange and completion of a sale are different observations; the definition concerns the arrangement that makes trading possible.
