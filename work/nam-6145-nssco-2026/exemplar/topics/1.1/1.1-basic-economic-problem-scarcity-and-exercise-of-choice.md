## Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

### Scarcity is the starting point

Economics begins with the fact that available resources do not stretch far enough to satisfy the wants people would like to meet. Resources include land, labour, capital equipment and the time available to use them. A community may want better roads, more classrooms, sports facilities and health services, while the workers, materials and funds available cannot provide the full collection immediately. Choosing becomes necessary because these resources have alternative uses. Scarcity is therefore a relationship between resources and wants, rather than a statement that nothing is available. A wealthy household can face scarcity when several worthwhile plans compete for its limited time. A busy workshop can face it when a machine is fully booked even though customers are ready to pay for more output.

### Scarcity and a shortage are different

Imagine a shop with rice on every shelf. The shop has no immediate shortage of rice at its current price, but its customers still have limited incomes and competing wants. Spending more on food may leave less available for transport or clothing. The owner also has a limited building and staff time. Well-stocked shelves do not settle these allocation choices. In contrast, an unexpected delivery delay may create a temporary shortage of a particular item. Another delivery can remove that shortage without ending economic scarcity. To test the distinction, ask whether the difficulty is a temporary lack of the particular product or the continuing need to choose between uses of limited resources. Explaining the choice is more informative than simply saying that people are poor.

### Identify the opportunity cost

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative forgone when a decision is made. Start by listing the feasible options, then identify the chosen option and the most valuable rejected alternative. The chosen option is what the decision gains; the next-best rejected option identifies what it gives up. Suppose a learner can attend a Saturday art session or a football practice, but the times clash. If football is the next-best option and the learner chooses art, the opportunity cost is the valued football practice forgone. It is not the art session attended. If a third, less attractive activity was available, its value is not added to football. Those rejected options could not all have been taken in the same time slot.

### Compare feasible workshop choices

A small invented workshop has six available machine-hours. It could use them to make one bench or two shelves. If it chooses the bench and the two shelves are its next-best feasible alternative, the opportunity cost is the two shelves forgone. A plan requiring ten machine-hours is not an available alternative within this six-hour decision. When comparing monetary figures, use a consistent basis, such as net receipts after the relevant costs, rather than comparing sales revenue for one option with profit for another. When the benefit is non-monetary, describe it clearly instead of inventing a cash value. Time, enjoyment and a useful service can be valuable alternatives even when the choice involves no payment. A free training session can therefore have an opportunity cost in paid work or leisure forgone.

### Trace the trade-off

A clear explanation links the resource constraint to the rejected alternative. The workshop has a fixed amount of machine time for the afternoon. Making the bench uses that available time. The shelves cannot be produced during those hours, so their valued output is forgone. This chain shows why the sacrifice arises. Saying that the bench is expensive does not identify the alternative lost. The same reasoning can be used with a household budget, a school room or a worker’s study time. Restate the facts needed for the particular case so another reader can follow the decision without seeing an earlier example. If extra resources become available, reconsider which combinations are feasible rather than assuming that the original trade-off remains identical.

### Use a goal and a time horizon

Identifying opportunity cost helps a decision maker compare benefits consistently, but it does not choose the goal for that person. One workshop owner may need cash today, while another gives priority to training that could improve later work. A higher immediate return may be important when bills are due, whereas a future benefit may matter more when present finances are secure. Neither argument should treat an uncertain benefit as guaranteed. Consider the evidence for the expected improvement, the length of time before it arrives and whether a more valuable alternative must be sacrificed. A useful judgement states which goal matters most in the stated circumstances and what change in the facts would reverse the recommendation. Repeating that both options have advantages does not resolve the decision.

### A decision method you can reuse

For a new case, begin with the resource that is limited and the alternatives that genuinely compete for it. Name the option selected, then identify the next-best feasible alternative and its benefit. Compare like with like over a stated period. If the decision affects several people, explain whose priorities your conclusion follows. A school might value wider access to books while learners value additional sports opportunities; recognising different goals makes the trade-off clearer. End a judgement with a reason based on the case and a condition that could change the choice. Keep a definition, a worked application and a judgement distinct: each does a different job. In a short explanation, select the strongest relevant link instead of listing unrelated ideas.
