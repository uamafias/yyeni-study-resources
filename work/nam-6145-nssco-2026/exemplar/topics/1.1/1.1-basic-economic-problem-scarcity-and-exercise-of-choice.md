## Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

### Why choices arise

Scarcity exists because available resources cannot satisfy the wants competing for them. A school may want more library books, a shaded playground and additional computers. Its budget, available rooms and staff time restrict what can be provided during the year. The economic problem is therefore a problem of choosing between competing uses of resources. Having a positive bank balance does not remove it. A larger budget increases the feasible choices, but the school can still face further wants that its resources cannot meet. Scarcity describes this relationship between resources and wants, rather than simply describing poverty.

### A full shelf can still involve scarce resources

Imagine a bakery with bread left on its shelves at closing time. The unsold bread does not prove that resources are unlimited. The bakery used flour, workers’ time, equipment and electricity to make it. Those resources could have been used for other production. Some bread may be unwanted at the price charged even though the resources used in producing it have alternative uses. To test whether the economic problem remains, ask whether competing uses still exist for the available resources. Do not use the number of finished products on display as the test of scarcity.

### The next-best alternative

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best feasible alternative forgone. Start by identifying the decision-maker and what has actually been chosen. Next identify the best available alternative that the decision rules out. Other rejected alternatives are not added together. A learner deciding between an hour of revision, an hour of football and an hour of music practice gives up the most valued alternative to the selected activity. If revision is selected and football is the next-best option, the opportunity cost is the benefit of that football session. Money need not change hands for an opportunity cost to exist. In an invented Windhoek garden, fixed resources can produce weekly combinations of bean and tomato crates (0,12), (2,8), (4,4) and (6,0). Plot beans horizontally and tomatoes vertically, both in crates per week, and connect the four efficient combinations. Moving from (2,8) to (4,4) gives two more bean crates but sacrifices four tomato crates: two tomato crates per extra bean crate. This is a movement along a straight production possibility frontier, with constant opportunity cost, given resources and technology.

### A community room

Use these invented teaching data. The fictional Otjomuise community group in Windhoek has a N$600 room budget and one room available on Saturday afternoon. It must choose between a reading club and an art workshop, which cannot run together. The group ranks the reading club first and the art workshop second. Choosing the reading club means giving up the benefits of the art workshop. Those workshop benefits are the opportunity cost. The room hire bill is a separate money payment and is not automatically the value of the alternative activity. If another room becomes available without displacing any other activity, the feasible options change, so the original opportunity-cost comparison must be reconsidered.

### How recognising the sacrifice helps

Consider an invented neighbourhood committee choosing how to use a plot of land. It prefers a vegetable garden, while a shaded meeting area is the next-best feasible use. Recognising the forgone meeting space forces the committee to examine who gains and who loses from the garden decision. Food production may benefit participating households, but residents who need a meeting place lose that particular use of the plot. A committee that states both benefits can explain its priority more clearly. Recognising opportunity cost improves the comparison; it does not by itself decide which benefit should receive the greater weight.

### A decision needs a stated objective

Suppose a learning centre can use a small room for either an additional reading group or a computer-skills group. Staff time allows one group this term. Its stated priority is to support learners who struggle with reading. There are applicants for both groups. The case for the reading group is that it serves the chosen priority directly. The case against it is the computer learning forgone by the other applicants. The reading choice is defensible if the reading need is more urgent under the centre’s stated objective. It could be reconsidered if a new volunteer can run the computer group at another time without reducing the reading support.

### Feasibility, value and new information

A rejected option must be feasible before it can be the next-best alternative. A family cannot give up a holiday that its budget could not finance in the first place. Its relevant alternatives may instead be repairing a bicycle, buying school equipment or retaining money for a later purchase. The family needs to state which of those feasible alternatives it values most. New information may change that ranking. If the bicycle is essential for travelling to school, repairing it may become more urgent. A sound explanation identifies the changed condition and then shows why the preferred choice changes.

### Keeping the comparison fair

Compare alternatives over the same period and using the same available resources. A weekly activity should not be compared with an annual benefit without explaining the difference in time. Likewise, an option requiring extra staff is not directly comparable with one that fits the existing staff allocation. State the constraint before judging the sacrifice. This helps separate a genuine change in preferences from a change in what the decision-maker can actually achieve.

### Where the idea leads

Topic 6.2 applies choices to government policy, while topic 9.1 examines conflicts between different interests and different time horizons. The opportunity-cost method remains the same: identify the selected use, identify the next-best feasible alternative, and explain the benefit forgone. Later topics add detail about the people affected and the mechanisms connecting the decision to its consequences. Keeping that basic comparison clear makes those later explanations more precise.
