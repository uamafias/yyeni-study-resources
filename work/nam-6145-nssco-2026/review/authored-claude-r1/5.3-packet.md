# 5.3 Production costs and revenue

## Notes

### B-6145-5.3-001 Set a period, output and cost boundary
Costs describe resources used in production, while revenue describes receipts from sales. Begin with a specified period and a consistent output unit. A weekly total cannot be divided by annual output to obtain a meaningful weekly unit cost. Likewise, a cost stated only for materials is not the full cost of production. The terms total and average indicate different measures; fixed and variable classify costs by their response to output in the relevant period.

Use an invented weekly example at Red Valley Soap Works. Existing capacity can produce up to 300 comparable bars. Weekly fixed cost is N$600. Variable cost is N$4 per bar over the supplied range. At 100 bars, total variable cost is N$400 and total cost N$1,000. At 200 bars, total variable cost is N$800 and total cost N$1,400. These values use the same capacity and period. Their increase does not mean every category of cost has increased.

The key formulas are total cost = fixed cost + variable cost, and average cost = total cost / output. The first combines cost categories; the second converts a total into a per-unit measure. At 100 bars average cost is N$10 per bar; at 200 it is N$7. Total cost rises by N$400 while average cost falls by N$3 per bar. Those statements are compatible because the total is spread over twice as much output. Keep the units visible when comparing them.

To organise the schedule, record each output alongside fixed cost, total variable cost and total cost. Then divide each total by that row’s output when a unit measure is needed. Check the addition before division: if fixed and variable costs do not sum to total cost, a later correct division still produces the wrong economic result. At positive output, average fixed and average variable cost should likewise sum to average total cost.

### B-6145-5.3-002 Total cost includes both categories
Total cost is the full cost of producing a specified output in a period. It adds total fixed and total variable costs rather than selecting whichever one seems most relevant to a decision. A soap producer using rented premises and materials incurs both. If the premises charge remains due even with no production, it remains part of the weekly total cost under the stated arrangement.

At 100 bars, the invented N$600 fixed cost plus N$400 variable cost gives total cost N$1,000. At zero bars, variable cost is zero in this particular schedule, but fixed cost remains N$600, so total cost is N$600. The absence of production does not make every payment disappear. Do not assume that a zero-output firm has no costs or that a cost stops being fixed because it is inconvenient.

Total cost should be compared with total revenue when finding total profit. Subtracting average cost from total revenue mixes money per bar with money per week. If the case gives a unit cost, multiply it by the relevant output before using it as a total, ensuring that the figure really covers all relevant costs. A partial materials total alone cannot establish profit. Similarly, the word total needs a defined boundary: a daily total of materials is still not a weekly total of all production costs.

### B-6145-5.3-003 Average cost measures each output unit’s share
Average cost is total cost per unit of output. Divide the full cost for the period by the number of comparable units produced in that period. At 200 soap bars, N$1,400 divided by 200 gives N$7 per bar. This is a calculated average; it does not mean that each physical bar required a separate N$7 payment or that the next bar will add exactly N$7 to cost.

Average cost can also be separated into average fixed and average variable cost. With weekly fixed cost N$600 at 200 bars, average fixed cost is N$3. Variable cost N$800 divided by 200 gives N$4. The sum is N$7. This breakdown explains the fall from N$10 at 100 bars: the fixed component falls from N$6 to N$3, while the supplied variable cost per bar remains N$4.

At zero output, average cost is undefined because division by zero is not possible. Report the known total cost without assigning a zero average cost. The period and output measure still matter at positive output: different bar sizes or quality grades may not be directly comparable. A low measured cost per bar is not necessarily evidence of better performance if the bars have become smaller or defective. Use the comparable units stated in the example.

### B-6145-5.3-004 Fixed means independent of current output within a range
A fixed cost does not change as output varies over the relevant short-run range and period. An agreed premises charge of N$600 a week can remain due whether the workshop makes 0, 100 or 200 bars. The classification applies to the stated arrangement rather than fixing the payment for all future periods. A new rental agreement, larger premises or a different period can change the amount; those changes lie outside the unchanged arrangement being analysed.

Distinguish total fixed cost from fixed cost per unit. N$600 spread across 100 bars contributes N$6 to each bar’s average cost. Spread across 200 bars, it contributes N$3. The fixed total remains N$600. Saying that fixed cost falls as output expands is ambiguous unless the statement specifies the per-unit component. Within existing capacity, falling average fixed cost can help reduce average total cost, but a rise in another component may offset it.

Capacity matters. If producing 400 bars requires renting an additional room, the previous fixed-cost assumption cannot simply be carried beyond 300-bar capacity. Use the new stated cost when comparing that option. In the long run a firm can change its scale and inputs more extensively; topic 5.4 owns the resulting economies and diseconomies of scale. Spreading an existing weekly premises cost across more bars is the narrower short-run mechanism in this example.

### B-6145-5.3-005 Variable cost and unit rates
A variable cost changes with output. Materials used for additional soap bars are a straightforward example. In the invented simple schedule, variable cost is N$4 per bar, so 100 bars use N$400 and 200 use N$800. The constant unit amount is an assumption of this schedule, not part of the definition of a variable cost.

In another case, extra output might require overtime at a higher rate or lead to more waste. Variable cost would then rise more rapidly at high output. Alternatively, more efficient use of materials could reduce the variable amount per unit over a range. Read the supplied conditions before multiplying all output by one rate. A rising total variable cost does not establish that its average rises: if output grows in the same proportion, average variable cost is unchanged.

Cost categories depend on the payment arrangement and period. A guaranteed weekly salary can be fixed over a stated short-run range, while piece-rate payments may vary directly with units made. Electricity may include a standing charge and a usage charge, giving fixed and variable components. Classify the supplied payment component using its stated terms. This is why a useful example states how the payment responds to output.

### B-6145-5.3-006 Revenue uses sales rather than unsold production
Total revenue is sales receipts over a period. Average revenue is revenue per unit sold, calculated as total revenue divided by sales quantity. Production and sales need not be equal: unsold goods may remain in stock. Where a workshop makes 120 bars but sells 100 for N$8 each, total revenue is N$800 and average revenue N$8 per bar sold. Dividing by 120 would answer a different ratio and would not give average revenue per unit sold.

Average revenue also differs from marginal revenue. Marginal revenue measures the additional revenue from selling an extra unit. Suppose total revenue at 100 units sold is N$800 and at 101 units is N$806. Marginal revenue for that extra unit is N$6. Average revenue at 101 is N$806 divided by 101, approximately N$7.98 per unit sold. The difference between totals is not the same operation as dividing one total by quantity.

When every unit sells at an unchanged uniform price, average and marginal revenue can coincide with that price. This special case does not make the definitions equivalent. A change in the price needed to sell more can make additional revenue different from the average. Keep marginal revenue limited to this distinction and the profit-maximisation reasoning in topic 5.1. Revenue is also distinct from profit: costs must be subtracted before the sales receipts can be interpreted as an excess available to the firm.

### B-6145-5.3-007 Total revenue: money from sales
Total revenue is the money received from selling output in a period. At one uniform price, multiply that price by units sold. Selling 200 bars at N$8 gives N$1,600. If only 150 of the produced bars sell, revenue at that price is N$1,200. The remaining production has not generated sales receipts in this example merely because resources were used to make it.

With different selling prices, add the revenue from each group. An invented sale of 100 bars at N$8 and 50 at N$6 gives N$800 plus N$300, or N$1,100. Multiplying all 150 by the higher price would overstate receipts. State sales volumes and prices clearly before applying a shortcut formula.

A larger sales quantity does not establish a larger total revenue when the price also changes. Compare 100 units at N$10, giving N$1,000, with 150 at N$6, giving N$900. More units are sold but receipts fall. Topic 3.3 explains the price-elasticity link to revenue. The immediate requirement here is to use both the sales quantity and price information instead of treating output growth as an automatic increase in receipts.

### B-6145-5.3-008 Average revenue: receipts per unit sold
Average revenue is total revenue divided by quantity sold. At a uniform N$8 selling price, N$1,600 from 200 bars gives N$8 per bar. This equals the price because each sale contributes that same amount. The equality is conditional on uniform pricing; the definition remains total sales revenue per unit sold.

For the mixed-price example, N$1,100 from 150 bars gives average revenue of approximately N$7.33 per bar sold. It lies between N$6 and N$8 because the units are split between those prices. A simple unweighted mean of the two prices, N$7, would be incorrect because twice as many units sell at N$8 as at N$6. Sales-weighted averaging follows directly from dividing the combined receipts by the combined sales quantity.

Average revenue cannot be calculated at zero sales by dividing zero by zero. Report that no average-revenue value is established. Nor should average revenue be called average profit: if average cost is N$7 and average revenue N$8 for the same quantity produced and sold, the N$1 difference is unit profit under that matched condition. N$8 itself still describes receipts before cost. Keeping the measures distinct prevents an apparent price advantage being confused with the final profitability of production.

For 200 bars both produced and sold, that N$1 unit profit corresponds to N$200 total profit. Multiplying the matched unit difference by 200 gives the same answer as subtracting N$1,400 total cost from N$1,600 total revenue. This provides a consistency check under the stated equal production-and-sales condition. If inventories change, do not silently use different production and sales quantities in a unit shortcut. Follow the particular costs and revenue recognised in the supplied period, and explain the boundary where needed instead of assuming all production earns receipts immediately.

### B-6145-5.3-009 Changing output can move totals and averages differently
Within existing capacity, producing more generally requires more variable inputs. Total cost can rise while average cost falls because the fixed component is spread over more units. In the invented schedule, increasing from 100 to 200 bars raises total cost N$1,000 to N$1,400 but lowers average cost N$10 to N$7. The fixed-cost spreading condition explains the direction; it is not a promise that average cost falls at every output.

Beyond some point, adding more of a variable factor to fixed equipment can produce progressively smaller additions to output. This is diminishing returns. Crowding workers around a fixed-size mixer may reduce each extra worker’s contribution. More input may then be needed for each extra bar, tending to raise additional production cost. This short-run condition differs from changing the scale of all inputs over the long run. Do not label every rise in unit cost a diseconomy of scale.

Revenue depends on sales. If all bars sell at N$8, total revenue at 100 and 200 bars is N$800 and N$1,600. Comparing with the schedule’s total costs gives a loss of N$200 at 100 and profit N$200 at 200. The higher output helps under these particular demand and cost conditions. If the extra bars cannot be sold, production may add cost without the expected receipts. If a price cut is required, recalculate revenue rather than retaining the original price for every unit.

Profit is total revenue minus total cost. A revenue increase can coincide with a profit fall if costs rise more. Conversely, falling receipts can coexist with higher profit if relevant costs fall sufficiently. Work with matched totals for the same period and state the sales assumption. These calculations concern a firm’s operations; topic 7.3 covers the different meaning and measurement of national output.

### B-6145-5.3-010 Technology changes the cost structure
Technology can change capacity, quality and the factor combination. New equipment may increase fixed commitments while reducing material waste or labour time per unit. Whether total cost falls then depends on output. A lower variable cost is not sufficient evidence that the technology is cheaper overall.

Compare invented same-quality weekly methods. The existing method has N$600 fixed cost and N$4 variable cost per bar. A new method has N$1,000 fixed cost and N$2 variable cost per bar; these figures include all relevant costs for the comparison. At 100 bars, existing total cost is N$1,000 and new total cost N$1,200. The N$200 variable saving is smaller than the N$400 added fixed commitment. At 300 bars, existing total cost is N$1,800 and new total cost N$1,600. The N$600 variable saving now exceeds that N$400 commitment.

If all 300 bars sell at the same N$8 price, revenue under either method is N$2,400. Profit is N$600 with the existing method and N$800 with the new one. At 100 bars sold at N$8, both lose money, with losses N$200 and N$400 respectively. New technology is preferable at the stated high output for profit comparison, but less attractive at low output. Sales and feasibility still matter: a cheaper cost at an unsaleable output does not justify producing it.

Training, reliability and maintenance affect whether these costs and quality can be achieved. Technology may also improve product quality and demand, but a numerical revenue gain needs evidence rather than assumption. Evaluate the changed cost structure alongside the output that can realistically be sold. This preserves the distinction between a potential technical improvement and a demonstrated economic advantage.

At 200 bars the two methods each cost N$1,400: the N$400 variable saving exactly matches the added fixed commitment. This equality identifies the cost-comparison boundary in this particular schedule. It is not a claim of zero profit, since that depends separately on revenue. At the stated N$8 price, each method earns N$200 profit there.
