# 7.3 Output

## Notes

### B-6145-7.3-001 Domestic, national and income describe different boundaries
National accounts measure production and the incomes and spending associated with it over a period. Gross Domestic Product, or GDP, concerns production within the territory. Gross National Product, or GNP, adjusts domestic product for net income earned from abroad by residents under the stated convention. National Income concerns income earned by a country’s factors of production. The names are related, but they do not permit every figure to be substituted for another without checking its definition.

Specify the period and basis before comparing. A monthly current-price total and an annual constant-price figure answer different questions. Domestic and national boundaries can differ where residents earn abroad or non-residents earn within the country. Gross and net measures also differ where capital-consumption allowance is deducted. These distinctions prevent a meaningful definition from becoming an unsupported numerical equality.

The simple invented cases below isolate one distinction at a time. They do not claim that current published national accounts contain no further adjustments. Where a question states a particular accounting convention, use it consistently and describe the measure actually obtained. Topic 5.3 measures an individual firm’s costs and revenue; the national account combines activity across producers while preventing repeated counting of the same production.

### B-6145-7.3-002 GDP: production inside the territory
Gross Domestic Product is the value of final goods and services produced within a country’s territory over a specified period, avoiding double counting. Final refers to output used for final consumption or investment rather than an input whose value is already included in another product. Services count alongside tangible goods. A year’s GDP is a flow of production, not the stock of wealth or cash balances at one date.

The domestic boundary follows location. If an invented non-resident-owned factory produces furniture inside Namibia, that production contributes to domestic output. Whether its owners later receive income abroad is relevant to a national-income adjustment, not a reason to erase the factory’s domestic production. Conversely, a resident’s production abroad does not become domestic output merely because the resident has a Namibian connection.

Current-price GDP values output at the prices of the period. It can rise because more is produced, prices rise or both. A real or constant-price comparison holds the price effect apart so changes in production can be examined. If the same quantities are valued 10% higher solely because prices rise, that is not evidence of a 10% expansion in real output.

GDP also differs from total sales of every producer added without adjustment. A material can be sold to a manufacturer and its value appear again in the final product. Counting both full values would repeat the intermediate component. The output method addresses that problem through value added, while the expenditure method focuses on final purchases.

### B-6145-7.3-003 GNP: adjust for net income from abroad
GNP adjusts domestic product for net factor income from abroad under the course’s stated convention. Add relevant income received from abroad by residents and subtract corresponding income paid to non-residents. Net means the difference between these opposing flows. It does not mean simply adding whichever foreign receipt is mentioned and ignoring payments outward.

Use invented annual figures in N$ million. Domestic product is 1,000, factor income received from abroad is 70 and factor income paid abroad is 90. Net income from abroad is 70 minus 90, or negative 20. GNP is therefore 980 on this specified basis. A negative net figure makes the national measure smaller than the domestic measure; it is not added as a positive 20.

The adjustment concerns factor income, not the trade balance. Exports and imports are purchases of output, while a factor-income flow is a reward from productive resources. An imported machine and a resident’s foreign interest receipt belong to different transactions. Their shared international character does not make them interchangeable in the adjustment.

Residence is also different from citizenship alone. The relevant national-accounting boundary concerns residents and their income under the stated convention. Do not decide inclusion merely from a passport description where the question supplies residence and production facts. The simple adjustment clarifies the domestic-national distinction without requiring an unsupported claim that every transfer from abroad is factor income.

### B-6145-7.3-004 National Income: earnings from production
National Income concerns the income earned by a country’s factors of production over a stated period. Wages reward labour; rent, interest and profit are other factor-income categories in the elementary treatment. The focus is earned income associated with production rather than every receipt of money. A transfer received without supplying current goods or services in exchange is not newly earned factor income.

Suppose an invented production example generates N$600 wages, N$100 rent, N$50 interest and N$250 profit, with all categories mutually exclusive and all relevant factor incomes included. Their sum is N$1,000. A separate N$80 support transfer to a household should not be added as new production income. It reallocates purchasing power rather than creating another N$80 of output.

The exact National Income measure depends on the accounting basis. A gross output value, a net income value and a value including product taxes need not be identical without adjustments. Gross means before capital-consumption allowance is deducted; net means after the relevant capital-consumption allowance adjustment. Use the convention supplied rather than inventing a bridge from one headline figure to another. The simple factor-income example sets aside such adjustments explicitly.

This discipline also helps prevent double counting profits. If wages have already been subtracted in arriving at the stated profit, adding wages and that profit counts different shares of value added. Adding sales receipts again would repeat the income generated by the same output. A household’s receipt and subsequent spending are connected stages, not two separate additions to National Income.

### B-6145-7.3-005 Three views of the same productive activity
The output method looks at what is produced or the value added by producers. The income method looks at the factor earnings generated. The expenditure method looks at final spending on the output. With the same period, scope and valuation, these are three views of the same production, not three totals to add together.

An invented self-contained repair service illustrates the relationship. It sells N$1,000 of final repair services, uses no bought-in intermediate inputs in this simplified example, and generates wages N$700 plus profit N$300 with no other factor income. Final expenditure, value added and factor income each total N$1,000. Adding the three N$1,000 figures would count the same service three times.

In real collection, records can be incomplete or timing and valuation can differ. A discrepancy does not mean the methods are intended to measure three independent economies. Consistency adjustments and statistical coverage matter. For a simple supplied case, first identify what the figure represents, then apply the relevant method using its boundary.

The methods can be useful cross-checks. Output records can reveal a sector’s contribution; earnings records can describe income generated; spending records can show final demand composition. Their uses differ in emphasis even when they reconcile to the same overall activity. The following sections explain what each counts and the main repeated-counting error to avoid.

Consistency also means recording the same production period. Payment for a service delivered earlier need not mean that service was produced again when the cash arrives. A production account and a cash-flow record answer different questions. The simplified examples assume their timing is aligned so the economic boundary can be seen clearly.

### B-6145-7.3-006 Output method: count value added once
The output method sums value added across producers or the value of final output, using a consistent basis. Value added is output value minus the value of bought-in intermediate inputs. This prevents an input’s full value being counted at each stage as if it were new production each time. It is not the same as profit because labour and other factor rewards can be paid from value added.

Use an invented chain with no other intermediate inputs. A grower sells produce for N$100 to a processor. The processor transforms it and sells to a household for N$250. The grower adds N$100 and the processor adds N$150, giving total value added N$250. Adding the two sales totals, N$350, would double count the produce’s N$100 value. The final purchase already embodies it.

Now suppose the processor pays N$90 in wages from its N$150 value added and the remaining N$60 is profit under the simplified arrangement. Value added is still N$150, not only N$60. Subtracting wages to find profit serves a firm-accounting purpose; it is not the same operation as subtracting intermediate inputs to find value added.

The method can reveal changes in sectors’ contributions. A larger value at current prices may reflect prices as well as quantities, so real comparisons need a consistent price basis. It is also important to cover relevant informal and non-market production as the chosen measure allows. Missing records can understate activity, but they should not be replaced with arbitrary invented additions in a calculation.

### B-6145-7.3-007 Income method: count earned factor rewards
The income method sums factor incomes earned from current production on a consistent accounting basis. In the simple treatment these include wages, rent, interest and profit. The categories must not overlap and must relate to the period’s production. A loan received is a liability-financing transaction, not automatically additional earned income; a support transfer is a redistribution rather than a fresh factor reward.

Using invented income categories of wages N$600, rent N$100, interest N$50 and profit N$250 gives N$1,000. If a worker saves N$100 of wages, the wage income is still earned. Saving changes how that income is used, not whether the original labour reward exists. Likewise, subsequently buying a household good is expenditure, not a second income-method entry for the buyer.

Selling an existing financial asset shifts ownership and money between parties. The sale price itself is not proof of new output or new factor income. A separate service charge for arranging the sale can represent current service production, but that is a different item requiring its own facts. This boundary keeps large financial transfers from being mistaken for productive income.

For a national rather than domestic income basis, the relevant resident and abroad adjustments must be consistent. Do not combine domestic wages with an unrelated national-profit figure without checking coverage. Data on recorded earnings can also miss activity where records are incomplete. The method is conceptually about income generated from production, not simply the sum of visible bank credits.

### B-6145-7.3-008 Expenditure method: final spending on domestic output
The expenditure method sums final expenditure on domestically produced goods and services over the period. It includes final consumption and investment and the relevant government and external components under the stated convention. Exports are included because they buy domestic production; imports are excluded because that part of spending buys foreign production rather than current domestic output.

An invented simple expenditure case supplies consumption N$500, investment N$200, government final purchases N$250, exports N$100 and imports N$50, all on a consistent basis. The total is N$500 + N$200 + N$250 + N$100 minus N$50, giving N$1,000. Imports are subtracted to remove foreign output included in the spending components, not because importing is inherently a loss in welfare.

Do not add intermediate purchases again. In the produce example, household expenditure N$250 on the processed final product already includes the grower’s output. Adding the processor’s N$100 input purchase would repeat that value. Similarly, a government transfer is not itself a government purchase of a newly produced service; later spending by the recipient is classified where it occurs.

Investment here concerns productive capital formation rather than merely depositing money in an account. Final use also does not mean useful for only a short time: equipment used to produce later output is investment. The distinction is its role in the production account, not whether an object is physically durable. Read each supplied transaction before assigning it to an expenditure component.

### B-6145-7.3-009 Use the measures with their limits visible
National-income measures help describe the level and change of economic activity. Real comparisons across time can show whether production expands after allowing for prices. Sector figures can inform priorities by showing where value added is generated. A government can use these data to assess an employment or growth policy, while businesses may use them alongside other information about likely markets.

Total and per-capita measures answer different questions. An invented real output N$1,000 million with population one million gives N$1,000 per person. Real output N$1,100 million with population 1.2 million gives about N$916.67 per person. The total grows but the average falls. Population can change through natural increase or migration; neither additional people alone nor a larger money total establishes improved real output per person.

Even higher real output per person does not prove that all households are better off. Distribution can be unequal, environmental damage can impose costs, and leisure or service quality can change. Coverage of informal or unpaid activity may differ. Cross-country comparisons also need consistent definitions, prices and currency conversion. The measure is useful evidence of activity, not a complete welfare score.

To explain a use, connect the data to a decision. A consistent real series can reveal a production slowdown and help target investigation. A sector contribution can guide further research into a bottleneck. The figures alone do not identify every cause or establish that one policy produced the change. Combine the measure with the relevant context, and use topic 8.2 for fuller living-standard assessment.

An apparent improvement should therefore prompt a check of the denominator and price basis. More current-price output for a larger population can coexist with less real output per person. The three statements are compatible because they measure different aspects of the economy. Naming the precise measure makes the intended use and its limitation easier to explain.
