# 1.1 Basic economic problem: scarcity and exercise of choice

## Notes

### B-6145-1.1-001 Scarcity creates choices
Resources are limited relative to the wants that could be satisfied with them. This is scarcity, the basic economic problem. A household may want safer transport, improved meals and money for future study. Available income limits the purchases it can make now. At the level of production, land, people’s working time, equipment and materials restrict the goods and services that can be supplied. Resources used for one purpose cannot simultaneously perform every competing use. Choices therefore arise even when people have some income and producers have useful equipment.

Scarcity is a relationship between resources and competing wants, rather than a synonym for having no possessions. More resources can make additional choices possible, but new or previously unmet wants can remain. An invented repair workshop in Tsumeb might buy another workbench and still have more repair requests than its mechanics can complete. The improvement matters; it simply does not demonstrate that all resource constraints have disappeared.

### B-6145-1.1-002 Scarcity and unsold products
Suppose that workshop has unused bicycle tyres in stock. It would be wrong to infer that tyres, mechanics’ time and storage space are unlimited. The stock might remain because customers want another tyre size, cannot afford the price, or do not need repairs this week. Unsold goods concern what happened in a particular market. Resource scarcity concerns whether competing uses exceed what resources can achieve. A mechanic could repair a bicycle, service a handcart or train an apprentice during the same hour. The unused stock does not remove that choice. Ask which resources still have alternative uses before deciding whether scarcity remains.

### B-6145-1.1-003 Identify the next-best feasible alternative
Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative forgone when a choice is made. First identify whose decision is being considered. Then identify the chosen use and the best feasible alternative that it rules out. Do not add the benefits of every rejected option. Those alternatives may exclude one another too. A person with one free evening cannot give up three separate full-evening activities that could never have been undertaken together.

The sacrifice is a benefit forgone, which need not equal a cash payment. If a learner chooses revision instead of a walk with friends, and that walk is the next-best feasible activity, the opportunity cost is the enjoyment or companionship forgone. The revision may cost no money. Feasibility also matters: an unaffordable holiday cannot be the relevant alternative to a small purchase when the holiday lay outside the available budget.

### B-6145-1.1-004 An original production illustration
Use these invented weekly possibilities for a small workshop with fixed working time and equipment. It can complete combinations of bicycle services and handcart services: (0,8), (2,6), (4,4), (6,2) and (8,0). Put bicycle services on the horizontal axis and handcart services on the vertical axis, with both measured per week. Plot the five efficient combinations and join them to obtain a straight production possibility frontier.

Moving from four bicycle services and four handcart services to six bicycle services and two handcart services gains two bicycle services but sacrifices two handcart services. The opportunity cost is one handcart service for each extra bicycle service over this interval. Resources and technology have not increased: their use has changed. The straight line represents constant opportunity cost in this deliberately simple example. A point inside the frontier indicates that these stated possibilities are not fully used; a point beyond it cannot be achieved with the given resources and technology. The illustration describes this workshop’s choices, not a measured national frontier.

### B-6145-1.1-005 Compare benefits and sacrifices
Opportunity cost makes the sacrifice behind a decision explicit, but does not rank everyone’s priorities for them. Imagine the fictional Sand River Youth Hub in Rundu. It can staff one afternoon activity: mathematics tutoring or bicycle-maintenance training. Its stated priority is helping learners with an urgent mathematics assessment, so it ranks tutoring first. Bicycle training is feasible and ranks second. Choosing tutoring supports the priority but gives up the benefit of the training session.

A developed explanation connects the constraint to the sacrifice. One staffed session cannot accommodate both activities. Selecting tutoring uses that session, making training unavailable then. Because training is the best feasible rejected activity, its benefit supplies the opportunity cost. Calling the hub’s N$800 materials budget the opportunity cost would skip this comparison: the cash budget and the forgone activity are different pieces of information.

### B-6145-1.1-006 A conditional judgement
There is a case for tutoring because it directly addresses the hub’s urgent priority. There is a case against the choice because practical bicycle skills also have value for the applicants. Tutoring is justified if the urgency of mathematics support outweighs the training benefits under the stated objective. That conclusion identifies whose priority determines the decision, rather than claiming one activity is better for everyone.

Now suppose an additional volunteer and another afternoon become available for bicycle training without reducing tutoring. The original conflict can change: the hub may be able to provide both activities. Before concluding that the revised arrangement is preferable, check whether volunteer time, rooms or materials would displace another valued use. A changed constraint can alter the choice, but it does not prove that all opportunity costs everywhere have vanished. Likewise, new information about applicants’ needs could alter the ranking even if resources stayed unchanged.

### B-6145-1.1-007 From individual choices to wider allocation
Topic 1.2 explains the factors of production being allocated and the economic systems coordinating their use. The same opportunity-cost reasoning later helps evaluate government policy in topic 6.2 and conflicts of interest in topic 9.1. Keep the decision-maker, period and constraint consistent. A household’s sacrifice may differ from a producer’s sacrifice, while a policy can affect several groups. In each case begin with the feasible alternative actually forgone, then explain the benefits and conditions supporting the judgement.
