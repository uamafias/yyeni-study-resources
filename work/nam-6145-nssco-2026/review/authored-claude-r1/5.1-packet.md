# 5.1 The private firm as producer and employer

## Notes

### B-6145-5.1-001 Profit is a difference, and maximisation is a comparison
A private firm may aim to maximise profit: obtain the largest possible difference between total revenue and total cost from its feasible choices. Profit is not the same as revenue, and maximum profit is not the same as maximum output. More sales can bring additional receipts while adding even more cost. A method that increases profit from its old level may still fall short of the best available alternative.

Use invented weekly figures for Kalahari Clay Works, with all relevant costs included and each output sold. At 10 batches total revenue is N$1,000 and total cost N$800; at 20 batches the figures are N$1,800 and N$1,300; at 30 batches they are N$2,400 and N$2,000. Profits are N$200, N$500 and N$400. The 20-batch option maximises profit among these supplied choices. The 30-batch option has the highest revenue and output but less profit. Unless all feasible choices are represented, describe the conclusion as the best of those listed.

In marginal terms, marginal revenue is additional revenue from another unit and marginal cost is its additional cost. Extra output adds to profit while marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost. A profit maximum can occur where they are equal with marginal cost crossing marginal revenue from below; equality alone is not sufficient evidence of a maximum. For a discrete table, compare total profit directly rather than inventing an exact equality absent from the data. Topic 5.3 develops the cost and revenue measures.

Profit is a goal, but a supplied firm may also value survival, service or stable employment. An evaluation should apply its stated objective rather than assume maximum current profit settles every decision. Reliability and future demand can also affect which option really delivers the highest attainable profit over the relevant period.

### B-6145-5.1-002 Production includes useful services
Production is the creation of goods and services to satisfy wants. A potter making a bowl produces a good; a repairer restoring a bicycle provides a service. Both use resources to generate something that meets a want. Restricting production to factory manufacture would omit agriculture and services, even though those activities contribute output.

A private firm acts as a producer when it organises resources to provide such output. It acts as an employer when it hires workers to help. These roles are connected but distinct: a self-employed person can produce alone, while an expanding firm may recruit workers. Production is not synonymous with employment, and buying an existing asset is not itself evidence that the buyer has created new output.

At Kalahari Clay Works, clay, worker time, a kiln and the owner’s organisation combine to make bowls. Repairing the kiln is a service input that supports later bowl production. Naming both the output and the resources used makes the description precise. The physical transformation of clay is one example of production, not a requirement that every productive activity transform material into a new object.

### B-6145-5.1-003 Demand for resources follows the need for output
Factors of production are demanded because of their contribution to output. If customers want more bowls at profitable prices, the pottery may demand more labour, clay and kiln time. This is derived demand: it comes from demand for the final output rather than workers or equipment being acquired simply for their own sake. The strength of the effect depends on how much additional production is needed and how it can be achieved.

Factor prices and productivity influence the chosen method. A machine providing the same usable output with lower relevant costs may become attractive as wages rise. However, a cheaper machine is not a useful substitute if it cannot make the required product. Some factors complement each other: more machinery can increase demand for skilled maintenance labour. Distinguish the tasks replaced from the tasks newly required.

The time available matters. In the short run, existing premises or equipment may constrain expansion, so the firm cannot immediately change every input. Training or installing machinery can take time. Stronger product demand may initially lead to overtime rather than a new production line. Explain the supplied conditions and available alternatives instead of assuming every increase in sales demand creates the same number or type of jobs.

### B-6145-5.1-004 Substituting capital for labour
Substitution changes the factor combination used to produce output. A pottery may replace some manual shaping tasks with a machine while retaining skilled workers for design, operation and checking. Capital then substitutes for particular labour tasks and complements the workers who operate or maintain it. The effect on total employment depends on these changes and the scale of production, not simply the presence of a machine.

Use invented weekly methods producing and selling the same 100 acceptable pieces. The labour-intensive method has total relevant cost N$7,000. A machine-assisted method has total relevant cost N$6,200, including operation, training and maintenance for the comparison period. Both earn N$9,000 revenue. Profits are N$2,000 and N$2,800. The machine-assisted method improves profit by N$800 under those conditions. The comparison works because output, quality, sales and the cost coverage are stated.

Weak demand can change the conclusion. If a machine’s large fixed commitment remains while sales are low, its cost per sold unit may be unattractive. Unreliable electricity, unavailable maintenance or a need for customised manual work can reduce feasibility. Conversely, strong sustained demand can allow equipment to be used productively. Evaluate the actual total cost and revenue rather than calling capital automatically cheaper.

Before recommending substitution, test whether the output can be sold, the quality maintained and the required skills secured. If a listed cost is only a wage saving, subtracting it does not establish a net profit gain until machine costs are included. The best method is conditional on the firm’s objective and the relevant period; a method that gives more profit than before still needs comparison with the other feasible methods before it is called profit maximising.

As a contrasting invented case, weekly revenue under weak demand is N$3,000 with either method. Total relevant cost is N$2,500 for the labour method and N$3,200 for the machine method. Labour now yields N$500 profit while machine assistance produces a N$200 loss. Choosing labour is consistent with the profit goal under these new conditions. The previous N$800 machine advantage cannot be carried into this case because utilisation and full costs have changed. Nor does this comparison establish that labour is preferable at every output. It demonstrates why the decision requires matched data for the particular demand conditions being evaluated.
