# 7.2 Employment

## Notes

### B-6145-7.2-001 Describe levels and patterns separately
Employment is work used in producing goods and services. Its level is the number employed, while its pattern concerns how jobs are distributed across sectors, occupations, places and working arrangements. Growth in services can coexist with declining manufacturing employment, or both can grow at different rates. A statement about one share does not establish the direction of the total.

Use invented employment figures. In Year 1 agriculture employs 200 people and services 300, giving 500 in total. In Year 2 agriculture employs 220 and services 380, giving 600. Agricultural employment rises by 20, yet its share falls from 40% to about 36.67%. Services grow faster. Describing agriculture as losing jobs because its share fell would confuse the relative pattern with the absolute level.

Changing customer demand, technology and skills can change occupational requirements. More machines can reduce demand for a manual task and raise demand for maintenance skills. The net job effect depends on those changes and the output sold. Full-time, part-time and self-employed work can also change differently, so a headcount alone may hide a change in hours or security.

A simple unemployment rate divides unemployed people by the labour force, employed plus unemployed under the specified definition. If 600 are employed and 100 unemployed, the labour force is 700 and the rate about 14.29%. Children and people outside the labour force are not added to the denominator merely because they are residents. Use a consistent measure before comparing periods, since changed definitions or search behaviour can affect the figures.

### B-6145-7.2-002 Identify the cause rather than assume unwillingness
Unemployment concerns people without work who are willing and able to work and seeking employment under the stated measure. A person studying full-time without seeking work is not automatically unemployed. Nor does unemployment alone show unwillingness to work: an employer may have no vacancy, or available jobs may require different skills. The relevant evidence is the labour-market situation.

Cyclical or demand-deficient unemployment can follow a broad fall in orders. Firms reduce output and labour demand even when workers have useful skills. Structural unemployment arises when the pattern of jobs changes and workers’ skills or location do not match available vacancies. Technological unemployment is one possible structural case when equipment replaces particular tasks and displaced workers cannot readily fill new roles.

Seasonal unemployment arises when work is available only during part of the year, such as a supplied harvesting period. Frictional unemployment reflects time spent finding and moving into a suitable job. These cases need different explanations: a search delay differs from a permanent skill mismatch or a broad absence of buyers. More than one cause can affect the same person.

For example, an invented packing plant loses orders during a general spending decline and dismisses workers who could perform the former tasks. Demand support addresses a different problem from retraining a worker whose former task has permanently changed while suitable new vacancies exist. Neither case is diagnosed from the unemployment label alone. Name the initiating change and the reason it reduces access to paid work.

### B-6145-7.2-003 Follow the consequences to households and the economy
Losing a job can reduce earned income and the ability to meet household spending commitments. Existing savings, another household income or support can cushion the immediate effect, but they are not guaranteed or unlimited. A household may reduce discretionary purchases first, while essential bills remain difficult to adjust. Reduced purchases can also weaken orders at other businesses if the spending loss is not offset elsewhere.

Unused willing and suitable labour represents potential output forgone when productive opportunities exist. Tax revenue may fall as earnings and spending decline, while support needs can increase. The fiscal effect depends on the tax and benefit arrangements supplied; no automatic payment or exact current amount is assumed. These are economy-wide channels rather than simply a cost shown in one employer’s accounts.

Long unemployment may allow skills to deteriorate or make it harder to maintain contacts and confidence. This can weaken later recruitment prospects, reinforcing the original loss. Short search unemployment need not have the same lasting effect. Duration and access to training matter when comparing consequences.

An invented closure removing 30 jobs at one plant is a gross local loss. If other businesses employ 20 of the affected workers during the same period, the net position differs from assuming all 30 remain unemployed. Relocation can still impose costs and the replacement jobs may differ in hours or pay. Assess both the net numbers and the individual circumstances rather than assuming a job replacement removes every consequence.

### B-6145-7.2-004 Match the remedy to the binding cause
Training can help workers meet actual vacancies requiring new skills. It is more convincing when the course is relevant, affordable and accessible and employers genuinely need the skill. Training takes time and does not create customers by itself. If most firms face weak demand, newly qualified workers may still lack vacancies. Job information and placement support can instead reduce search delays where suitable vacancies already exist.

Demand support, such as feasible funded public purchases, can help where suitable workers and capacity are idle because orders are weak. It may create output and jobs if resources respond. Its limits include financing, implementation delays, imports and price pressure when capacity is tight. Topic 6.2 explains those policy channels; the present task is matching them to the unemployment cause.

Mobility support can help where vacancies are elsewhere, but housing, transport and family commitments affect feasibility. Occupational mobility can require training; geographical mobility concerns location. Seasonal diversification can provide work outside the main production season if demand for the alternative output exists. A calendar change does not itself create that demand.

Evaluate a remedy through cause, mechanism, cost and time. For a skill mismatch with unfilled vacancies, relevant training is well targeted. For a general fall in demand with unchanged useful skills, demand support is more direct, provided it is affordable and capacity can respond. A combination may be needed where both problems exist. A conditional choice grounded in the actual constraint is stronger than declaring one remedy best for all unemployed people.

Check additionality when counting the benefit. A programme hiring 50 people may replace existing work or draw some recruits from other employers. Its gross places are not automatically 50 extra jobs for the economy. Similarly, a completed course is not itself evidence of later employment. Relevant follow-up would compare actual placement into sustained suitable work, including the time needed to obtain it.

Measures can also complement each other. Information about genuine vacancies can help a trained worker find a match, while affordable transport can make that match feasible. Where those barriers coexist, improving skills alone may leave the last obstacle intact. Diagnose the sequence of constraints before deciding which resources to spend.

The relevant comparison is sustained suitable employment, including the actual hours and earnings, rather than course attendance or announced vacancies alone.
