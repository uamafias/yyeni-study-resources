## Migration: why people move, and what it does

### What migration is

Migration is a move to live somewhere else, not a visit or a seasonal stay for work alone. Internal migration stays within one country, and the largest internal stream in most countries is the move from countryside to city. International migration crosses a national border, which is what makes it need papers, permission or risk. Wherever the move ends, it has a place of origin and a destination, and every reason for it belongs to one side or the other.

### Push and pull

Push factors are things about the origin that make people want to leave: a poor harvest, no work, low pay, few schools and clinics, or danger. Pull factors are things about the destination that attract: better-paid jobs, services, family already settled, safer and more stable conditions. A migration usually needs both, because a reason to leave does not by itself say where to go, and an attraction does not by itself force anyone out of the origin. Migration streams then follow routes people can afford and are allowed to use: distance, cost, borders and information filter who moves, so most international migrants from a poor region head for the nearest country with work, or for one where relatives have already settled and can pass on jobs, rooms and know-how.

### Whose side is the question on?

Migration has separate impacts on the origin, the destination and the migrants, and the commonest error is answering for the wrong one: writing about the migrants when the question asks about the destination, or listing problems when it asks for benefits. Before writing, decide whose side the question asks about, and keep every sentence on that side.

### Impacts on the origin

A village that loses its young adults to the city or abroad feels the loss and the gain together. The departure leaves fewer workers to farm the land and fewer carers for the old, so some fields go unploughed and grandparents raise children alone. But the migrants send money home, and those remittances build houses, pay school fees and lift the families left above what local work could pay.

### Impacts on the destination

A city or country that receives many migrants gains workers who fill jobs, often the work local people avoid, and pay taxes into services as their children enter the schools. But rapid arrivals strain housing and services before they can grow, rents can rise, and resentment can build where the newcomers are blamed for crowding. Some migrants end up in poor-quality, overcrowded housing because that is all the market offers them on arrival.

### Impacts on the migrants

For the movers themselves, the balance usually improves: pay is higher, children enter better schools, and the move can lift a whole family's prospects. The costs are real too: families are separated across borders, language and paperwork slow people down, and undocumented or unskilled arrivals are open to exploitation because complaining risks the job or the stay.

## Case study: Mexico to the United States

### The stream and its causes

More than a million people a year move from Mexico to the United States, most of them crossing at the border cities to work in farming, construction and services: one of the world's largest and longest-running international migration streams. The push is farm poverty in parts of Mexico, where a day's work pays a fraction of United States wages, and the pull is that same gap seen from the other side: a worker can earn several times more across the border. Family networks in states such as California and Texas settle newcomers into jobs and housing, so each migrant makes the next one's move easier, which is why the stream has run for decades rather than drying up.

### The impacts

For Mexico, the stream empties villages of working-age adults, and the money the migrants send home, about 60 billion United States dollars a year in the early 2020s, builds houses and keeps children in school, one of the largest remittance flows in the world. For the United States border states, the arrivals supply labour for agriculture, construction and services, and Spanish has entered daily use across the south. The impacts on the two sides are different, so an answer must say which one it is giving.

### Where this connects

This case study answers any 7-mark question that names an international migration. Its impacts use the origin-and-destination structure of the first half of this topic, and its remittances connect to development, topic 3.1.
