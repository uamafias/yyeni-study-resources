## Water: supply, use, shortage and management

### The methods of water supply

Three methods supply most of the world's piped water. Reservoirs behind dams store river water, which is then treated and piped to towns; a reservoir turns a seasonal flow into a year-round supply. Wells and boreholes draw groundwater held in rocks below the water table - a hand-dug well serves a village, a drilled borehole with a pump serves a town. Desalination removes salt from sea water, which matters where a coast has little fresh water, though the process is energy-hungry. The test of a description is how water travels from source to user: stored, then treated, then delivered.

### Who uses the water

Water is used for agriculture, domestic purposes and industry, and the shares differ sharply with development. Globally, agriculture takes roughly 70 per cent of fresh water used (rounded). In many low-income countries, farming takes well over half the national supply, much of it irrigation for crops in dry seasons; industry and homes take small shares because little is piped and few factories run. In many high-income countries, the agricultural share is smaller and industry and households take more, because most homes have piped water and most economies manufacture heavily. Worked example: a low-income pattern of roughly 80, 10 and 10 per cent sits beside a high-income pattern nearer 40, 15 and 45 - the shares track what the economy does.

### Follow the water from source to user

A water-supply answer earns its marks by following the water: the scheme is named, then how the water is collected and stored, then how it is treated, then how it is piped or carried to homes, farms or industry. Listing methods - a dam, some wells, a pipeline - without developing how the water reaches the users is a list, and background about a project's history is off the point. The test: does the answer follow the water from source to user?

### Why water shortages happen

A shortage exists where demand exceeds what the local supply can deliver, and its causes are physical and human. Physical: low or seasonal rainfall; rivers that are small, seasonal or far from the people; droughts that stretch over years, as in the Sahel and in Namibia's dry years. Human: population growth raises demand in towns built around smaller supplies; irrigation and over-pumped boreholes draw groundwater faster than rain refills it; and where the built supply - pipelines, pumps, treatment works - was never constructed, water that exists a few kilometres away cannot reach the households that need it.

### Too little, or unsafe?

A shortage and a quality problem are not the same thing. A shortage is too little water: low rainfall, distant rivers, no pipes or boreholes. Poor quality is water that is unsafe to drink, polluted or untreated. A village beside a polluted river may hold plenty of water and still suffer disease; a desert town with a clean piped supply may face scarcity in a drought year. The test: too little water, or water that is unsafe?

### What the lack of clean water does

Lack of access to clean water harms local people and holds back economic development. Hours each day go to collecting water, time not spent farming, trading or studying - and the carriers are usually women and girls, so their schooling suffers first. Where the only water is unsafe, water-borne diseases such as cholera spread, burdening health services and keeping adults from work. Farmers without irrigation lose crops in dry years, so incomes fall and the region grows slower - water supply is a development question, not only a comfort.

### Management for future supplies

The pressures on supply do not ease on their own: populations grow, the climate is warming, and aquifers are being drawn down, so future supplies need careful management. The strategies that work: repairing leaks in the delivery network, which saves water already collected and treated; recycling waste water for industry and gardens; drip irrigation, which delivers water straight to crop roots and wastes far less than flood irrigation; desalination where the sea is near and energy is affordable; and metering with realistic tariffs, which makes each household's use visible and cuts waste. Each strategy names what is built or changed, which is what an answer must do.

### Name what would be built

Writing that an area needs "better infrastructure" explains nothing, because the word names no pipe, pump or plant. The answer must name what would be built: pipelines to carry water from the source, pumps to move it uphill, reservoirs to store it, treatment works to make it safe, boreholes to reach the aquifer. The test: what exactly would be built?

## Case study: water supply in Windhoek, Namibia

### Windhoek: the supply

Windhoek, Namibia's capital, supplies about 450 000 people (mid-2010s, rounded) on the Central Plateau, one of the driest parts of the country, with roughly 300-400 millimetres of rain a year, nearly all in summer. The sources are several: boreholes into aquifers beneath and beside the city; the Goreangab and Avis dams on small seasonal rivers, which fill in the wet months and stand low after dry ones; and the direct re-use plant, which treats the city's waste water and returns it to the drinking taps - the oldest such plant in continuous use worldwide, running since the late 1960s. The sources are told apart by how their water travels: pumped from the aquifer, drawn from the dam, or cycled from the city's own drains.

### The management, step by step

The water is managed in steps. Tariffs rise with use, so the more a household consumes, the more each litre costs - in the drought year of 2016 these tariffs were central to cutting the city's consumption sharply. The re-use plant recycles waste water, so a litre drawn once serves the city twice, which stretches every borehole and dam. And the boreholes are pumped within measured limits, so the aquifers are drawn down slowly and recover in wetter years. The city also stores water in aquifers on purpose, recharging them in wet seasons. Together the steps show management as the condition of supply: a desert capital keeps its taps through metering, recycling and restraint, and every strategy names something specific - a tariff, a plant, a pumping limit - rather than an abstraction.
