## Urban land use, urban problems and their answers

### The zones of land use

A city's land use sorts into zones, and the names matter. The CBD, the Central Business District, is the commercial centre: the shops, offices, banks and civic buildings around the main street, where land values are highest. Around and beyond it lie the inner-city areas, older and denser housing built when workers had to walk to work. Further out lie the suburbs, newer housing at lower density with gardens and space, which spread as trams, buses and cars freed people from walking. At the edge lies the rural-urban fringe, the zone where urban and rural land uses meet: smallholdings, market gardens, new housing estates, warehouses and open land awaiting the city's arrival.

### Why the land use sorts as it does

The sorting has a mechanism behind it: accessibility. The centre of a city is the one point the whole urban area can reach most easily, so businesses compete for it, and their bidding pushes land values to their highest point. Whoever pays those values builds high and uses every floor, which is why CBDs rise into high-rise offices and shops and hold little else; a car park or a wheat field on the busiest corner cannot pay its rent. Further out, land is cheaper and travel slower, so the uses that need space, houses with gardens, warehouses, factories, take the ground instead. Describing a CBD means naming what stands there, high offices, shops, banks, little open space; explaining it means giving the mechanism, highest accessibility, so highest values, so highest buildings.

### Land use changes

The zones are a snapshot of a moving process. Cities grow outwards, so industry and new housing push into the rural-urban fringe and farmland gives way to estates; the suburbs of one generation become the inner city of the next as their housing ages; and trade itself moves, because a CBD can lose shoppers to out-of-town malls with free parking, leaving empty upper floors behind. So a land-use map always carries a date on it, and answers about change should name what is arriving and what it is arriving over.

### The problems and their causes

Urban problems grow from density and growth. Traffic congestion, because every trip converges on the same streets and the CBD's oldest streets were built narrowest. Housing shortage and squatter settlements, because in a fast-growing city the arrivals outnumber the homes built, so newcomers settle on land they do not own and build for themselves. Pressure on services, because water pipes, sewer lines and refuse rounds are sized for one population and then handed another. And the loss of farmland at the fringe, because a spreading city builds on exactly the land that used to feed it. Each problem names its cause, and an answer that names the mechanism earns its marks: congestion follows trips, shortage follows growth, service failure follows population outrunning pipes.

### The solutions

Every solution either manages the flow or adds the supply. For traffic, more road space, bypasses, ring roads, dual carriageways, redirects vehicles around the centre, while public transport moves more people per road-space than private cars, so bus priority and rapid transit cut congestion with the same tarmac. For housing, three tools: servicing land with water, roads and power so that new settlement builds on prepared ground; upgrading existing settlements in place rather than clearing them; and self-help schemes, in which households build their own homes with organised support. For the fringe, green belts and planned expansion boundaries hold the city's shape. Every solution trades costs against reach, so an answer should say which family it belongs to and what it gives up.

## Case study: Windhoek

### Windhoek's land use

Windhoek holds about 486 000 people (2023) in its plateau basin, and its land use reads like the zone model. The CBD runs along Independence Avenue: high-rise offices, the bigger shops, banks and government buildings, where land values are highest. To the north-west lies Katutura, the older, denser suburb laid out in the 1960s, its grid of small houses and shebeens. The southern and eastern suburbs, Ludwigsdorf, Auas Valley and their neighbours, hold low-density housing on large plots. And the northern fringe holds the fastest-growing informal settlements, Havana and Okahandja Park among them, where arrivals build for themselves while they wait for services.

### The problems and the answers

Windhoek's growth outran its services. The northern informal settlements grew faster than water points and toilets followed, so households shared standpipes between many families and queued for taxis on unsurfaced streets. The answers tried are specific and named: the mass-housing programme and the serviced-land programme launched from 2014, which prepare plots with water and power before settlement arrives; the Shack Dwellers Federation of Namibia, which since the 1990s has helped thousands of families secure land and build homes at well below market cost through saving schemes; and traffic answers in both families, the southern bypass that takes through-traffic out of the CBD and the MoveWindhoek plan of the 2010s, which proposes bus rapid transit so that fewer journeys need a private car. A case-study answer names the city, the districts, the schemes and what each one does.

### Where this connects

Windhoek's growth is the urbanisation of topic 1.7 measured in one place; its land-use zones answer any question on urban land use with a named example; and its service shortfalls trace the migration of topic 1.2.
