## Climates, ecosystems and deforestation

### The equatorial climate

Places within about ten degrees of the Equator, such as Manaus in Brazil, share the equatorial climate. Every month is hot: average temperatures sit between about 26 and 28 degrees Celsius all year, so the annual temperature range is tiny, often under 3 degrees. Rainfall is heavy in every month, usually more than 2000 millimetres a year, and there is no dry season. The daily rhythm matters as much as the yearly totals: mornings are hot and sunny, and most afternoons bring a violent thunderstorm. The reason is the sun, which stands high in the sky at midday all year, so the ground heats strongly every day. Air heated at the surface rises, cools and condenses into tall storm clouds, giving convectional rainfall most afternoons. Humidity stays high because the forest and the rivers return moisture to the air. A climate graph shows this pattern at a glance, and reading one accurately is part of this topic.

### Reading a climate graph

On a climate graph the rainfall is drawn as bars, read against the left axis in millimetres, and the temperature as a line, read against the right axis in degrees Celsius. Two ranges are easy to mix up as well. The daily (diurnal) temperature range is the difference between the highest and the lowest temperature within one day; the annual temperature range is the difference between the warmest and the coldest month. At an equatorial station the daily range is often larger than the annual one, which surprises many learners: every day is hot, but no month is much hotter than another.

### The hot desert climate

Hot deserts, from the interior of the Sahara to the Namib coast, are defined by dryness: rainfall under 250 millimetres a year, often far less. Temperatures swing widely. Afternoons are scorching, in summer sometimes above 45 degrees Celsius, while the clear, dry nights can feel cold, so the daily temperature range is large, because dry air holds little moisture to soften the day's heat. The annual temperature range is large too, since winter months are warm rather than hot. Cloud is rare, so the sun burns through most days and humidity stays low.

### Why hot deserts are so dry

Desert dryness has three linked causes. First, around the tropics air sinks from high above and warms as it descends; sinking air does not form clouds, so high pressure dominates and rain rarely falls. Second, some deserts border cold ocean currents: the Benguela Current chills the air above the sea off Namibia, and cold air holds little moisture, so the onshore winds arrive dry. Third, deserts deep inside continents, like the central Sahara, lie too far from the sea for moist winds to reach them. Any one of these is usually enough to keep rainfall low; many deserts have two or all three.

### Ecosystems and the rainforest layers

An ecosystem is all the plants and animals of an area together with the soil, water and climate they depend on. The tropical rainforest ecosystem grows in layers: emergent trees break the sky above a dense canopy about thirty metres up, younger trees and shrubs stand below it, and the forest floor lies deep in shade. Only a sliver of the light reaches the floor, so few plants grow there until a fallen tree opens a gap. Dead leaves rot within weeks in the heat and damp, and the roots, often shallow and propped by buttresses, take the nutrients up at once, so the forest keeps its fertility in its vegetation rather than in its soil. A single hectare can hold more than two hundred tree species, which makes the rainforest richer in species than almost any other ecosystem on land.

### Drip tips and other rainforest adaptations

Rainforest plants compete for light, not water, and their adaptations show it. Drip tips are pointed leaf ends that channel heavy rain off the leaf, so it dries quickly instead of collecting water, growing fungus or being shaded. Buttress roots spread wide at the trunk's base to hold tall trees upright in the shallow soil. Lianas climb the trunks to reach the canopy light. A common error is to write that drip tips keep moisture in: in a forest that rains nearly every day, the difficulty is too much water, and the leaf sheds it.

### Desert plants and animals

Desert organisms manage scarce water in three main ways. Succulents such as cacti and euphorbias store water in fat stems and carry spines or small leaves, which cut the water lost to the dry air. Others grow roots that are long and deep or wide and shallow, catching water from brief showers before it evaporates. The welwitschia of the Namib, a living fossil with two ribbon-like leaves that can live for over a thousand years, drinks the coastal fog instead. Animals such as the oryx graze at dawn and dusk and rest in shade through the heat of the day, and many smaller desert creatures are nocturnal, emerging only once the air cools.

### Deforestation: from cause to effect

Deforestation is the large-scale clearing of forest. In the tropics the causes are commercial logging, cattle ranching, shifting cultivation and, increasingly, commercial crops such as soya and oil palm; the fruit of the oil palm gives palm oil, a vegetable oil, not a mineral oil pumped from the ground. Dams, mines and roads clear more forest directly. The chain of effects starts the moment the trees go. Without leaves to intercept it, rain strikes bare soil; without roots to bind it, the soil washes into rivers, so fields lose fertility and flooding downstream grows worse. The habitat is destroyed with the forest, and the people who lived in it lose their land. The effects then reach the whole planet: burning and rotting vegetation releases carbon dioxide, which adds to global warming, and species are lost for good. Local effects such as erosion, flooding and habitat loss are felt near the forest; global effects, carbon and biodiversity, belong to everyone.

### Brazil's clearing figures

Brazil monitors the Amazon by satellite and publishes the area cleared each year. The estimates show clearing falling steeply from about 28 000 square kilometres in 2004 to about 4 600 in 2012, after which the figures rose again, to about 13 000 in 2021. A trend answer names the whole journey, the fall and the renewed rise, not just the end points, and gives the years for each figure.

## Case studies: the Amazon and the Namib

### Case study: the Amazon rainforest, Brazil

The Amazon rainforest in South America is the largest area of tropical rainforest on Earth, covering about 5.5 million square kilometres, of which about 60 per cent lies inside Brazil. Its climate is equatorial, hot and wet in every month, and its rivers carry a vast share of the world's fresh water to the sea. For a place-specific focus, take the state of Rondonia in western Brazil, a famous deforestation frontier. The BR-364 highway, paved in the 1980s, opened the forest to settlement: migrants cleared plots for small farms, and loggers, cattle ranches and later soya farms followed the road inland. Cattle ranching is the leading use of the land after clearing, and herds graze pastures that were rainforest a generation ago. Brazil protects parts of the Amazon with national parks and indigenous reserves, and its satellite monitoring publishes clearing figures each year: about 28 000 square kilometres cleared in 2004, about 4 600 in 2012, and about 13 000 by 2021. The detail worth remembering is Rondonia, the BR-364, cattle and soya on the cleared land, and the swing of the satellite figures. The forest's own character explains why the loss matters: the canopy holds so much moisture that a cleared plot dries hard within a few years, the thin soil exhausts after two or three harvests, and the farmers move on to clear again, so the frontier advances rather than settles. The rainforest's structure, layer on layer from emergent trees through closed canopy to the dark floor, is what the chainsaws remove, and with it the plants, the insects and the birds that no other ground can replace.

### Case study: the Namib Desert

The Namib runs along the Atlantic coast of southern Angola, Namibia and the Northern Cape of South Africa for about 2000 kilometres, and it is counted among the oldest deserts on Earth. Rainfall near the coast is usually under 20 millimetres a year, but fog drifting inland over the cold Benguela Current dampens the coast on up to 180 days a year, and much of the desert's life depends on that fog. The welwitschia, a plant with two ragged ribbon-like leaves that lives for over a thousand years, drinks moisture from the air; oryx and other antelope graze the sparse grass at dawn and dusk. People cluster where water can be found. Topnaar communities herd goats along the dry Kuiseb valley and gather the nara melon, and boreholes supply homesteads far from any river. The desert is also a working landscape: the open-pit uranium mine at Rossing, near Arandis inland of Walvis Bay, has operated since 1976, its mine and town drawing water from underground, and tourism is the second pillar, centred on the red dunes above 300 metres at Sossusvlei in the Namib-Naukluft area. Along the coast, Swakopmund and Walvis Bay sit where the desert meets the cold ocean, and their moored fishing fleet, salt works and tour operators all live off the desert's edge: the fog cools the towns, the dunes bring the visitors, and the cold current feeds the fish. The two case studies of this unit answer any 7-mark question that names an area of tropical rainforest or an area of hot desert, and they pair as opposites: one ecosystem drowning in rain it cannot store, the other drinking fog because rain almost never comes, and the same syllabus skills, structure, adaptation, human use and threat, answer for both.
