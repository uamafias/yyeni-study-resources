## Tourism: growth, attractions, impacts and management

### Tourism and the receiving area

Tourism is travel away from a person's usual home for leisure, business, sport or visiting friends and family, normally involving at least one night away. The people who travel are tourists; the place they travel to is the receiving area. Geography studies tourism because it links places: money, people and expectations move from the tourists' home region into the receiving area, and the landscape, economy and daily life of that area are changed by their arrival.

### Why tourism has grown

International tourism grew from about 25 million arrivals a year in 1950 to about 1 400 million in 2019 (rounded figures), a rise of more than fifty-fold in one lifetime. Four changes explain it. Jet aircraft and later budget airlines cut the time and the cost of long journeys, so a week away came within reach of ordinary incomes. Incomes themselves rose in many countries, leaving households with money to spend on travel. Paid holidays spread through the world of work, giving people the time as well as the money. And booking became easy, first through package tours and then through the internet, which opened travel to people who would not have arranged a trip alone. In 2020 the pandemic closed borders and arrivals collapsed, but the long-run growth came from conditions that still hold: cheaper transport, rising incomes, paid time and easy booking.

### Attractions: physical and human

Attractions come from the physical landscape and the human landscape, and an attraction explains a resort only when it is tied to what the visitor does there. Physical attractions: a sheltered sandy bay and a warm sea suit swimming and sunbathing; a coral reef suits diving and snorkelling; mountains suit hiking and winter sports; a hot, dry winter climate suits sun-seekers escaping a cold season; waterholes full of wildlife suit game viewing. Human attractions: historic buildings and museums draw sightseers; festivals draw visitors who travel for music and culture; markets and craft sellers draw shoppers. Namibia shows both kinds. The dunes of Sossusvlei draw photographers and sand-boarders; the waterholes of Etosha draw wildlife watchers; Swakopmund's old buildings and its adventure activities draw holiday-makers who want heritage and sport side by side. A place with attractions of both kinds draws more kinds of visitor.

### A list is not an explanation

A common error in written answers is to stop at the list: beach, museum, mountains, good climate. A list names features; it does not say why they attract tourists. Each attraction must be described together with the visitors it draws - a coral reef attracts divers, a hot dry winter attracts sun-seekers, a waterhole attracts wildlife watchers. The test: does each attraction say what the visitor does there?

### What tourism gives a receiving area

Tourism benefits the receiving area in three main ways. It creates jobs: direct jobs in hotels, restaurants, tour companies and craft markets, and indirect jobs in farming, fishing, building and transport, which supply the tourist industry with food, materials and services. It brings in foreign income: visitors arrive with money earned elsewhere and spend it in the receiving area, so tourism works like an export for the local economy, and in some countries it is one of the largest sources of foreign currency. And it can bring improvements local people share in: roads, water supplies, electricity and airports built for visitors also serve residents, and park or museum entry fees can help fund public services.

### What tourism costs a receiving area

The same industry brings problems. Much tourist work is seasonal: hotels and tour companies take on staff for the busy months and lay them off in the quiet ones, and the pay in many of these jobs is low. Money leaks away: where airlines, hotel chains and tour operators are foreign-owned, a large share of what visitors spend leaves the receiving area again. Prices rise: demand from visitors can push up the cost of land, food and services, which prices local people out of their own town in the busy season. And the environment suffers: crowding, traffic, litter, footpath erosion and building on dunes and wetlands damage the very landscape the visitors came for.

### People or environment?

Questions about tourism's impacts ask about a receiving area, and an area has two parts: its people and its environment. Impacts are sorted by who or what feels them. Local people feel jobs, wages, prices and crowding; the natural environment feels erosion, litter and habitat loss. Writing about litter when the question asks about local people is answering a different question - the test is: people or environment? A complete evaluation knows impacts on both, and answers the one the question asks.

### Sustainable tourism

Sustainable tourism meets the needs of visitors, the industry and local people today without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs. In practice that means protecting the landscape, wildlife and culture that attract the visitors, keeping a fair share of the money in the host community, and not consuming the place faster than it can renew itself. Sustainable development of any kind balances present use against future need, and tourism is no different.

### Why management is required

Unmanaged tourism destroys its own attraction. Path networks erode, wildlife is disturbed, litter accumulates, and crowding drives the next season's visitors somewhere quieter - the industry declines on the strength of the damage it causes. Management keeps the use within what the place can bear. The strategies that work: caps on daily visitor numbers at fragile sites; entry and conservation fees, so the visitors pay for protecting what they visit; boardwalks and marked trails, which concentrate feet on a hardened line and let the surrounding ground recover; and local ownership through community conservancies or local partnerships, so that a share of the income stays with the people who host the visitors - and who then have a reason to protect what draws them.

## Case study: tourism at Etosha National Park, Namibia

### Etosha National Park

Etosha National Park covers about 22 000 square kilometres of dry woodland and grassland in northern Namibia, around a salt pan so large it dominates the park's centre. Visitors stay at the state rest camps at Okaukuejo, Halali and Namutoni, each built beside an artificial waterhole. The waterholes are the great attraction: elephants, giraffes, zebras, lions and the black rhinos Etosha is known for protecting come to drink, and at Okaukuejo the waterhole is lit at night so visitors can watch after dark. The park received roughly 200 000 visitors a year in the late 2010s (rounded figure), arriving by road from Windhoek or through Outjo.

### Why tourism matters around Etosha

The region is dry, with unreliable rainfall and little other employment, so the park's conservation fees and rest-camp wages are a foundation of the local economy. Because the wildlife stays protected, the earnings continue: visitors pay precisely because the animals are there. Outside the gates, neighbouring communal conservancies - registered areas where a community holds the rights to manage and benefit from its wildlife - receive levies from joint-venture lodges, so tourism income reaches villages that never see a tourist bus. The same fees fund anti-poaching patrols and wildlife monitoring, which keep the attraction alive.

### The management, and why it works

The management follows the place's limits. Beds are capped at the three state rest camps instead of allowing hotels to spread across the park; day visitors pay a conservation fee on entry; speed limits and a ban on off-road driving protect the animals and the pan's fragile crust. The result is a park where the visits that pay for conservation are kept small enough not to damage what they pay for - management as the condition of the income, not its enemy.
