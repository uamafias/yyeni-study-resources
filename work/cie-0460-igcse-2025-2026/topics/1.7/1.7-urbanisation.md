**What this topic asks of you**

## Why cities grow, and what growth does

### Urbanisation and the rural area

Urbanisation is the growth in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas. The word is about a share, not a city: Windhoek growing does not by itself urbanise Namibia, but the rising share of Namibians who live in towns does, and Namibia's census counted about 49.5 per cent of its people in urban areas in 2023, against roughly a third at independence in 1990. A second term keeps its sense here: a rural area is countryside, with farmland and small settlements. The term describes the land and its settlement, and being less developed is not part of the definition, so a village with a school and a clinic is still rural.

### The two engines of rapid growth

Two engines drive rapid urban growth. The first is rural-urban migration, taught with its push and pull factors in topic 1.2: young adults are pushed by failing farm incomes, drought years and schooling that ends early, and pulled by specific things a reader can picture, more jobs in factories and offices, secondary school places, hospital care and piped water. Saying a city has a better standard of living or more facilities explains nothing, because nobody can picture what is better; naming the jobs and the schools is the reasoned answer. The second engine is natural increase: migrants are mostly young adults, so the city holds many births and few elderly deaths, and its population keeps climbing even in a year when migration pauses. In developing countries both engines run hard at once, which is why their urban populations often grow by several per cent a year.

### One drought, two places

One chain shows the whole process. A drought year cuts the harvest in a rural district, so farm incomes fall; the young adults of a household move to the city, because factory and office jobs there pay wages the farm can no longer match; they settle in a self-built house at the city's edge, where land is cheap, and send part of their wages home; the district they left ages as its young adults go, and the settlement they join grows as its water points do not. Growth at one end and thinning at the other are one process seen from two places.

### What growth does to the city

In the city, the impacts follow the growth. Arrivals build faster than the city can service, so squatter settlements of self-built houses spread on land the residents do not own, often without drains or taps. More trips on the same streets bring congestion, and pipes sized for one population serve another, so water, sewerage and electricity fall short. Jobs, too, are created more slowly than workers arrive, so unemployment and under-employment grow beside the settlements.

### What growth does to the countryside

The countryside people leave changes too. Fewer young adults remain, so the population structure ages and farm labour grows scarce; grandparents raise children whose parents work in the city. The link runs both ways, because migrants send money home, and those remittances can pay school fees and put seed back into the farm.

### Solutions at both ends

The answers work at both ends of the move. In the city, upgrading informal settlements in place, adding water points, drains and power to the homes people have already built, reaches far more households for the same budget than clearing and rebuilding; servicing land before settlement arrives does the same for the next arrivals. In the countryside, developing rural roads, clinics and secondary schools makes staying a real choice, which slows the migration that outgrows the services. A full answer names the tool, where it acts and what it changes.

### Where this connects

The migration engine here is topic 1.2's push and pull measured in town-sized numbers; the squatter settlements and transport answers are topic 1.6's urban problems at a developing-country scale; and Namibia's rising urban share carries topic 1.1's population dynamics into towns.

## Case study: Lagos

### The growth of Lagos

Lagos, on Nigeria's coast, is one of the largest cities in Africa, with an estimated 16.5 million people in 2024 and growth of hundreds of thousands a year, so a city the size of Namibia's whole population arrives every few years. It was Nigeria's capital until 1991 and remains the country's chief port and commercial city: the port at Apapa, the factories and markets around it and the office towers of Victoria Island draw workers, and migrants arrive from the rural north and east of Nigeria and from neighbouring countries for what a city sells, wages, secondary schools and hospitals. The growth has outpaced the services, and housing, water and traffic all fall short of the arrivals, which is what the second block shows.

### The impacts and the answers, by name

The impacts are specific and named. Makoko is the best known: a settlement of houses on stilts above the lagoon, where thousands of families live without secure land or piped water, and where daily life runs on canoes through the channels between the houses. The city's traffic is heavy, and the Bus Rapid Transit system, launched in 2008, carries commuters in dedicated lanes to ease it. On the Atlantic side, Eko Atlantic is a new district on land reclaimed from the sea behind a long sea wall, adding office and apartment ground the old city has none of. Together they show both kinds of answer at work: serving the people the growth has already brought, with taps and transit, and building the ground the growth will need, which is the two-part answer an urban-growth question rewards. A case-study answer names the city, the districts and the schemes, and lets the places carry the argument.

### Where this connects

Lagos's migration is topic 1.2's push and pull in one place; Makoko and the 2008 transit system are topic 1.6's problems and solutions at a developing-country scale.
