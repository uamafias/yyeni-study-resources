## Aims and hypotheses

### Hypothesis and aim

An aim says what the enquiry sets out to find, and a hypothesis is the claim about the answer that the data will confirm or reject. A hypothesis is a statement that can be tested by collecting relevant data, analysing it and drawing a conclusion from the evidence, which is why it is written as a claim and not a question. "Does the river get bigger downstream?" is an aim with no claim to test; "the bedload of the river becomes smaller and rounder downstream" is a hypothesis the stones can confirm or reject.

### What makes it testable

A testable hypothesis names the places, the measure and the expected direction. Places: the Kuiseb river above and below the Naukluft confluence, not just "a river". Measure: pebble long-axis in centimetres, not "size". Direction: smaller and rounder downstream, not "different". With those three fixed, the data collection knows what to measure, the analysis knows what pattern would confirm the claim, and the conclusion has something definite to say, true, false or partly true with figures attached. A vague hypothesis collects data that cannot settle anything, so the sharpest sentence of the enquiry is written first.

### Two hypotheses, one method apart

Take the same street and two hypotheses to see the difference a claim makes. "People visit the town centre more on Saturdays than weekdays" names a comparison, a measure, a direction and a time, so the counts can settle it, Saturday against Wednesday, hour by hour. "The town centre is busier at weekends" names a feeling, no measure of busy, no definition of weekend, no direction to test, so no data collection could confirm or reject it and the enquiry would drift. The discipline is the same for a river: "discharge increases downstream" is measurable, width, depth and speed at four sites; "the river gets bigger" is a sketch of an idea. Write the claim, fix the measure, name the direction, and the fieldwork has a destination.

## Collecting the data: questionnaires, counts and measurements

### Designing a questionnaire

A questionnaire succeeds or fails by four decisions. Layout: clear, uncluttered, with room for answers, so the interviewer reads and records quickly. Format: mostly closed questions, tick boxes and short choices, quick to tally, with one or two open questions where reasons are wanted. Wording: short, neutral, one thing per question, no jargon and no leading phrases such as "surely you agree". Number: few enough that a stranger finishes in two or three minutes, because a long questionnaire is refused or answered carelessly. Every question earns its place by producing data the hypothesis needs.

### Question wording gone wrong

Three wording faults ruin data silently. A leading question suggests its own answer, "don't you find the town centre pleasant?", so the respondent agrees to be polite. A double question asks two things at once, "are you satisfied with the shops and the parking?", so one answer covers two opinions. A jargon question uses a term the respondent may not hold, "is the CBD's sphere of influence extensive?", which collects guesses. One plain thing per question is the rule.

### Conducting the survey

Three practical decisions govern the survey. The sampling method: random gives every person an equal chance, systematic takes every nth person, stratified samples groups in proportion to their share, and the choice decides whether the sample represents the population, so the method is named, never just "we asked people". The pilot survey: a small trial of the questions and the method on a handful of respondents first, so unclear wording, missing answer boxes and overlong interviews are fixed before the real survey wastes its day. The location: where the people the hypothesis is about actually are, the market square for shoppers, the terminus for commuters, and the same location for every session so the sample is consistent.

### The vague reason

A method is justified by what it measures, not by convenience. "We used a pilot survey because it is easy" says nothing; "the pilot survey tested whether our questions were understood, so the three questions respondents misread were rewritten before the real survey" says what would have gone wrong without it. The same test applies to every method choice: what exactly would go wrong without it, and the answer names the defect the method prevents.

### Observation

Observation records what is seen on the spot, and it is disciplined by four tools. A base map with the features marked where they stand. A recording sheet with columns for what is counted or scored, filled in as the observation happens, not remembered afterwards. A field sketch, drawn from one fixed viewpoint, framed by a rectangle, labelled with the viewpoint, the direction and the date, and annotated with the features the enquiry needs. An annotated photograph, printed and labelled with the same features, so the record can be read by someone who was not there.

### Recording sheets and counts

A recording sheet for a count carries its identifying details: time, date, location and the recorder's name, so any two sheets can be compared fairly and nothing is left as an anonymous column of numbers. Counts at different sites are made at the same time of day, because a morning count and an evening count differ for reasons that have nothing to do with the site. Two designs cover most counts: a tally count of everything passing in a set period, or a timing count of how long a set number takes, and the choice follows the numbers expected, a busy site needs timing, a quiet site needs tally.

### The sheet without details

A count sheet with no time, date, location or recorder is not evidence: it cannot be compared with another sheet fairly, because the reader cannot know whether the difference between two totals is the site or the hour. And a count made at one site in the morning and another in the evening has let the time of day vary with the site, so the comparison is not fair. The details on the sheet are what make two sheets comparable.

### Instruments and their siting

Physical measurement uses its own instruments, each with a siting rule. A quadrat frames a known area, one square metre, thrown or placed at sampled points, so vegetation counts are per unit area and comparable. A clinometer reads the slope angle between two ranging poles held upright at the top and bottom of the slope, on the line being measured. Callipers or a pebbleometer measure stone size, the long axis, and the shape follows from comparing the axes. The sampling method, every fifth stone along the transect, say, is stated with the instrument, because the instrument measures and the sampling decides what it measures.

### River measurements

River measurements are made in a marked cross-section. Width: a tape held bank to bank at the water surface. Depth: a metre rule at equal intervals across, 0, 50, 100 centimetres, so the channel profile is drawn from the readings. Speed: a measured 10-metre distance along the bank marked with two ranging poles, a float released at the upstream pole, and a stopwatch timing it to the downstream pole, repeated three times and averaged, because one float catches or misses an eddy. Bedload: a sample of stones, measured for size with callipers and shape from the axes, at fixed points. The full method can be repeated exactly by someone who was not there, which is the standard a written method meets.

### The float timed vaguely

A speed measurement described as "we timed a float" cannot be repeated, because it names no distance and no timing. The repeatable version names everything: a 10-metre distance measured along the bank between two ranging poles, the float released at the upstream pole, the stopwatch started on release and stopped at the downstream pole, three runs averaged. The test of a method description is whether someone could repeat it exactly from the words.

### Beach measurements

A beach study measures the profile, the material and its movement. The profile: a clinometer between ranging poles set at the top and bottom of each facet of the slope, angle by angle down the beach, with the pole heights equal so the angle is read level. Pebbles: sampled at fixed points along a transect, sized with callipers, the long axis, and shape from the axes compared. Movement: marked pebbles, a painted cross or a number, left at a known point and found again after a storm or a season, so the drift along the coast is traced on the map.

### Weather measurements

Weather instruments are sited so they measure the air, not their surroundings. A rain gauge is sunk into open level ground with the funnel rim above the grass and away from trees and buildings, because a gauge under a tree misses rain and a raised gauge catches splash; it is read at the same time each day and its water poured into a graduated cylinder. The thermometers live in a Stevenson screen, a white louvred box on legs over short grass, which shades them while the air moves freely, and it stands away from walls and tarmac that hold their own heat. An anemometer measures wind speed and a wind vane shows the direction the wind blows from, both in the open, and every instrument is read at the same time daily so one day's figures compare with the next's.

### The badly sited rain gauge

A rain gauge under a tree collects the rain the tree catches first, a gauge against a wall takes splashed runoff in wet weather and shade in the reading, and a gauge on a table is not the rain the ground receives. The siting rules, open level ground, funnel above the rim, away from obstacles, are part of the measurement, and a gauge whose parts are not known cannot be read or emptied. The test is whether rain could reach it freely and be measured the same way each day.

### Human fieldwork: surveys and counts

Human fieldwork has its standard designs. A pedestrian count: two counters at each point, counting everyone passing in ten minutes, at the same points at the same time. A traffic count: vehicles classified, car, minibus, lorry, counted for ten minutes at junction points. A land-use survey: a base map of the street, each frontage classified by a category key, shop, office, service, vacant, walked once. An environmental quality survey: a score sheet of fixed criteria, litter, noise, upkeep, scored site by site on the same scale by every member of the group, so the sites compare. Every design keeps the what, the where and the when the same across sites, because the comparison is the finding.

### One enquiry, planned end to end

Plan a pedestrian study of a town centre as the whole discipline in one pass. The hypothesis first: pedestrian counts fall with distance from the market square, a claim with a measure and a direction. The sites next: the square itself and three points outward along one road, at 200-metre intervals, so the gradient has four points to draw. The sheet: a column per site, the ten-minute period printed, time, date, location and recorder at the head. The sampling for any interview: every fifth passer-by, a rule, not a choice. The timing: all four sites in the same ten-minute window, so the hour does not masquerade as the place, with the whole study repeated on a second day to steady the totals. The pilot: the interview question tried on five respondents first, so the misread wording is fixed before the day. Every choice names its reason, and the plan reads as a method another class could execute from the page.

### The fieldwork kit

The physical kit holds four instruments, each with its job. The ranging poles mark the two ends of a measured distance, on the slope for the clinometer or along the bank for the float. The clinometer reads the angle between them, eye-level to eye-level. The quadrat frames one square metre of ground, placed the same way up at each sampled point. The callipers or pebbleometer measure the stone, long axis and short, in centimetres. Round the kit out with a tape for the transect, a stopwatch for the float, a graduated cylinder for the rain gauge, and the recording sheets for everything: the kit is small, but each item answers one measurement exactly.

## Presenting the data

### The graph and its data

Each graph type suits its data. A line graph for values over time; a bar chart for categories; a histogram for grouped continuous counts; a divided bar or pie graph for shares of a total; a flow diagram for movement between places; a wind-rose for the frequency of winds from each direction; an isoline map for values spread over space; a scatter graph with a best-fit line for the relationship between two measures; a triangular graph for three shares totalling 100 per cent; a radial graph for cycles through the day; a dispersion graph for the spread of a value at many sites; a choropleth map for values by area; a kite diagram for proportions changing along a transect; a pictogram for simple totals. The data decides the graph, and the graph that distorts the data is wrong however neatly drawn.

### Completing a printed graph

Half-drawn graphs test convention as much as plotting. The completed part matches the printed part: the same scale, so the new bars rise against the same axis divisions; the key's shading, so a striped segment is striped, not solid; the key's order, so the pie's segments run clockwise from the top in the order the key lists; and bars from the correct end of the axis, so a horizontal bar chart grows from its baseline. A completed graph that ignores the printed half looks finished and reads wrong, and the test is whether it matches the others on the page.

### A completion worked through

Suppose a divided bar of a town's employment is printed with the primary and secondary segments shaded and the tertiary segment missing. The completion works in three checks. The scale: the printed segments sit on a 100 per cent bar, so the missing segment's width is the tertiary share of that whole, read off the axis. The order: the key lists primary, secondary, tertiary, and the printed segments run left to right in that order, so the missing segment joins the right-hand end, after secondary, not wherever space remains. The shading: the key's tertiary pattern is used, not a fresh one, because a completed graph that invents its own key cannot be read against the page. Three checks, and the bar is finished to the page's own conventions.

## Describing the results

### Pattern, change, exception

A results description has three moves, in order. The overall pattern first, the direction of the finding: counts fall with distance from the centre, pebbes shrink downstream. The main change next, sized with figures: from 240 pedestrians in ten minutes at site A to 45 at site D, or a mean pebble long-axis of 12.4 centimetres at site 1 against 4.8 at site 4. The exception last, named honestly: site C outruns site B, the weekend count breaks the weekday pattern. Figures carry units and the sites or dates they came from, and the geography is referred in, the description says what the pattern means about the place, not just what the numbers did.

### A description worked through

A results table reads, down a beach transect: site 1, 310 milligrams of shell per sample; site 2, 240; site 3, 150; site 4, 60. The description opens with the overall pattern, shell falls landward down the transect, then sizes it with the end figures, 310 milligrams at the seaward site against 60 at the landward, a fall of 250 milligrams, then reads the gradient, the largest single drop, 90 milligrams, between sites 2 and 3, and closes with the exception if there is one, and here no site breaks the trend. The geography is referred in as the description runs: shell thins landward because the waves sort the beach, carrying the light shell up and leaving the heavy, which is what the pattern means about the shore. Figures with units, direction with size, exception named honestly, sense attached, and the description is complete.

## Conclusions, evaluation and extensions

### The conclusion

A conclusion states whether the hypothesis is true, false or partly true, and then supports the decision with paired figures from the data. The decision first: true, false, partly true, the three words a conclusion opens with. The evidence next, in pairs: the values at two sites or two dates that show the pattern, 12.4 centimetres at site 1 against 4.8 at site 4, so the bedload does shrink downstream. A decision without figures is an opinion, and a conclusion that states no decision loses the mark the decision carries, so both halves are written, judgement then proof.

### True without proof

A conclusion that says the hypothesis is true and stops has answered nothing, because nothing in it shows why. The reader needs a figure that proves the decision: the sites, the values and the direction they run. The test is whether there is a figure in the conclusion that could not have been written by someone who never saw the data.

### The evaluation

Reliability means the method would give the same results if repeated, and the evaluation asks it of every choice. The sample: ten pebbles is a sample the sea laughs at, and fifty widens it. The sites: two sites cannot show a pattern a valley long, and eight can. The timing: a weekday survey says nothing about Saturday, and a dry-season river says nothing about the flood. The method: a float catches the surface, a flow meter reads the channel, and the error of the instrument is named. Each evaluation names what to change and why the change makes the results more reliable, more sites, a larger sample, a different time, a method less open to error, because an evaluation is a repair list, not a confession.

### "Repeat it" is not an improvement

Suggesting "repeat it" or "do it again" as the improvement changes nothing, so the same errors return with the same results. The improvement names what is done differently and what defect it fixes: repeat the count at four more sites, because two sites cannot show a fall with distance; sample fifty pebbles instead of ten, because a small sample swings on one boulder. What exactly is done differently, and what does it fix, is the pair an improvement carries.

### The extension

An extension tests something new: another place, the same enquiry on a second river to see whether the finding holds; another season, the pedestrian count repeated in December against March; another variable, the pebble survey that adds shape to size, or the quality survey that adds interviews to scores. The new enquiry answers a different question, which is what separates an extension from a rerun, and a good extension follows from the first enquiry's own findings, the pattern that surprised, the site that broke the trend, the variable that went unmeasured.

### An enquiry closed, start to finish

Close the river study as the discipline runs. The hypothesis: bedload becomes smaller and rounder downstream, measurable, directed, testable. The data: twenty stones a site, every fifth stone along a tape, callipers and a roundness scale, four sites on one day. The description: the means fall from 14.2 to 5.1 centimetres and the roundness rises from 2.1 to 3.2, smooth trends, no exceptions. The conclusions: both hypotheses true, each decision followed by its paired figures. The evaluation: fifty stones a site would steady the means, six sites would fill the gaps, two judges would dilute the roundness judgement. The extension: a second catchment, because the finding's reach is a different question from the finding itself. One enquiry, and every stage is a skill this topic has taught.
