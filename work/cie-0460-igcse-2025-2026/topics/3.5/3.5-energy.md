## Energy sources: importance, nuclear and renewables

### Fossil fuels: the non-renewables

The fossil fuels are coal, oil and natural gas, formed from ancient plant and animal remains over millions of years. They are non-renewable because we burn them far faster than new ones form: a tank of petrol takes geological time to replace. Their importance is still enormous - about 80 per cent of world energy came from them in the early 2020s (rounded figure) - because they are concentrated, easy to store and move, and can be burned whenever power is wanted. Nuclear power, from splitting uranium, is also non-renewable in the everyday sense, since uranium is a finite mined fuel.

### Energy use follows development

Energy use per person rises with a country's level of development, and the chain runs from wealth to demand. High-income countries run energy-hungry industries, fill roads with motor vehicles, and heat or cool buildings full of appliances, so a person there may use several times more energy than a person in a low-income country. Low-income countries often skip the grid altogether: fuelwood and other biomass, gathered locally, remain the main household fuels. Middle-income, industrialising countries sit between the two, and their coal use rises fastest as factories and cities grow.

### Fuelwood: the everyday fuel of low-income countries

Fuelwood matters because it is the energy source that hundreds of millions of households actually use. In many low-income countries it cooks the meals and heats the water; it is gathered rather than bought, so it reaches homes no grid or fuel market has reached. Its costs are paid in time and in trees: hours of collection, and deforestation around growing towns.

### Nuclear power and the renewables

Nuclear power generates roughly a tenth of the world's electricity (early 2020s, rounded), mostly in higher-income countries. Its fuel is uranium, and a small volume yields a very large output. The renewable supplies are the ones not used up: solar power, wind power, hydro-electric power (HEP), geothermal energy from hot rocks, tidal and wave power, and biofuels from crops. Their importance varies with a country's landscape - mountain rivers for HEP, sunny deserts for solar, windy coasts for turbines - and their share of world electricity has been rising quickly.

### Petrol and HEP are not fossil fuels

A common error lists petrol or hydro-electric power among the fossil fuels. The fossil fuels are coal, oil and natural gas alone. Petrol is refined from oil, so it is a product of a fossil fuel rather than one of the raw fuels, and HEP is renewable because flowing water is not used up. The test: is it a raw fuel formed from ancient remains? If not, it does not belong on the list.

### What nuclear power offers

Nuclear power's benefits are scale and cleanliness at the point of generation. One station generates a large, steady output for decades, so a few stations can supply a whole grid, whatever the weather. Generation releases no carbon dioxide, so nuclear does not add to the enhanced greenhouse effect the way burning coal or gas does. And the fuel volumes are small, so deliveries are light compared with coal trains.

### What nuclear power costs

The disadvantages are serious. Radioactive waste must be stored safely for very long periods, because it stays hazardous far beyond a human lifetime, and no country finds that easy. Stations are very expensive to build, and later expensive to take apart at the end of their lives. And an accident, though rare, can spread contamination over a wide area - Chernobyl in 1986 and Fukushima in 2011 are the reasons many people hesitate.

### Renewables: which cost is lower?

Renewable sources are cheaper to run once built, because they need no fuel - no coal trains, no gas pipelines, no uranium - but the installations themselves are often expensive to build, so which cost is lower must always be said. Their other weakness is intermittency: the wind drops, the sun sets, and dry years cut HEP output, so wind and solar need back-up stations or storage, which adds to the building bill. Where a country has sunshine, wind or mountain rivers, renewables can generate without carbon dioxide and without fuel imports; the choice between sources weighs building costs, running costs, reliability and climate impact.

### Greenhouse or ozone?

Carbon dioxide from burning fossil fuels enhances the greenhouse effect, trapping more heat and warming the climate. Some learners write that it thins the ozone layer instead - a different problem, caused mainly by CFC gases. The two are not the same thing: one warms the lower atmosphere, the other lets more ultraviolet through the stratosphere, and their remedies differ.

## Case study: energy supply in Namibia

### Namibia: what the supply is

Namibia's largest domestic power station is the hydro plant at Ruacana, on the Kunene River in the far north-west, with a capacity of about 340 megawatts (2010s figure). Most of the rest of the country's generating need is not generated at home: roughly half of Namibia's electricity was imported in the 2010s (rounded), from the grids of neighbouring countries. Solar farms have been added since the mid-2010s, sensible in a country with more than 3 000 hours of sunshine a year.

### Why the supply is this way

The chain runs from geography to supply. The population is small - about 2.5 million - and thinly spread, so the domestic market is too small to repay many large power stations. The country's rivers are mostly dry or seasonal; the Kunene, on the Angolan border, is one of the few with reliable flow worth damming, which is why Ruacana sits there. Importing from neighbouring grids has cost less than building large new stations, so imports filled the gap. And the sunshine is abundant, which is why solar generation, cheap to run once the farms are built because it needs no fuel, is the fastest-growing domestic source.

### The judgement the case study teaches

Namibia shows the trade-offs of 3.5.1 in one place. Imports cost money that leaves the country and leave supply dependent on neighbours, so the country gains by generating more at home. But new stations are expensive to build, and the market is small, so each plant must fit what the country can repay. Solar fits that test: its building costs per unit have fallen, its running costs are low because no fuel is bought, and the resource - sunshine - is the best in the region. The result is a supply moving from imports and one hydro station towards solar, without pretending the transition is free.
