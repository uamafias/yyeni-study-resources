## Normal

Question: Solve $\sin^{-1}(4x)=-\frac\pi6$, giving an exact solution, or explain why it has no real solution. [3]

Action: establish the relevant inverse property. Reason: the principal output of inverse sine lies in $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$. Result: $-\pi/6$ belongs to that interval [P-B1]. Apply it to the equation by taking sine:
$$4x=\sin\!\left(-\frac\pi6\right)=-\frac12.\qquad\text{[P-M1]}$$
Divide by four to obtain $x=-1/8$ [P-A1]. Total3. Independent inverse substitution gives $\sin^{-1}(4\cdot(-1/8))=-\pi/6$. A correct decimal intermediate $4x=-0.5$ is permitted; exact terminating decimal $x=-0.125$ equals the exact fraction and is accepted. A naked value earns zero, not implied-method credit.

## Changed

Question: Solve $\sin^{-1}(3x)=-\frac{3\pi}4$, giving an exact solution, or explain why it has no real solution. [3]

The principal inverse-sine interval is $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ [P-B1]. The actual target satisfies $-3\pi/4<-\pi/2$ [P-M1]. Hence no real solution exists [P-A1], total3.

A full valid alternative avoids a rote interval statement. Taking sine forces the only possible forward candidate:
$$3x=\sin\!\left(-\frac{3\pi}4\right)=-\frac{\sqrt2}{2},\qquad x=-\frac{\sqrt2}{6}.$$
But its actual principal inverse value is
$$\sin^{-1}\!\left(3\cdot\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{6}\right)\right)=-\frac\pi4\ne-\frac{3\pi}4.$$
The correct principal evaluation fills P-B1, forcing and rejecting the sole candidate fills P-M1, and the justified no-solution conclusion fills P-A1:3 marks. One failed arbitrary guess would not prove the conclusion.

## Admitted boundary

Question: Solve $\sin^{-1}(5x)=\frac\pi3$, giving an exact solution, or explain why it has no real solution. [3]

The target is inside $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ [B1]. Apply the exact special-angle value:
$$5x=\sin\!\left(\frac\pi3\right)=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}.\qquad\text{[M1]}$$
Thus $x=\sqrt3/10$ [A1], total3. The inverse check holds. A method line such as $5x=0.866\ldots$ can earn M1; the final exact-form award still requires an exact equivalent. This marks the method and final form separately, as the Cambridge source permits a decimal forward ratio.
