## Osmosis

### Key points

| Learn | |
|---|---|
| Osmosis | net movement of water from a dilute to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane |
| Plant cell, more dilute outside | water in, turgid; the wall stops it bursting |
| Plant cell, more concentrated outside | water out, flaccid, then plasmolysed (membrane pulls away from the wall) |
| Animal cell | no wall: swells and can burst, or shrinks and shrivels |
| Words | turgid, flaccid, plasmolysed describe cells; hypotonic, isotonic, hypertonic describe solutions |
| Roots | water enters root hairs by osmosis and moves down a water potential gradient to the xylem |

### Osmosis and water potential

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a dilute solution to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane. Net means overall: water molecules cross the membrane in both directions, but more cross from the dilute side, so water builds up on the concentrated side. The membrane lets water molecules through but holds back larger solute molecules such as sucrose. Biologists describe the same idea with water potential: water moves by osmosis from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential. Water potential depends mainly on how dilute a solution is, and also on pressure. At the same pressure, a dilute solution has a higher water potential than a concentrated one.

### Plant cells in a more dilute solution

In a plant cell the cell membrane is the partially permeable membrane and the cell sap in the vacuole is the solution inside. Put the cell into a hypotonic solution, one more dilute than its cell sap, and water enters by osmosis. The vacuole swells and pushes the cytoplasm against the cell wall. This outward push is turgor pressure, and the wall pushes back. The cell becomes turgid but does not burst, because the wall is strong. In an isotonic solution, with the same concentration as the cell sap, there is no net movement of water and the cell stays the same size.

### Plant cells in a more concentrated solution

Put the same cell into a hypertonic solution, one more concentrated than its cell sap, and water leaves by osmosis. The vacuole shrinks and the cell becomes flaccid, or soft. If water keeps leaving, the cell membrane, with the cytoplasm inside it, pulls away from the cell wall. The cell is then plasmolysed. The space between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the wall lets it through.

### Animal cells have no wall

An animal cell, such as a red blood cell, also gains or loses water by osmosis, but it has no cell wall. In a solution more dilute than its cytoplasm it takes in water and swells, and with nothing to resist the swelling it can burst. In a solution more concentrated than its cytoplasm it loses water and shrinks, becoming shrivelled. In an isotonic solution it keeps its shape. This is why fluids given to patients through a drip have the same concentration as blood plasma.

### Cell words and solution words

Two sets of words are easy to mix up. Turgid, flaccid and plasmolysed describe the cell. Hypertonic, isotonic and hypotonic describe the solution, compared with the cell contents. In an answer about osmosis, use a solution word for the solution and a cell word for the cell.

### How roots take in water

Soil water is usually more dilute than the cell sap of a root hair cell, so it usually has a higher water potential, and water enters the root hair by osmosis. From there water moves across the root towards the xylem, down a water potential gradient. Some of it travels through the cells and some along the cell walls between them. The xylem then carries it up the plant. The gradient can be reversed. If the soil water becomes more concentrated than the root cell sap, its water potential is lower, and water leaves the roots by osmosis: the plant can wilt even though the soil is wet. Adding too much fertiliser is one way this can happen.

### How turgor pressure supports a plant

A young stem of pumpkin or a leaf of spinach has no wood to hold it up. It stays firm because its cells are turgid: water has entered by osmosis, each vacuole has swollen, and the turgor pressure of each cell pushes its contents against its wall. Turgid cells press against one another like tightly packed balloons, and the whole stem or leaf becomes firm. When the plant loses more water than it takes in, the cells become flaccid and the soft parts droop.

### Seeing plasmolysis under the microscope

Peel a thin strip of epidermis from the inside of an onion scale leaf. Mount one piece in a drop of water and another in a drop of concentrated salt solution, add coverslips and leave both for about ten minutes. Under the microscope the cells in water are turgid, with the cytoplasm pressed against the walls. The cells in salt solution are plasmolysed: the cell membrane and cytoplasm have pulled away from the walls. Red onion makes this easier to see, because the coloured cell sap shows the shrinking vacuole. To turn this into an investigation, mount strips in a range of salt concentrations, wait the same time for each, and count the percentage of cells plasmolysed in a field of view.

### Investigating osmosis with potato cylinders

Plan it first. The independent variable is the concentration of the solution; the dependent variable is the percentage change in mass; keep the size of the cylinders, the time, the temperature and the potato the same, and repeat each concentration. Then cut cylinders of potato of the same length and diameter with a cork borer. Blot each one dry and weigh it. Put one cylinder into each of a range of sucrose solutions, for example from 0.0 to 0.8 mol/dm³, for the same time and at the same temperature. Then remove each cylinder, blot it in the same way and weigh it again. A gain in mass means water entered the cells; a loss means water left.

### Worked example: percentage change in mass

A potato cylinder weighs 2.00 g at the start and 2.16 g after 30 minutes in 0.1 mol/dm³ sucrose. The change is 2.16 - 2.00 = 0.16 g. As a percentage of the starting mass, 0.16 ÷ 2.00 = 0.08, and 0.08 × 100 = 8, so the cylinder gained 8%. Percentages let you compare cylinders that started at different masses. If a second cylinder lost mass in 0.3 mol/dm³, the concentration of the cell contents lies between 0.1 and 0.3 mol/dm³: the solution with no change in mass would match it.

### A model cell made of Visking tubing

Visking tubing is a partially permeable membrane. Tie one end, fill the tube with sucrose solution, tie the other end and weigh it. Leave it in a beaker of water. Water enters by osmosis, so the bag swells, becomes firm and gains mass, much as a plant cell becomes turgid. A bag of water placed in sucrose solution does the opposite.

### Making the results trustworthy

Solution left on the surface of a piece of tissue adds mass that the cells did not take up, so blot every piece in the same way before each weighing. One piece can behave unusually, so repeat each concentration and work out the mean. Water moves slowly, so give the tissue enough time in each solution, and keep the time the same for every piece. If an expected change is not seen, think about what else could explain it: too little time, or a solution that is not concentrated enough.

### Where this connects

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion (3.3.1), for water through a partially permeable membrane. Active transport (3.3.3) moves particles the other way, against a gradient. Water uptake by roots continues in Transport in plants (3.7), which explains transpiration and wilting. The skills the investigations use, naming the independent, dependent and controlled variables, setting out a results table and working out a percentage change, are taught in Scientific processes (1.1 and 1.2).

## Active transport

### Key points

| Learn | |
|---|---|
| Active transport | particles move against a concentration gradient, using energy from respiration, through carrier proteins |
| Where it matters | ions into root hairs; glucose into villus cells; glucose reabsorbed in kidney tubules |
| What stops it | anything that stops respiration, such as no oxygen in waterlogged soil |
| Diffusion and osmosis | down a gradient, no energy from respiration |

### Active transport

Active transport is the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration using energy from respiration.

### How carrier proteins move particles

Active transport is done by carrier proteins in the cell membrane. A particle binds to a carrier protein that fits it, on one side of the membrane. The carrier changes shape and moves the particle across, releasing it on the other side. The energy for this comes from respiration: some carriers use it directly, others use it indirectly, but either way active transport stops when respiration stops. Cells that do a lot of active transport contain many mitochondria, which release this energy.

### Root hair cells

Mineral ions such as nitrate are often more concentrated inside root hair cells than in the soil water around them. Diffusion would carry them out, not in, so root hair cells take them in against the concentration gradient by active transport. This needs energy from respiration, so roots need oxygen. In waterlogged soil, which holds little oxygen, the roots take up fewer ions, and crops can show signs of mineral deficiency.

### The villi and the kidney tubules

Epithelial cells of the villi in the small intestine take in glucose by active transport. Glucose can then be absorbed even when there is less of it in the gut than in the cells, so little is left to be lost. Cells lining the kidney tubules reabsorb glucose in the same way, returning it to the blood so that it is not lost in the urine.

### Three ways across a membrane

The table sets the three processes side by side.

| | Diffusion | Osmosis | Active transport |
|---|---|---|---|
| What moves | particles of a gas or solute | water molecules | particles such as ions or glucose |
| Direction | down a concentration gradient | from a dilute to a more concentrated solution | against a concentration gradient |
| Energy from respiration | not needed | not needed | needed |
| Proteins in the membrane | none needed for simple diffusion | water also passes through water channel proteins | carrier proteins needed |

A quick test: ask which way the particles go compared with their gradient. Down the gradient, the cell spends no energy from respiration. Against the gradient, it must.

### Where this connects

A concentration gradient, and diffusion down one, are explained in Diffusion (3.3.1). Mitochondria and their job are in Cell structure (3.2). Root hairs return in Transport in plants (3.7), the villi in Absorption (3.6.2.4), and the kidney in Excretion in humans (3.11).

### Not every movement needs energy

Only active transport needs energy from respiration, because it moves particles against a concentration gradient. Diffusion and osmosis move particles down a gradient, from where there are more to where there are fewer, and need no energy from respiration.

(a) Identify the independent variable and the dependent variable. [2]
(b) Complete the table by calculating the three missing percentage changes. [3]
(c) Determine, from the table, the range of concentrations that contains the concentration of the potato cell contents, and explain your answer. [2]
(d) State one variable, other than time, that was kept the same. [1]
(e) Suggest one source of error in this method and an improvement for it. [2]

**Answer.** (a) Independent: the concentration of the sucrose solution. Dependent: the percentage change in mass.
(b) 0.2: 2.52 - 2.40 = 0.12 g, and 0.12 ÷ 2.40 = 0.05, which is +5%. 0.4: 2.45 - 2.50 = -0.05 g, and 0.05 ÷ 2.50 = 0.02, which is -2%. 0.6: 2.34 - 2.60 = -0.26 g, and 0.26 ÷ 2.60 = 0.10, which is -10%.
(c) Between 0.2 and 0.4 mol/dm³: the cylinder gained mass at 0.2 and lost mass at 0.4, so the solution matching the cell contents lies between them.
(d) Temperature, or the size of the cylinders, or the potato they were cut from.
(e) Only one cylinder was used at each concentration, so one unusual cylinder could mislead; repeat each concentration three times and calculate the mean.

- (a) 2 marks: 1 mark for each variable correctly identified.
- (b) 3 marks: one mark per correct percentage with its sign; the table headings already carry the units.
- (c) 2 marks: 1 mark for the range from the data; 1 mark for the conclusion that no change in mass occurs where the solution matches the cell contents.
- (d) 1 mark: any one variable the method states was kept the same: the size of the cylinders, the temperature or the potato used.
- (e) 2 marks: 1 mark for a source of error; 1 mark for an improvement tied to that error.
