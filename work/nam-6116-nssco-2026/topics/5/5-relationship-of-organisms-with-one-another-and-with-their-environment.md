## 5.1 Energy flow

### Key points

| Role or diagram | Key idea |
|---|---|
| Producer | makes organic nutrients, usually by photosynthesis |
| Consumer | gets energy by feeding on other organisms |
| Decomposer | gets energy from dead and waste organic material |
| Food chain or web | one route, or linked routes, of energy transfer |
| Pyramid of biomass | dry mass at each feeding level at one moment |

Energy enters most ecosystems as light and leaves as heat. Less energy is left at each feeding step because some material is not eaten, some is egested, and much is used in respiration and lost as heat, so chains are short. Arrows point from the food to the feeder. A pyramid of biomass shows mass at one moment, not the rate of energy flow.

### Energy from the Sun

Sunlight is the energy source for most food chains. Photosynthesis transfers light energy into chemical energy stored in organic substances such as sugars and starch.

When organisms feed, some of that chemical energy passes along the food chain. Respiration and other activities transfer energy to the surroundings as heat, so an ecosystem needs a continuing supply of energy from the Sun.

Energy flows through an ecosystem in one direction: from the Sun into producers, along the food chain by feeding, and out to the surroundings as heat. Matter such as carbon and nitrogen is recycled, but the heat is not recycled by plants into the food chain. This is why the Sun must keep supplying energy.

Producers respire as well as photosynthesise, so even a grass plant transfers some energy to the surroundings as heat. A mammal such as a lion keeps its body warmer than its surroundings and so transfers a great deal of heat. When we say that energy is "lost" from a food chain, we mean it is no longer available to the next feeder. It has not been destroyed.

A good description of energy flow names three stages in order: light energy becomes chemical energy in photosynthesis, feeding passes some chemical energy on, and respiration and activity transfer energy to the surroundings as heat.

### Producer

A producer is an organism that makes its own organic nutrients, usually using energy from sunlight, through photosynthesis.

Producers supply organic material for food chains. They also respire and use some of that material themselves.

A grass plant makes sugars from carbon dioxide and water using light energy. It does not obtain its organic food by absorbing it from the soil. Mineral ions such as nitrate are nutrients in a different sense: they are needed to build proteins but do not supply energy.

Producers include trees, grasses, pond algae and the microscopic phytoplankton of the sea. Off the coast of Namibia, the cold, nutrient-rich water of the Benguela upwelling supports large numbers of phytoplankton. These producers are the base of food chains that lead to fish, seabirds and Cape fur seals.

A common mistake is to call any organism at the bottom of a diagram a producer. Check how it gets its food. A producer makes its food by photosynthesis, while a fungus on a dead leaf, even at the start of a decay chain, is a decomposer.

### Consumer

A consumer is an organism that gets its energy by feeding on other organisms.

A consumer obtains organic material by feeding. It does not make its whole organic food supply from inorganic substances by photosynthesis.

A grasshopper eating grass is a primary consumer. A lizard eating the grasshopper is a secondary consumer. On the savanna, a springbok eating grass is a primary consumer and a cheetah that catches the springbok is a secondary consumer. The level of a consumer depends on what it eats, not on how large it is.

An organism can occupy more than one trophic level in a food web if it eats different foods. A jackal that eats fruit is a primary consumer at that moment, and one that eats a mouse is a secondary consumer.

### Food chain

A food chain shows the transfer of energy from one organism to the next, beginning with a producer.

Arrows point from the food organism towards the feeder that receives its energy, not from a predator towards the prey it attacks.

If grass is eaten by a grasshopper, which is eaten by a lizard, the food chain is grass → grasshopper → lizard. Each arrow means "energy passes to". A sea food chain off Namibia could be phytoplankton → zooplankton → anchovy → Cape fur seal.

A chain is a simplified route. It leaves out the other foods each organism eats and the decomposers that act on dead material, so it shows one pathway through an ecosystem and not the whole of it.

### Constructing a supported food chain

A food chain built from feeding observations must begin with a producer, follow what actually eats what, and have arrows pointing towards the consumer.

Two organisms living in the same habitat are not necessarily linked in a chain. Evidence that one feeds on the other is needed.

Start by finding the photosynthetic organism. Then find what eats it, and then what eats that consumer. For example, from the observations that beetle larvae eat grass roots and a lizard eats the larvae, the chain is grass → beetle larva → lizard.

If an observation mentions an organism that does not link to the others, leave it out. A beetle that feeds on dead wood is a detritus feeder and belongs to a different chain. If two different animals both eat the same food, you can draw two valid chains, so always use only the links the evidence supports.

### Food web

A food web is a network of interconnected food chains.

One organism may have several food sources and several predators, linking different energy-transfer routes.

A bird that eats both seeds and insects joins a producer-to-consumer chain to another consumer chain. If one food source is removed, the effect passes through several links. The result depends on whether the animal can switch to other foods and on how much other animals compete for them.

When you read a web, follow the arrows. The arrows show energy transfer, so an organism drawn near the top of a diagram is not necessarily a top consumer. Count the links from the producer to find its level.

### Herbivore

A herbivore is an organism that gets its energy by eating plants.

In a chain that begins with a green plant, the herbivore is the primary consumer.

"Herbivore" describes the food source and not the size of the animal. A large antelope and a tiny leaf-eating insect are both herbivores. Plants contain chemical energy, but the herbivore must still digest the food and absorb the products before its cells can use them.

### Carnivore

A carnivore is an animal that gets its energy by eating other animals.

A carnivore can occupy different consumer levels depending on what its prey eats.

A spider that eats a plant-eating insect is a secondary consumer in that chain. A bird that eats the spider is a tertiary consumer. The level depends on how many feeding steps lie between the animal and the producer, so the biggest animal in a diagram is not automatically a carnivore at the top level. Animals that eat both plants and animals are omnivores.

### Decomposer

A decomposer is an organism that gets its energy from dead or waste organic material.

Many bacteria and fungi are decomposers. They release soluble products and mineral ions, and their respiration transfers carbon back to the environment as carbon dioxide.

Decomposers do not make food from sunlight. A fungus releases enzymes onto dead leaves, digests them outside its cells and absorbs the products. This is why dead material does not pile up in an ecosystem. Decomposers act on material from every trophic level, including dead plants, dead animals, faeces and leaf litter.

Animals such as beetles and earthworms that eat dead material speed up decay by breaking it into smaller pieces, but bacteria and fungi do most of the chemical breakdown that recycles mineral ions.

### Trophic level

Trophic level is the position of an organism in a food chain, food web, pyramid of numbers or pyramid of biomass.

Producers form the first level, followed by primary, secondary and further consumers along a stated feeding route.

An omnivore can occupy different levels in different chains. The position of a bar in a pyramid shows the feeding level and has nothing to do with the physical height of the organisms. Trace the feeding links before you name a level, and do not confuse primary production (the producers) with primary consumption (the herbivores).

### Naming trophic positions

Producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, tertiary consumer and quaternary consumer identify successive positions along a food chain.

The prefix counts consumer steps after the producer: a quaternary consumer is the fourth consumer and sits at the fifth trophic level.

In the chain grass → caterpillar → predatory beetle → lizard → hawk, the caterpillar is the primary consumer, the beetle is secondary, the lizard is tertiary and the hawk is quaternary. Most natural chains are shorter than this.

Pyramids of numbers count the organisms at each level. A pyramid of numbers can be an odd shape, for example when one large tree supports thousands of insects, because the count ignores body size. A pyramid of biomass shows the dry mass at each level, so it takes size into account.

### Transfer between trophic levels

Food transfers chemical energy to the next consumer, but some material is uneaten or egested, and some energy is used in respiration and transferred to the surroundings as heat.

The energy retained in new consumer biomass is therefore smaller than the energy in the food that was available.

There are three routes for the energy in food. Some is egested as faeces, and this material goes on to decomposers. Some is used in respiration to power movement, growth and keeping warm, and ends up as heat. Only the remainder is stored in new biomass, which is what the next feeder can eat.

Worked example. A consumer eats food containing 400 kJ. It egests 120 kJ and uses 240 kJ in respiration. The energy left for new biomass is 400 − 120 − 240 = 40 kJ, which is 10% of the energy eaten. The percentage that reaches the next level varies from one food chain to another. Ten per cent is a useful approximation, not a fixed law.

### Limits to chain length

At each trophic transfer less energy remains in new biomass for the next consumer level.

This limits how much biomass and how many top consumers a producer base can support, so food chains usually have fewer than five trophic levels.

Chain length is not set by a rule against a fifth level. It is set by the energy available at the base, how efficiently energy passes between levels, and how much energy a top consumer needs. A top predator with a large territory, such as a lion, can draw on a broad base of producers through many herbivores.

Worked example. A base supplies 1000 kJ per year and each transfer keeps 20%. The next levels receive 200 kJ, then 40 kJ, then 8 kJ. If a population of consumers needs 30 kJ per year to survive, the primary and secondary consumer levels (200 kJ and 40 kJ) can be supported but a tertiary consumer level (8 kJ) cannot. Real systems have different percentages, so calculations always use the fraction the question states.

### Biomass pyramids

A pyramid of biomass shows the dry mass of living material at each trophic level, with the width of each bar proportional to dry mass per unit area or volume.

Most land pyramids narrow upwards. A pyramid of biomass is a snapshot of standing mass, which is not the same as the rate of energy flow, and it can be inverted where producers reproduce very quickly.

Read the labels and units before comparing bar widths. A wide producer bar shows mass, not a large number of plants. If producers have 800 g/m² and primary consumers have 80 g/m², the producers have ten times the mass. This tells you the mass at that moment. It does not show the percentage of energy passed on in a year.

In the sea, an inverted pyramid can occur. Phytoplankton have a small mass at any moment because they reproduce and are eaten within days, yet they support a larger standing mass of zooplankton and fish.

### Food supply and trophic efficiency

Eating suitable crop plants directly avoids an extra trophic transfer through livestock, with its respiratory and material losses.

Feeding edible crops to animals usually leaves less of their energy available as human food, although land suitability, nutrition and the use of inedible material also matter.

Energy is lost at every level. If people eat maize directly, they gain from the first transfer only. If the maize is fed to cattle and people then eat the beef, there is an extra transfer with respiration, heat and uneaten material. So the same land can feed more people when people eat crops directly.

There are good reasons to keep livestock as well. In much of Namibia, rainfall is too low and too unreliable for crops, but cattle, goats and sheep can turn the rangeland vegetation, which people cannot digest, into meat and milk. Animal products also supply good-quality protein and some minerals. Which route is better depends on the land, the crop and the needs of the people.

## 5.2 Nitrogen and carbon cycles

### Key points

| Nitrogen process | Organisms and conditions |
|---|---|
| Fixation | bacteria turn nitrogen gas into compounds |
| Decomposition | decomposers release ammonium from dead material |
| Nitrification | bacteria turn ammonium through nitrite into nitrate, with oxygen |
| Denitrification | bacteria turn nitrate into nitrogen gas, without oxygen |

Carbon moves in by photosynthesis, along food chains by feeding, and back to the air by respiration, decomposition and combustion. Fossilisation locks carbon away for a very long time. Matter cycles, but energy leaves as heat.

### Overview of the cycles

Nitrogen moves through air, soil and organisms. Fixation makes atmospheric nitrogen available in compounds, plants take up mineral nitrogen, and feeding passes organic nitrogen to animals.

Decomposition returns nitrogen compounds from dead and waste material, nitrification forms nitrates in oxygenated conditions, and denitrification returns nitrogen gas to the air in oxygen-poor conditions.

Plants need nitrogen to make amino acids and proteins. Nitrogen gas makes up about four-fifths of the air, but plants cannot use it directly. Microorganisms carry out the key conversions that make nitrogen usable and return it to the air.

Follow the chemical form at each stage. Nitrogen in a protein is not yet nitrate, and nitrogen gas is not yet ammonium. Decomposition and the other cycles move matter back into reusable forms. They do not recycle energy.

The later sections follow carbon in the same way, through photosynthesis, feeding, respiration, decomposition, fossilisation and combustion.

### Decomposition and ammonium

Decomposer microorganisms break down nitrogen-containing dead and waste organic material and release ammonium compounds.

This supplies a form of nitrogen that other bacteria can convert further.

Their enzymes act on organic material such as proteins and urea. Decomposition is not the same as taking nitrogen gas from the air, which is fixation. Decomposers work faster in warm, moist, oxygenated soil. Waterlogged or very dry soil slows them down.

### Nitrification

Nitrifying microorganisms convert ammonium compounds into nitrites and then nitrates.

Oxygen is needed for this conversion, and it makes nitrate available for plants to take up.

Keep nitrification separate from fixation. In nitrification the nitrogen is already combined in ammonium, not in nitrogen gas. The nitrate that results can be absorbed by roots, but it is also very soluble and can be washed out of the soil by rain, which is called leaching.

### Nitrogen fixation

Nitrogen-fixing microorganisms convert atmospheric nitrogen gas into nitrogen compounds that can enter living things.

Some live freely in the soil, and others live in root nodules of legumes such as peas, beans and cowpeas.

The bacteria in root nodules receive sugars from the legume, and the legume receives nitrogen compounds from the bacteria. Cowpeas, which are grown in northern Namibia, are an example of a legume that can add usable nitrogen to the soil. The amount depends on healthy nodules and suitable conditions.

The plant does not absorb nitrogen gas as if it were nitrate. The nitrogen reaches the next crop mainly when the legume residues decompose in the soil.

### Denitrification

Denitrifying microorganisms convert nitrate to nitrogen gas in oxygen-poor conditions.

This returns nitrogen to the atmosphere and can reduce the nitrate available to plants.

Denitrification is not nitrification in reverse. It happens in soil with little oxygen, such as soil that is waterlogged after heavy rain. In those conditions, denitrifying bacteria use nitrate in place of oxygen, and nitrogen gas escapes into the air. For a farmer, this means a waterlogged field can lose nitrate that was meant for the crop.

### Photosynthesis and carbon

Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the air or water and incorporates its carbon into organic compounds in producers.

That carbon can become sugars, starch, cellulose and other biological material.

The carbon comes from carbon dioxide, and light supplies the energy. Plants also respire, so whether a plant takes in or gives out carbon dioxide overall depends on the balance of photosynthesis and respiration. In bright light, a leaf takes in more carbon dioxide than it releases. In the dark, only respiration continues.

### Respiration and carbon

Respiration of organic substances can release carbon dioxide to the environment from producers, consumers and decomposers.

This returns carbon from living material to the pool that photosynthesis draws on.

Respiration is not just breathing in animals. Plants respire in light and in darkness, and so do fungi and bacteria. The breakdown of glucose in aerobic respiration releases energy and produces carbon dioxide and water.

### Feeding and carbon

Feeding transfers carbon-containing organic material from one organism to another.

Some of that material is absorbed and built into the consumer's body, while some is egested or respired.

A feeding arrow shows the transfer of matter as well as energy. Carbon that enters an animal is not all kept. Some becomes body tissue, some is respired as carbon dioxide and some leaves in faeces. Faeces and dead animals then pass carbon to decomposers.

### Decomposition and carbon dioxide

Decomposers process dead and waste organic material, using some of its carbon compounds in respiration.

Aerobic decomposition releases carbon dioxide, returning carbon to the environment, while some carbon is built into decomposer bodies or remains in the soil.

Decomposition slows when the temperature is low, when the material is too dry or waterlogged, or when oxygen is short. A fallen log does not turn into gas at once. It breaks down gradually, and its carbon moves into the air, into fungi and bacteria, and into the soil.

### Fossilisation and carbon

In particular burial and preservation conditions, some ancient organic carbon avoids complete decomposition and enters long-term geological stores.

Over geological time this can form fossil fuels, removing that carbon from short-term biological exchange until it is extracted and burnt.

Most dead organisms decompose and never become fuel. Fossil fuels formed from remains that were buried without much oxygen and then changed over millions of years. Coal formed mainly from plant material, and oil and natural gas formed mainly from tiny marine organisms.

### Combustion and carbon

Combustion of carbon-containing fuels releases carbon dioxide because the carbon is oxidised.

Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from long-term stores into the atmosphere on a much shorter timescale.

Complete combustion uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water. Wood, charcoal, coal and petrol all release carbon dioxide when they burn. Burning does not create carbon from nothing. It moves carbon that was stored for a long time into the air.

### Fossil fuels and the balance of gases

Burning fossil fuels uses oxygen and releases carbon dioxide, transferring carbon from a long-term store into the air.

If the carbon dioxide released is more than the amount removed, the concentration in the atmosphere rises. The effect depends on the whole balance and not on one source.

Burning uses up oxygen, but the air holds so much oxygen that the overall change in atmospheric oxygen is very small. The important change is the extra carbon dioxide. Compare the rates of release, uptake and storage when you judge the effect on the atmosphere.

Worked example. A model atmosphere was in balance until fuel burning added 9 carbon units each year. Extra uptake by plants and the sea now removes 6 units a year. The net gain is 9 − 6 = +3 units a year, so the carbon in the air keeps increasing, only more slowly. The system is in balance only when uptake equals release.

### Forest removal and carbon balance

Deforestation reduces the uptake of carbon dioxide by photosynthesis and can release stored carbon when trees are burnt or decay.

Cleared land also loses habitat and soil protection. The net effect on the air depends on how much carbon is released and how much new growth takes up.

Fewer trees means less photosynthesis, so less oxygen is released and less carbon dioxide is removed from the air. Burning or decay of the felled trees uses oxygen and releases carbon dioxide. As with fossil fuels, the oxygen used is a very small fraction of the oxygen in the air, so the percentage change in atmospheric oxygen is tiny.

A forest holds carbon in three places: the standing trees, the soil and the dead material on the ground. Removing trees reduces the current carbon store, and it reduces the amount that will be taken up each year in future.

Planting seedlings starts to replace the uptake, but a young plantation takes many years to match the carbon store, the habitat and the soil conditions of an old forest. Planting one seedling is not an immediate equal replacement for a felled tree.

### Long-term increase in carbon dioxide

A sustained increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide strengthens heat retention and can change the climate, affecting temperature, rainfall and the movement of carbon through living things.

Changing conditions can alter photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition and carbon storage, so any feedback needs its direction and its conditions stated.

Warmer conditions can speed up decomposition if there is enough water. Drought can slow decomposers and photosynthesis. More carbon dioxide does not guarantee more plant growth if water, mineral ions or another factor is limiting. Namibia has a dry climate with variable rainfall, so water is often the factor that limits plant growth.

Other effects follow from warming. Rainfall patterns change, so some regions have more droughts and others more floods. Melting ice and the expansion of warmer sea water raise sea levels. Species may lose the conditions they need where they live.

## 5.3 Populations

### Key points

A population is one species in one area at one time. A community is all the populations in an ecosystem, and an ecosystem also includes the physical environment. Births and immigration add individuals, and deaths and emigration remove them. An S-shaped curve levels off when limiting factors act. On a graph, height shows size and slope shows the change per unit time. Human impact depends on the number of people, on what each person uses, and on how resources and waste are managed.

### What a population is

A population is a group of organisms of one species that live in the same area at the same time.

A count needs its species, area and time to be stated. Changing any of them changes which population is being described.

All the springbok in Etosha National Park during one survey year form a population. If you count several species together, you are describing part of a community instead. Counting the animals around one waterhole does not give the whole population of the park, because the animals are spread over a large area.

### Population growth factors

Births and immigration add individuals to a population. Deaths and emigration remove them.

Food, water, space, disease, predation and other conditions change these rates and so affect net population growth.

A population can grow without any births if immigration is high enough. A population with a high birth rate can still shrink if deaths and emigration are higher. To judge the change in size, work out additions minus removals over a stated time.

Worked example. A population of 200 antelope in a reserve has 40 births, 15 deaths, 5 immigrants and 10 emigrants in a year. Additions are 40 + 5 = 45 and removals are 15 + 10 = 25, so the population grows by 20 to 220.

### Community

A community is all of the populations of different species in an ecosystem.

It includes the living populations only. The non-living water, soil, light and temperature are part of the wider ecosystem.

The fish, algae, insects and microorganisms in a pond form a community of interacting populations. A list of fish alone is not the whole community. A water temperature reading is a physical factor of the environment and not a population. A community study must therefore include several groups of organisms and the links between them.

### Ecosystem

An ecosystem is a unit containing the community of organisms and their environment, interacting together.

A decomposing log or a pond can be studied as an ecosystem, because organisms interact with each other and with physical and chemical conditions.

Light, temperature, dissolved gases and nutrients affect the organisms. The organisms in turn change the shade, the oxygen and the nutrient supply. An ecosystem is therefore more than a place name or a list of animals. The Namib Desert is an ecosystem. Its organisms, such as Welwitschia plants and fog-basking beetles, are adapted to the dry conditions and the fog that drifts in from the Atlantic Ocean.

### Sigmoid population growth

Where resources are limited, a population grows through a lag phase, an exponential (log) phase and a stationary phase, and may then enter a death phase.

As the population becomes denser, shortage of resources or other pressures reduce net growth. The carrying capacity depends on conditions and is not a permanent fixed number.

The curve is S-shaped, which is the meaning of sigmoid. In the lag phase there are few breeding individuals and they are still settling in and maturing, so growth is slow. In the exponential (log) phase resources are plentiful and limiting factors are weak, so births greatly exceed deaths and numbers rise steeply. In the stationary phase food, water, space, disease or predation limit growth, births roughly equal deaths, and the curve levels off. If a resource runs out or waste builds up, deaths exceed births and the population falls: this is the death phase.

A level curve means that net change is close to zero, because additions are roughly equal to removals. It does not mean that births and deaths have stopped. A steep curve means a large increase per unit time. A high but flat curve means a large population with little change.

### Human population increase

Where births exceed deaths and movement of people does not offset the difference, the human population increases.

When limiting factors are weak and the growth rate stays constant, the number added each year gets bigger, because the same percentage is applied to a larger population.

Worked example. A population of 1000 grows by 2% each year with no migration. The first year adds 20 people to give 1020. The next year adds 2% of 1020, which is about 20.4. The additions get larger every year, although the percentage rate is the same.

Human numbers have risen quickly because better medical care, sanitation and food supply have reduced death rates. Birth patterns also change over time. This is a model of how growth works and not a forecast.

### Population size and environmental demand

Better survival can increase the size of a population when other rates do not offset it. A larger population raises the demand for food, water, housing and energy.

The effect on the environment also depends on how much each person consumes, on technology and on management, so the number of people alone does not decide the impact.

More people need more farmland for food, and farming uses land and water. More people produce more domestic waste, which needs collection and treatment. More housing can replace natural habitat, and more water use can lower river flows. Good planning, efficient use of resources and fair access can reduce these pressures.

### Reading population graphs

To read a population graph, check the time scale, the units and whether it shows total size, yearly additions or a percentage rate.

A steeper size curve means a greater increase per unit time. A rising total can occur while the percentage growth rate is falling.

Worked example. A model town has 20 000 people. The growth rate is 5.0% in the first year, 4.0% in the second year and 2.5% in the third year.

Year 1: 5.0% of 20 000 adds 1000 people, giving 21 000.
Year 2: 4.0% of 21 000 adds 840 people, giving 21 840.
Year 3: 2.5% of 21 840 adds 546 people, giving 22 386.

The percentage rate has halved but the population has kept rising, because a smaller percentage of a larger population still adds people. Always check whether a graph shows size, additions or rate before describing the trend.

## 5.4.1 Human influences on ecosystems

### Key points

Food security depends on production, distribution and the ability to buy food. Drought, floods, population growth and poverty can each break one of those links. Monocultures and intensive farming reduce diversity or concentrate waste. Pollution can work by enrichment, by poison, by pathogens or by physical harm. Eutrophication runs from excess nutrients to algal growth, dead material, aerobic decomposition, low oxygen and fish deaths. Pollution is reduced by controlling its source, by recycling, and by matching fertiliser to crop need.

### Monocultures

A large monoculture replaces a more varied habitat with a single crop, reducing the range of food and shelter available to other organisms.

A uniform crop can allow pests and disease to spread quickly, and repeated cultivation of one crop can use up particular soil nutrients and increase the need for fertiliser and pesticides.

A field of one crop is not completely without other species, but it supports far fewer than the natural vegetation it replaced. Fewer plant species mean fewer insects, birds and soil organisms.

Pests spread easily because their food is spread across the whole field with no gaps. Farmers then often use more pesticide, which can harm other organisms. A single crop also takes the same nutrients from the soil each year.

High production can help to meet food needs, so the ecosystem cost depends on how the field is managed. Rotating crops, leaving strips of natural vegetation and protecting the soil can reduce some of the effects.

### Intensive livestock production

Large numbers of livestock kept close together produce concentrated manure and waste, which can pollute water if it is not stored and treated properly.

Growing feed and keeping animals demands land and water, and too many animals on the land can damage vegetation and soil.

Intensive housing and overgrazing are different problems. In a feedlot or a large poultry house, waste is concentrated in one place, and it can run into streams and cause eutrophication. Overgrazing happens on open land when too many animals eat the plant cover, which leaves bare soil open to erosion. In Namibia, where large areas are used as rangeland for cattle, goats and sheep, careful stocking rates help to protect the vegetation.

Manure is a useful fertiliser when it is applied in suitable amounts. It becomes a pollutant when more is released than the soil and crops can use.

### Providing sufficient food

Increasing the food supply can improve nutrition and livelihoods but may need more land, water, energy and money.

Distribution, affordability and waste reduction affect food security as well as total production, and environmental damage can undermine future food supply.

A social effect concerns people's health and access to food. An economic effect concerns income and costs. An environmental effect concerns habitats and resources. These effects are linked: damaged soil lowers future yields, which lowers farmers' income.

When you discuss food supply, explain the link in each case and do not just list three words. Producing more food is one answer to shortage. Reducing food losses in storage and transport, and making existing food accessible, can help without clearing more land.

### Reasons for habitat destruction

Land can be cleared for crops, livestock, housing, roads, mining and other development, removing or fragmenting habitat.

Demand for timber or fuel can also drive clearance. The reason for clearing is different from its ecological consequence.

A road can divide a connected habitat even when most of the land remains, which affects movement and breeding. When you answer a question about causes, name the activity or the demand behind it. When you answer a question about effects, explain what happens to the organisms, soil or water afterwards.

### Effects of deforestation

Removing forest destroys habitat. Without roots to bind the soil and a canopy to break the force of rain, the soil is more easily eroded.

Deforestation also changes how much water the land absorbs and releases, and it reduces carbon storage. The effects on local water flow and climate depend on the site.

Follow the mechanism step by step. Exposed soil is hit directly by raindrops and is washed into streams, where the sediment can harm aquatic life. With less water soaking in, more runs off quickly, which can make flooding worse in some catchments. The carbon cycle effects of deforestation are explained in section 5.2.

### Unequal distribution of food

Food can exist in sufficient total amounts while some groups cannot obtain it, because transport, markets or sharing are unequal.

A shortage in one place can occur without a shortage in the world as a whole.

Availability somewhere is different from access where it is needed. Blocked roads, conflict and exclusion can stop food reaching people even after a good harvest. The right response depends on the barrier: another harvest does not reopen a destroyed road.

Famine is a severe shortage of food that affects a large number of people and leads to starvation and death. Its causes often combine.

### Drought

Drought reduces the water available for crops and livestock and can reduce harvests and animal production.

It can also lower income and raise food prices, which reduces access as well as supply.

Namibia has a dry climate and highly variable rainfall, so drought is a recurring risk for crop and livestock farmers. The effect depends on stored food, irrigation, drought-tolerant crop varieties and support. A rainfall shortage does not always cause famine where food stores and support are available.

Explain the whole chain: water shortage leads to lower production, which leads to less food and less income, which leads to less access to food.

### Flooding

Floods can destroy crops, kill livestock, damage food stores and cut transport, which reduces both food supply and access.

Waterlogging cuts off the oxygen that roots need and can reduce crop growth even after the water has gone.

Floodwater can also contaminate drinking water and spread disease, which adds to the harm from poor nutrition. In northern Namibia, seasonal floods from the Cuvelai system can cover large areas and cut off communities. A small seasonal flood can have a very different effect from a severe one, so timing, size and preparation matter.

Water is not always good for plants. Too much water can be as harmful as too little when roots cannot get oxygen.

### Population increase and food supply

If the population grows faster than the food supply that people can reach, the food available per person falls.

Population growth alone does not guarantee famine, because production, trade, storage and access can also change.

Food per person is the food supply divided by the number of people. An unchanged harvest shared among twice as many people gives half as much each. A harvest that grows as fast as the population keeps the amount per person the same.

Worked example. If food rises by 10% and population rises by 25%, food per person changes by 1.10 ÷ 1.25 = 0.88, which is 12% lower than before.

### Poverty and access to food

Poverty can stop households buying food even when markets have enough in stock.

Poverty also limits access to land, irrigation, storage and farm inputs, which makes households more vulnerable to harvest loss.

Affordability and availability are different problems. Shops with full shelves do not help a household that cannot afford to buy. Rising prices or lost wages can reduce access without changing the size of the harvest.

Long-term responses include secure jobs and income, and support for the poorest households, as well as growing more food.

### Chemical waste

Industrial discharges and farm chemicals can enter rivers, lakes and coastal water through drains and runoff.

Toxic substances can damage organisms and disrupt food webs. The effects depend on the substance, the concentration and the length of exposure.

Not all chemical pollution is nutrient enrichment. A pesticide can poison organisms that were not its target, such as fish and aquatic insects, and a herbicide can kill water plants. Some substances persist for a long time and are carried through food chains.

The cause of damage should be matched to the evidence. If a chemical kills algae immediately and no nutrients were added, direct toxicity is the explanation, not an algal bloom.

### Discarded rubbish

Rubbish can reach water through dumping, drains and wind.

Animals can become tangled in it or swallow it, and piles of waste can damage habitats and make water unusable.

Physical harm and poisoning are different. A loop of fishing line can trap an animal without any chemical being involved. A blocked drain can raise the risk of flooding. In the sea, turtles, seals and seabirds can become entangled in discarded fishing line and nets or swallow plastic pieces.

Preventing rubbish at source, collecting it and recycling it each deal with a different part of the problem.

### Untreated sewage

Untreated sewage puts organic matter, disease-causing microorganisms and nutrients into water.

Microorganisms that break down the organic matter use up dissolved oxygen, and the pathogens are a health risk.

The loss of oxygen starts directly with the breakdown of organic waste. It does not need an algal bloom first. The nutrients in sewage can also cause excess growth of algae and plants.

Water that looks clear can still carry pathogens, so drinking water must be treated and tested.

### Fertilisers as pollutants

Rain and drainage can carry excess nitrate and phosphate from fertiliser from fields into water.

The added nutrients cause rapid growth of algae and plants, which can lead to eutrophication.

Fertiliser helps a crop when the amount and timing suit its needs. Nutrients that the crop does not take up are washed off the field or leached through the soil. Nitrate is very soluble, so it is easily carried into streams and groundwater.

The full sequence of eutrophication is described in the next section. Here the important point is how fertiliser reaches the water.

### Eutrophication

Excess nutrients cause rapid growth of algae and plants. Dense growth shades submerged plants, and when the algae and plants die the dead organic material is broken down by aerobic microorganisms, which use up the oxygen in the water.

The low oxygen concentration kills fish and other sensitive animals. Sewage adds organic matter that also uses oxygen, and pesticides can kill organisms by poisoning, not by enrichment.

Use the sequence to place observations. A lake receives fertiliser runoff in summer. Within two weeks the surface turns green; a month later the dissolved oxygen near the bottom has fallen from 8 to 2 mg per dm³ and dead fish are found. The green surface is the algal bloom, the low oxygen is the result of decomposers respiring on the dead material, and the dead fish are the final effect. The fish did not die of the nutrients themselves: the missing step is the oxygen loss.

Keep the causes separate. Sewage can lower oxygen directly, because its organic matter is broken down by microorganisms, and it adds nutrients too. A pesticide kills by poison, so it does not start the same sequence.

Algae produce oxygen in daylight, but they also respire, and the great quantity of dead material at the end of a bloom is the reason oxygen falls so far.

### Recycling and waste

Recycling recovers useful material from waste, which reduces the demand for new raw materials and cuts the amount of litter and waste that has to be disposed of.

The benefits depend on safe collection, sorting and processing. Hazardous waste needs proper treatment.

Recycling does not remove the need to reduce unnecessary use. A collection point must lead to a real recovery route. Moving waste from a street to an open dump only moves the problem, because wind and rain can carry it back into rivers and drains.

Glass, metal, paper and many plastics can be collected and reprocessed into new products. Recycling saves some of the energy and raw material that would be needed to make the product from scratch. A cleaner environment also lowers the risk of injury and blocked drains.

### Fertiliser overuse

Using more fertiliser than the crop needs leaves soluble nutrients in the soil that are washed out into water, and very high concentrations can harm plants.

Extra fertiliser wastes money and resources and increases pollution without a matching increase in yield.

Match the amount and timing to the soil and crop. Heavy rain soon after fertiliser is spread carries nutrients off the field, especially from bare soil. Extra nitrate does not help if another factor, such as water, is limiting growth.

High concentrations of fertiliser in the soil can also damage plant roots, because water tends to leave the roots when the soil solution is too concentrated. A soil test shows what nutrients are really needed, so the farmer can apply the correct amount.

### Alternatives to industrial fertiliser

Compost and manure return organic material and nutrients to the soil, and rotating crops with legumes can add nitrogen through nitrogen fixation.

Soil testing, accurate application and leaving crop residues in the field can reduce the need for manufactured fertiliser. Organic sources can also pollute water if too much is applied.

A legume crop such as cowpea helps the next crop if its residues stay in the field and decompose, which releases the nitrogen it fixed. If the whole plant is removed, much of the nitrogen leaves with it. In northern Namibia, cowpeas can be grown alongside or in rotation with mahangu, the pearl millet that is the staple grain.

Compost improves soil structure, but it releases its nutrients more slowly than a mineral fertiliser. A natural source is not automatically safe or sufficient. Manure can still run off and cause eutrophication if too much is spread.

### Causes of acid rain

Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, from sources such as burning fossil fuels, react in the air to form acids that fall in rain.

Wind can carry the pollutants far from their source before they fall, so the effects need not be beside the place that released them.

Burning coal and oil that contain sulfur releases sulfur dioxide. High-temperature combustion in engines forms nitrogen oxides from the nitrogen and oxygen of the air. Both gases dissolve in water droplets to form acids.

Acid rain is a different problem from the enhanced greenhouse effect. Both involve burning fuel, but acid rain is caused by sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, and global warming is caused mainly by carbon dioxide and methane. Natural rain is slightly acidic. The pollution problem is the extra acidity.

### Effects of acid rain

Acid rain lowers the pH of lakes and soil, which can harm aquatic organisms and release harmful metal ions from the soil.

It can also damage leaves and trees and attack limestone and some metals in buildings. The effect depends on how well the water or soil can neutralise acid and how much acid falls.

A lake in an area with limestone can neutralise acid better than a lake in an area of hard rock, so the same acid rain can have different effects in different places. A fall in pH means the water is more acidic. Fish eggs and young animals are often the first to be harmed.

When you describe an effect, say exactly what happens, for example that acidified water kills fish or that acid damages tree leaves. Do not just write that acid rain is poisonous.

### Reducing acid rain

Reducing the release of sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides reduces the amount of acid that forms in the air.

Using fuels with less sulfur, treating exhaust gases and using cleaner energy or transport all deal with the source. Treating a lake does not stop the emissions.

Match the action to the gas. Flue-gas treatment can remove sulfur dioxide from the exhaust of a power station or smelter, and catalytic converters in vehicle exhausts reduce nitrogen oxides. Using less fuel, through efficiency or less travel, reduces both gases.

Preventing the emissions is different from repairing damage afterwards. Adding lime to an acidified lake can raise its pH for a time, but the acid will return if the emissions continue.

### Persistent plastics

Plastics that are not biodegradable stay for a very long time on land and in water and cause entanglement, swallowing of plastic and damage to habitats.

When plastic breaks into smaller pieces it has not been broken down by living things. Small particles remain and can be carried widely.

An animal that swallows plastic may have its gut blocked or may feel full and feed less. Net and line fragments can trap limbs. Plastic pieces in the sea can be swallowed by seabirds, turtles and fish.

Prevention works at different stages: using less plastic, reusing it, collecting and recycling it, and stopping litter at source. Burning plastic without proper controls produces harmful gases, so it does not solve the problem.

## 5.4.2 Conservation

### Key points

Conservation maintains biodiversity and the resources that support it. Sustainable development meets present needs while preserving the environment. Match the measure to the threat: habitat loss, killing, pollution or changing conditions. Monitoring must use equal effort. Captive breeding and seed banks keep the potential for recovery but need suitable habitat in the wild. Tourism can pay for protection but can also cause disturbance, so it must be managed.

### What conservation means

Conservation is maintaining the environment and natural resources in a way that maintains biodiversity.

It includes managing the use of resources and protecting ecological processes. It is more than keeping a few attractive animals alive.

Biodiversity includes the number of species and the variation within species, and habitat condition supports their survival. Conservation can involve restoring damaged habitat, monitoring populations and agreeing sustainable use of a resource.

A few animals in an enclosure are not enough to show that a species or an ecosystem is secure. Conservation has to protect the habitat and the population in the wild, as well as the individual animals.

### Conserving fossil fuels

Fossil fuels are non-renewable on a human timescale because they form far more slowly than they are used.

Using them efficiently reduces depletion and the environmental effects of extracting and burning them, and it keeps options open while alternatives are developed.

The stock of fossil fuels is finite even if its exact size is not known. Efficiency means getting the same service from less fuel, for example a car with a more efficient engine. Reducing unnecessary use means needing less of the service in the first place. Both lower the rate of use, but neither makes a fuel renewable.

A new oil field does not change the fact that oil forms over millions of years. When you judge a rate of use, compare it with the time the resource takes to form, and think about the carbon dioxide released when it is burnt.

### Sustainable development

Sustainable development is development that provides for the needs of an increasing human population while preserving the environment.

The aim is to link present wellbeing with keeping the resources and ecological processes that are needed in future.

A project may raise output now but damage the soil or water that people will need later. That would not be sustainable, however much income it brings in the short term. To assess a project, look at the balance of resources, the pollution and who has access, as well as the immediate income.

Preserving the environment does not mean that nobody may use it. Use must be managed so that the supporting ecosystem stays healthy. For example, a forest can supply timber for many years if trees are felled no faster than new ones grow.

### Sustaining forests and fish stocks

Education can improve the way people use resources, quotas can limit how much is taken, and restocking can help depleted populations to recover.

These measures need monitoring, suitable habitat and effective management. Adding young organisms cannot make up for taking far too many.

A quota is a limit on how much may be taken, for example the mass of fish landed in a year. A quota helps only if it is set at a level the stock can sustain, and if it is enforced. Namibia manages its hake fishery in the Benguela ecosystem with annual catch quotas.

Restocking, for example replanting trees or releasing young fish, works only if the species, the habitat and the genetic variety are suitable. Forests need time and protection to regrow. Fish stocks need enough breeding adults and nursery areas such as shallow, sheltered water.

### Treating sewage

Sewage treatment removes large solids and suspended material and then uses controlled biological treatment to reduce organic pollution and oxygen demand.

The treated water is tested and may be disinfected before it is released or reused. Treated effluent is not automatically fit to drink.

The stages in a typical works are as follows. Screens remove large objects such as rags and plastic. Settling tanks let the solid particles sink as sludge. In the aeration tanks, air is bubbled through the liquid so that aerobic bacteria break down the dissolved organic matter. The liquid is then settled again to separate the bacterial sludge, and the cleaned water is tested and released or reused. The sludge is usually treated in separate tanks and may be used as fertiliser if it is safe.

Appearance cannot show whether water is safe. Pathogens, nutrients or dissolved chemicals can remain if the treatment is not complete.

### Climate change

Climate change can move the suitable temperature and rainfall conditions away from the place where a species lives.

If the species cannot move, adapt or reproduce fast enough, its survival and population size fall and its risk of extinction rises.

A species may be unable to move because a barrier or a gap in the habitat blocks the way. Different species respond differently, and some ranges expand while others shrink.

A species is endangered when it is at serious risk of extinction. It is extinct when no living members remain anywhere. The disappearance of a species from one area is not extinction if it survives elsewhere.

### Habitat destruction as a cause of endangerment

Habitat destruction removes food, shelter and breeding sites, or splits up the habitat so that animals cannot move and find mates.

Lower survival and breeding, and isolation, can shrink populations and raise the risk of extinction.

Follow the chain from the habitat lost to the resource or breeding site that has gone, and then to the effect on the population. Losing one patch can make a species disappear locally while other populations survive. Extinction means the loss of the species everywhere.

Conservation has to protect the particular habitat that the species needs. A large area of green cover is not enough if it lacks the feeding area or breeding site the species uses.

### Pollution as a cause of endangerment

Poisons, loss of oxygen or physical waste can reduce the survival and breeding success of organisms.

A species with a small population can become endangered when pollution affects much of its remaining habitat.

Name a mechanism that matches the pollutant. Nutrient enrichment can cause low oxygen, a pesticide can poison organisms that were not its target, and a plastic loop can entangle an animal. A fall in numbers near a polluted site suggests a link, but other causes must be considered before concluding that pollution is responsible.

### Hunting and poaching

Removing animals faster than the population can replace them reduces its size, and killing breeding adults reduces future births.

Illegal killing can threaten populations that are already small and can reduce income from tourism.

Poaching is illegal killing or taking of wildlife. A population can fall even when its habitat is intact, if the number of adults lost is greater than the number of young produced. Slow-breeding animals such as rhinoceros recover slowly because each female produces only a single calf at long intervals.

In north-west Namibia, local people help to monitor and protect the desert-adapted black rhinoceros. Information on exactly where threatened animals live is kept confidential, because it could help poachers.

### Monitoring and protecting species

Regular monitoring estimates population trends and finds the threats, so protection can be targeted and its success checked.

Protection can reduce harmful killing or disturbance, but a rising count is not proof of recovery if the amount of observation has changed.

Use the same places, times and effort in each survey, and record the uncertainty. A species can be hard to find, so failing to see it on one visit does not show that it is extinct.

Worked example. A count rises from 12 to 20 animals, but the number of survey days rises from 2 to 10. The animals seen per survey day fell from 12 ÷ 2 = 6 to 20 ÷ 10 = 2. The extra effort explains the higher count and the real trend may be a decline. Always compare counts for equal effort.

### Protecting habitats

Protecting suitable habitat preserves the food, shelter and breeding conditions that an endangered population needs.

Connected habitat and well-managed land around a reserve help animals to move and reduce isolation. A legal boundary works only if it is enforced.

A fenced reserve that cuts off a seasonal water source may fail even if it is large. The quality and position of the habitat matter as well as its size. Working with local communities and checking the real results helps long-term management.

A reserve does not protect against everything. Disease, climate change and pollution can cross its boundary. Etosha National Park shows how important water is: many animals gather at the waterholes in the dry season, so the water supply is part of the habitat that needs protecting.

### Education and conservation

Education can explain a conservation problem, its causes and what people can do to reduce it.

Changed behaviour can reduce threats, but awareness is not enough if people have no practical alternatives or incentives.

A useful education programme has a clear audience, an action that people can really take and a way to measure whether behaviour or the environment has changed. The number of posters displayed counts activity, not success. Respect local knowledge and people's livelihoods rather than assuming that people harm wildlife only because they lack information. In Namibia, conservancies pair training with real benefits, such as income from wildlife tourism.

### Captive breeding

Captive breeding can increase the number of a threatened species and keep genetic variety through managed choice of parents.

Release can help wild recovery when suitable habitat exists and the threats are controlled, but breeding in captivity alone does not secure a population in the wild.

Breeding programmes need healthy animals, good welfare and care to avoid mating close relatives. Animals also need survival skills before they are let out, and disease risks must be managed. A large captive population can hide low genetic variety.

Releasing animals into a place where the original threat continues, such as poaching or habitat loss, is likely to fail. To judge a programme, check the survival and breeding of released animals, not just how many were released.

### Seed banks

Seed banks conserve seeds of suitable plants under controlled conditions, check their viability from time to time, and keep them for future restoration or breeding.

The collection should represent the genetic variety of the species. Some seeds cannot be kept by standard dry, cold storage and need other methods.

Seeds are collected in a way that does not remove too many from wild populations. Storage slows deterioration, but seeds do not stay viable for ever, so a sample is germinated regularly to test viability and stocks are renewed when needed.

A seed bank keeps the potential for new plants. It does not restore a habitat, so suitable places to grow the plants are still needed.

### Tourism and conservation

Well-managed tourism can bring income and jobs that are linked to conserving wildlife and habitats, and can help to pay for protection and local livelihoods.

Too many visitors, new buildings, waste and disturbance can damage habitats if tourism is poorly managed, so the benefits depend on good planning and a fair sharing of income.

Namibia attracts visitors to its national parks and to the Namib Desert. In its communal conservancies, local people can earn an income from wildlife tourism, which gives them a reason to protect wildlife.

To evaluate tourism, give a benefit and a cost and then make a judgement. The judgement should say when the benefit outweighs the cost, for example when income is used to protect the wildlife and the number of visitors is controlled.

Worked example. A reserve earns N$120 for each visitor, and nesting birds breed successfully only when there are 40 or fewer visitors a day. With 80 visitors a day the income is 80 × 120 = N$9600 a day, but the birds will breed poorly. A cap of 40 earns N$4800 a day and protects the birds. Choosing the higher income would harm the wildlife the visitors come to see.

### Reasons for conservation programmes

Conservation programmes protect biodiversity and the natural processes that people depend on, and they support cultural values and sustainable livelihoods.

A programme needs a defined threat, a realistic aim and monitoring, so that its results can be told apart from the activity involved.

Pollination, water regulation and soil formation are examples of processes that support people and other organisms. A threatened species can also have value that does not depend on immediate profit. Different reasons can apply at once, and a programme should consider who gains and who bears the costs.

"Save nature" is an aim. To justify a programme, explain the mechanism: which pressure is reduced and how survival or habitat condition then improves.

### Investigating poaching and tourism

An ethical investigation can compare authorised records of poaching with measures of tourism, such as visits, bookings or income, over matched periods and at comparable sites.

Other causes of change in tourism, and differences in how the records were made, must be considered. An association does not by itself prove that poaching caused the change.

Do not interfere with poaching, reveal where animals live or ask people about illegal activity. Use public or authorised records without names or locations, and note any missing values. Define the area and time units clearly and compare similar seasons, because tourism changes with the time of year. Road closures, economic changes and new accommodation can also change visitor numbers.

A clear table, repeated over several periods or sites, makes the pattern more reliable. A small observational study cannot rule out every other cause, so the conclusion should say what the data suggest, not that poaching has been proved to cause the change.
