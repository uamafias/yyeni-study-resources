## 3.1 Magnification and the light microscope

### Key points

Magnification is a ratio: image size divided by actual size, in the same unit. Actual size is a length with a unit, not a value written with ×. Total magnification is eyepiece times objective. Locate a microscope specimen at low power, centre it, and only then increase the power.

### Magnification and specimen size

Magnification is image size divided by actual size with both lengths expressed in the same unit; it is a ratio, not a length.

Actual specimen size is image size divided by magnification. Convert millimetres to micrometres by multiplying by 1000 before or after dividing, so that the answer is in the unit the question asks for.

For a 16 mm image at ×400, actual size is 16 ÷ 400 = 0.04 mm, which is 40 μm. The answer 40 μm states a size, whereas ×400 states how many times larger the image is. To find magnification instead, divide image size by actual size: 45 mm ÷ 0.15 mm = ×300.

Measure a drawing between the stated end points with a ruler, in millimetres. A scale bar lets you work proportionally: a feature twice as long as a 25 μm bar is 50 μm long. If a picture has been enlarged or reduced, use its scale bar, since the stated magnification no longer applies.

### Using a light microscope

Begin with the low-power objective, secure the slide on the stage and adjust the illumination before bringing the specimen into focus. Centre the region of interest before changing to higher power.

Use fine focus for careful adjustment at high power, taking care that the objective does not touch the slide. Carry the microscope with both hands and follow the operating instructions.

The usual sequence is as follows. Turn the nosepiece to the lowest-power objective. Clip the slide flat on the stage. Look from the side while the coarse focus brings the objective close to the slide, then look through the eyepiece and focus by moving the objective away from the slide, so that the glass is not pushed into it. Adjust the lamp and the diaphragm until the field is evenly lit. Move the slide until the part you want is in the centre, then turn to the next objective and sharpen the image with fine focus only.

Low power gives a wider field, which makes a small specimen easier to find. Higher power narrows the field and lets in less light, so recentre and readjust the light after each change. Total magnification is the eyepiece magnification multiplied by the objective magnification: a ×10 eyepiece with a ×40 objective gives ×400.

## 3.2 Cell structures and levels of organisation

### Key points

| Structure | Role/position |
|---|---|
| Wall | outside the plant membrane; support |
| Membrane | boundary controlling exchange |
| Cytoplasm | many reactions |
| Nucleus | genetic information |
| Chloroplast | photosynthesis |
| Vacuole | cell sap |
| Ribosome | protein synthesis |
| Rough ER/vesicle | protein handling/transport |
| Mitochondrion | aerobic respiration |

A palisade cell and liver cell share membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus; the liver cell has no cellulose wall or chloroplasts. Draw what you can see, with clear lines and labels. Cells form tissues, tissues form organs and organs form organ systems.

### Palisade and liver cells

A palisade cell has a cell wall outside its cell membrane, cytoplasm containing a nucleus and chloroplasts, and a large cell-sap vacuole. A liver cell has a cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus but no cellulose wall or chloroplasts.

In a palisade cell the large vacuole pushes much of the cytoplasm towards the edge. A thin membrane can be difficult to distinguish from the adjoining wall in an ordinary light-microscope view.

Locate structures by their position in the whole cell. The wall forms an outer supporting boundary, and the membrane just inside it controls what enters and leaves. Cytoplasm is where many cell reactions occur. The nucleus contains the genetic information and controls cell activities. Chloroplasts are the sites of photosynthesis. The large vacuole contains cell sap, a solution of sugars and salts, so it is not an empty space.

### Comparing plant and animal cells

Both palisade and liver cells have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus. The palisade cell additionally has a cellulose wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole.

A cell is identified from a combination of structures that can be seen, not from a fixed rectangular or rounded outline, because real shapes and views vary.

Compare like with like: a cell wall is present or absent, and chloroplasts are present or absent. A root cell is a plant cell although it has no chloroplasts, because its cells are underground and do not photosynthesise. Absence of green bodies alone therefore does not make a cell an animal cell, whereas a cellulose wall is strong evidence for a plant cell.

A palisade cell is specialised for absorbing light, so it has many chloroplasts, whereas a liver cell has no need for them.

### Temporary plant-cell slides

A thin temporary mount lets light pass through the specimen and reduces overlap of cells. Place a small thin sample in a drop of liquid on the slide and lower a coverslip carefully to reduce air bubbles.

Observe at low power first, then draw the visible cells at a useful size with clear outlines and labels. Draw only structures that can be seen in the field of view.

A thin strip of onion epidermis is a good specimen. Peel a piece from the inner surface of a scale leaf with forceps, lay it flat in a drop of water on a slide, and lower a coverslip from one side with a mounting needle so that air is pushed out. A drop of iodine solution added at one edge and drawn under with filter paper stains the nuclei and makes them easier to see. Onion bulb epidermis has no chloroplasts, whereas a thin piece of a green leaf may show them.

For the drawing, use a sharp pencil and draw single, unbroken lines with no shading. Make the drawing large, show the cells in their true proportions, and label with ruled lines that touch the structure named and do not cross each other. Add a title and the magnification.

### Animal tissue observations

Animal cells in a prepared slide have membranes rather than cellulose walls; the visible shape and arrangement depend on the tissue and the plane of section.

A tissue drawing records an observed group of cells and their arrangement, not a collection of identical idealised animal-cell symbols.

A cheek-cell preparation shows separate, flattened cells, each with a nucleus that a stain makes visible. Epithelium shows cells packed together as a covering layer. On a slide of mammalian trachea, the lining epithelium has column-shaped cells with cilia on the free surface, with scattered goblet cells that secrete mucus; the lining is developed further in 3.9. Muscle tissue shows long cells arranged in parallel. In a tissue drawing, keep the cells in their true arrangement, because this shows what the tissue is.

An ordinary light microscope cannot resolve fine organelles such as ribosomes, so these are not labelled on a drawing of a slide.

### Rough endoplasmic reticulum

Rough endoplasmic reticulum is a membrane system with ribosomes attached to its surface.

It helps process and transport proteins made on attached ribosomes, including proteins that may be secreted.

The ribosomes make the protein, and the membrane system around them carries and processes it; “rough” describes the attached ribosomes, not a damaged surface.

### Ribosomes

Ribosomes are sites of protein synthesis.

They join amino acids in the sequence needed for a particular protein and can be free in cytoplasm or attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum.

A ribosome is a tiny structure, not a membrane system, although some ribosomes sit on one. Ribosomes make proteins, including the enzymes that catalyse reactions in the cell. The way amino acids join to build protein is taught in 3.4.

### Vesicles

Vesicles are small membrane-bound compartments that carry substances within cells.

They can transport material between cell compartments or towards the surface for release.

A vesicle that carries a protein from the endoplasmic reticulum to the cell surface fuses with the cell membrane and releases its contents outside the cell.

### Mitochondria

Mitochondria are sites of aerobic respiration and help release energy for cell activities.

Cells with high energy demands commonly contain many mitochondria.

Respiration transfers energy that was stored in food; it does not create energy from nothing. Plant cells as well as animal cells have mitochondria, because plants also respire. Muscle cells have many mitochondria because they use a lot of energy. Respiration is developed in 3.10.

### Mitochondria and energy demand

A cell needs energy for work such as contraction, synthesis and active transport. A high sustained demand is commonly associated with a large number of mitochondria.

Relate the structure to the particular work described: the more work a cell does that needs energy from respiration, the more mitochondria it is likely to have.

Two cells of the same size may differ in how much work they do. If one moves far more material against a concentration gradient, it needs a higher rate of energy release, so it is likely to contain more mitochondria. Cell size alone is not the reason. For an unfamiliar cell, look for what it does, such as contracting or active transport, and use that to predict its mitochondria. Active transport is explained in 3.3.3.

### Levels of organisation

A cell is a basic living unit; a tissue is a group of cells working together, and an organ contains different tissues performing a coordinated function.

Organs cooperate in an organ system, and the systems together form the organism. Identify the level from the structures present rather than from the apparent size of a drawing.

A muscle cell is a cell, muscle tissue is a tissue, the heart is an organ containing muscle, blood vessels and other tissues, and the circulatory system is an organ system. A leaf is an organ because it contains several tissues, such as palisade tissue, xylem and phloem, so it is not a single tissue even though it looks like a flat sheet.

Size on the page can mislead, because a magnified drawing of one cell may be larger than a small drawing of a whole organ. Decide the level by asking what the drawing shows: one cell, a group of similar cells, or several tissues working together.

### Where this connects

Protein synthesis is developed in 3.4 and enzymes in 3.5; membrane transport mechanisms are in 3.3.1 to 3.3.3. Levels of organisation lead into the tissue and organ accounts in later units.

## 3.3.1 Diffusion

### Key points

Diffusion is net movement down a concentration gradient from random particle motion, and it needs no energy from respiration. A larger area, a steeper gradient, higher temperature and shorter distance each make exchange faster when the other conditions are the same. Random motion continues at equilibrium. Investigations need a fixed endpoint and controlled variables.

### What diffusion is

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, using their kinetic energy.

Particles move randomly in both directions, but initially more leave the concentrated region, producing net movement down the gradient. At equilibrium random movement continues without a net concentration change.

A concentration gradient is a difference in concentration over a distance. Diffusion does not need a membrane: particles spread through a liquid or a gas, and if a membrane is permeable to them they can also cross it. “Net” describes the overall outcome, since individual particles move in all directions but more move down the gradient than up it.

A drop of ink in still water shows this. At first the ink is concentrated at one point. Its particles spread out until the colour is the same throughout. After that the particles still move, but there is no further change in colour. Diffusion is different from active transport, which can move particles against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration. Osmosis is the water-specific case through a partially permeable membrane, taught in 3.3.2.

### The energy source for diffusion

Diffusing molecules and ions already possess kinetic energy and move randomly. Their random movement supplies the energy involved in diffusion.

Diffusion does not require an input of energy from cellular respiration to drive particles down their concentration gradient.

A living cell does not spend respiratory energy to make each oxygen molecule diffuse in. The same spreading happens outside living things, for example when a scent spreads across a room or a dye spreads in water. The energy comes from the kinetic energy that the particles already have.

This is the main difference from active transport. In active transport the cell supplies energy from respiration to move particles against a gradient. In diffusion the particles move down a gradient using their own kinetic energy, and the cell does not need to supply energy for that movement.

### Surface area

Increasing the available surface area increases the total rate of diffusion when the other relevant conditions are the same.

More surface gives particles more routes across the boundary at the same time.

Compare equal volumes with different exposed areas while keeping concentration difference, temperature and diffusion distance the same. A folded or finely divided surface gives a larger area without a larger overall organ. This is why exchange surfaces such as the lining of the small intestine are folded. A larger area increases the total number of particles crossing each second, but it does not change how fast any single particle moves.

### Temperature

Raising temperature increases particle kinetic energy, so diffusion tends to occur faster when other relevant conditions remain the same.

The effect comes from faster particle movement, not from energy supplied by respiration.

A warmer dye experiment must keep the same amount of dye, the same volume of water and no stirring if temperature is the only thing that changes. Stirring or convection currents also spread material, so they must be excluded before a result is credited to temperature. Temperature has additional effects on enzyme reactions, explained in 3.5.

### Concentration gradient

A steeper concentration gradient gives a greater net diffusion rate when surface area, distance and temperature remain the same.

Removal on one side or a continued supply on the other keeps a concentration difference from disappearing.

The difference between the two sides matters, not the concentration on either side alone. A high concentration on both sides is not a steep gradient. If both sides reach the same concentration, random motion continues but net diffusion falls to zero. In a body, blood flow carries oxygen away from a gas exchange surface and brings carbon dioxide to it, which keeps the gradients steep. Oxygen and carbon dioxide can have opposite gradients at the same surface.

### Diffusion distance

A shorter diffusion path permits more rapid exchange under otherwise comparable conditions.

Thin exchange barriers reduce the distance between a source and a sink, while thick layers slow exchange.

Distance is not the same as area: a broad but thick barrier and a narrow but thin barrier change different factors. In a fair comparison keep the permeability and the concentration gradient the same. Short paths in cells and tissues are useful where fast exchange is needed. Alveoli have walls only one cell thick, and cells are never far from a capillary, which keeps the diffusion path short for oxygen.

### Why gases and solutes diffuse

Diffusion permits gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide and suitable dissolved substances to move down their own gradients between cells and their surroundings.

A gas or solute moves into a cell when its external concentration is higher than its internal concentration and the boundary permits passage; a reversed gradient gives a reversed net direction.

Oxygen can enter a respiring cell because the cell uses oxygen, which keeps the concentration inside lower than outside. Carbon dioxide made by respiration builds up inside, so it leaves down a gradient in the opposite direction. Blood flow, ventilation and short diffusion paths help keep these differences in place, but the movement itself is still random particle motion with a net outcome.

Not every substance crosses every membrane. A membrane is permeable to some substances and not others, and the gradient only drives movement of a substance the membrane lets through. For example, small gas molecules cross easily, whereas large molecules such as starch do not.

### Investigating diffusion along a tube

A teacher-supervised ammonia demonstration can use moist red litmus pieces at measured distances along a tube; a colour change to blue shows that ammonia has reached them.

Start timing at a defined release, record the time to the same colour endpoint at each distance and keep the tube, temperature and ammonia source the same. Ammonia needs teacher-controlled safe handling and ventilation.

Place equally moist pieces of red litmus paper at 5 cm intervals inside a glass tube. The teacher places a small cotton wool pad with ammonia solution at one end and starts the clock. Record the time at which each piece first turns blue. The nearer pieces change first and the farther pieces later, which shows that the ammonia spreads out from the source.

The independent variable is the distance from the source, and the dependent variable is the time to turn blue. Keep the same type of litmus paper, the same wetness, the same tube and a constant room temperature. Repeat the demonstration and calculate a mean time at each distance. The colour change shows that ammonia has arrived, but it does not show the speed of individual particles. Random motion and collisions with air particles make the spread much slower than the speed of the particles themselves.

### Testing one diffusion factor

An investigation of a diffusion factor changes that factor over a suitable range and measures a comparable diffusion endpoint while keeping the other factors the same.

A model such as the spread of a coloured solute in water can compare temperature, concentration difference or path length if mixing and endpoint definitions are controlled.

Choose a measurable endpoint before collecting data, for example the time for a fixed observation point to reach a reference colour. A shorter time to the same endpoint shows a faster relative rate. Use identical containers and volumes. For temperature, hold the water in baths at 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C, add one crystal of the same dye at the same place without stirring, and time how long the colour takes to reach a mark 3 cm away. Repeat at each temperature and calculate the mean time.

A table of temperature against mean time, and a graph with temperature on the horizontal axis, shows the trend. For concentration difference or path length, change only that factor and keep temperature and the container the same. Explain in the plan how the chosen factor is isolated, so that a change in the endpoint can be credited to it.

### Where this connects

Osmosis in 3.3.2 applies diffusion to water through a partially permeable membrane. Active transport in 3.3.3 moves particles against a gradient using respiratory energy. Gas exchange (3.9) uses the same diffusion principles.

## 3.3.2 Osmosis

### Key points

| Learn | |
|---|---|
| Osmosis | net movement of water from a dilute to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane |
| Plant cell, more dilute outside | water in, turgid; the wall stops it bursting |
| Plant cell, more concentrated outside | water out, flaccid, then plasmolysed (membrane pulls away from the wall) |
| Animal cell | no wall: swells and can burst, or shrinks and shrivels |
| Words | turgid, flaccid, plasmolysed describe cells; hypotonic, isotonic, hypertonic describe solutions |
| Roots | water enters root hairs by osmosis and moves down a water potential gradient to the xylem |

### Osmosis and water potential

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a dilute solution to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane. Net means overall: water molecules cross the membrane in both directions, but more cross from the dilute side, so water builds up on the concentrated side. The membrane lets water molecules through but holds back larger solute molecules such as sucrose. Biologists describe the same idea with water potential: water moves by osmosis from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential. Water potential depends mainly on how dilute a solution is, and also on pressure. At the same pressure, a dilute solution has a higher water potential than a concentrated one.

### Plant cells in a more dilute solution

In a plant cell the cell membrane is the partially permeable membrane and the cell sap in the vacuole is the solution inside. Put the cell into a hypotonic solution, one more dilute than its cell sap, and water enters by osmosis. The vacuole swells and pushes the cytoplasm against the cell wall. This outward push is turgor pressure, and the wall pushes back. The cell becomes turgid but does not burst, because the wall is strong. In an isotonic solution, with the same concentration as the cell sap, there is no net movement of water and the cell stays the same size.

### Plant cells in a more concentrated solution

Put the same cell into a hypertonic solution, one more concentrated than its cell sap, and water leaves by osmosis. The vacuole shrinks and the cell becomes flaccid, or soft. If water keeps leaving, the cell membrane, with the cytoplasm inside it, pulls away from the cell wall. The cell is then plasmolysed. The space between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the wall lets it through.

### Animal cells have no wall

An animal cell, such as a red blood cell, also gains or loses water by osmosis, but it has no cell wall. In a solution more dilute than its cytoplasm it takes in water and swells, and with nothing to resist the swelling it can burst. In a solution more concentrated than its cytoplasm it loses water and shrinks, becoming shrivelled. In an isotonic solution it keeps its shape. This is why fluids given to patients through a drip have the same concentration as blood plasma.

### Cell words and solution words

Two sets of words are easy to mix up. Turgid, flaccid and plasmolysed describe the cell. Hypertonic, isotonic and hypotonic describe the solution, compared with the cell contents. In an answer about osmosis, use a solution word for the solution and a cell word for the cell.

### How roots take in water

Soil water is usually more dilute than the cell sap of a root hair cell, so it usually has a higher water potential, and water enters the root hair by osmosis. From there water moves across the root towards the xylem, down a water potential gradient. Some of it travels through the cells and some along the cell walls between them. The xylem then carries it up the plant. The gradient can be reversed. If the soil water becomes more concentrated than the root cell sap, its water potential is lower, and water leaves the roots by osmosis: the plant can wilt even though the soil is wet. Adding too much fertiliser is one way this can happen.

### How turgor pressure supports a plant

A young stem of pumpkin or a leaf of spinach has no wood to hold it up. It stays firm because its cells are turgid: water has entered by osmosis, each vacuole has swollen, and the turgor pressure of each cell pushes its contents against its wall. Turgid cells press against one another like tightly packed balloons, and the whole stem or leaf becomes firm. When the plant loses more water than it takes in, the cells become flaccid and the soft parts droop.

### Seeing plasmolysis under the microscope

Peel a thin strip of epidermis from the inside of an onion scale leaf. Mount one piece in a drop of water and another in a drop of concentrated salt solution, add coverslips and leave both for about ten minutes. Under the microscope the cells in water are turgid, with the cytoplasm pressed against the walls. The cells in salt solution are plasmolysed: the cell membrane and cytoplasm have pulled away from the walls. Red onion makes this easier to see, because the coloured cell sap shows the shrinking vacuole. To turn this into an investigation, mount strips in a range of salt concentrations, wait the same time for each, and count the percentage of cells plasmolysed in a field of view.

### Investigating osmosis with potato cylinders

Plan it first. The independent variable is the concentration of the solution; the dependent variable is the percentage change in mass; keep the size of the cylinders, the time, the temperature and the potato the same, and repeat each concentration. Then cut cylinders of potato of the same length and diameter with a cork borer. Blot each one dry and weigh it. Put one cylinder into each of a range of sucrose solutions, for example from 0.0 to 0.8 mol/dm³, for the same time and at the same temperature. Then remove each cylinder, blot it in the same way and weigh it again. A gain in mass means water entered the cells; a loss means water left.

### Worked example: percentage change in mass

A potato cylinder weighs 2.00 g at the start and 2.16 g after 30 minutes in 0.1 mol/dm³ sucrose. The change is 2.16 - 2.00 = 0.16 g. As a percentage of the starting mass, 0.16 ÷ 2.00 = 0.08, and 0.08 × 100 = 8, so the cylinder gained 8%. Percentages let you compare cylinders that started at different masses. If a second cylinder lost mass in 0.3 mol/dm³, the concentration of the cell contents lies between 0.1 and 0.3 mol/dm³: the solution with no change in mass would match it.

### A model cell made of Visking tubing

Visking tubing is a partially permeable membrane. Tie one end, fill the tube with sucrose solution, tie the other end and weigh it. Leave it in a beaker of water. Water enters by osmosis, so the bag swells, becomes firm and gains mass, much as a plant cell becomes turgid. A bag of water placed in sucrose solution does the opposite.

### Making the results trustworthy

Solution left on the surface of a piece of tissue adds mass that the cells did not take up, so blot every piece in the same way before each weighing. One piece can behave unusually, so repeat each concentration and work out the mean. Water moves slowly, so give the tissue enough time in each solution, and keep the time the same for every piece. If an expected change is not seen, think about what else could explain it: too little time, or a solution that is not concentrated enough.

### Where this connects

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion (3.3.1), for water through a partially permeable membrane. Active transport (3.3.3) moves particles the other way, against a gradient. Water uptake by roots continues in Transport in plants (3.7), which explains transpiration and wilting. The skills the investigations use, naming the independent, dependent and controlled variables, setting out a results table and working out a percentage change, are taught in Scientific processes (1.1 and 1.2).

## 3.3.3 Active transport

### Key points

| Learn | |
|---|---|
| Active transport | particles move against a concentration gradient, using energy from respiration, through carrier proteins |
| Where it matters | ions into root hairs; glucose into villus cells; glucose reabsorbed in kidney tubules |
| What stops it | anything that stops respiration, such as no oxygen in waterlogged soil |
| Diffusion and osmosis | down a gradient, no energy from respiration |

### What active transport is

Active transport is the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration using energy from respiration.

### How carrier proteins move particles

Active transport is done by carrier proteins in the cell membrane. A particle binds to a carrier protein that fits it, on one side of the membrane. The carrier changes shape and moves the particle across, releasing it on the other side. The energy for this comes from respiration: some carriers use it directly, others use it indirectly, but either way active transport stops when respiration stops. Cells that do a lot of active transport contain many mitochondria, which release this energy.

### Root hair cells

Mineral ions such as nitrate are often more concentrated inside root hair cells than in the soil water around them. Diffusion would carry them out, not in, so root hair cells take them in against the concentration gradient by active transport. This needs energy from respiration, so roots need oxygen. In waterlogged soil, which holds little oxygen, the roots take up fewer ions, and crops can show signs of mineral deficiency.

### The villi and the kidney tubules

Epithelial cells of the villi in the small intestine take in glucose by active transport. Glucose can then be absorbed even when there is less of it in the gut than in the cells, so little is left to be lost. Cells lining the kidney tubules reabsorb glucose in the same way, returning it to the blood so that it is not lost in the urine.

### Three ways across a membrane

The table sets the three processes side by side.

| | Diffusion | Osmosis | Active transport |
|---|---|---|---|
| What moves | particles of a gas or solute | water molecules | particles such as ions or glucose |
| Direction | down a concentration gradient | from a dilute to a more concentrated solution | against a concentration gradient |
| Energy from respiration | not needed | not needed | needed |
| Proteins in the membrane | none needed for simple diffusion | none needed | carrier proteins needed |

A quick test: ask which way the particles go compared with their gradient. Down the gradient, the cell spends no energy from respiration. Against the gradient, it must.

### Where this connects

A concentration gradient, and diffusion down one, are explained in Diffusion (3.3.1). Mitochondria and their job are in Cell structure (3.2). Root hairs return in Transport in plants (3.7), the villi in Absorption (3.6.2.4), and the kidney in Excretion in humans (3.11).

### Not every movement needs energy

Only active transport needs energy from respiration, because it moves particles against a concentration gradient. Diffusion and osmosis move particles down a gradient, from where there are more to where there are fewer, and need no energy from respiration.

## 3.4 Biological molecules and food tests

### Key points

| Larger substance | Components/example role |
|---|---|
| Starch/glycogen | simple sugar units; storage |
| Protein | amino acids; structure and specialised functions |
| Fat/oil | fatty acids and glycerol; storage/insulation |
| DNA | paired strands in a double helix; A–T and C–G |

Tests: Benedict’s plus safe heating for reducing sugar; iodine for starch; Biuret for protein; ethanol emulsion for lipid; DCPIP decolourisation for vitamin C. Test separate portions and use equal amounts when comparing.

### Building large molecules: starch and glycogen

Starch and glycogen are large storage carbohydrates assembled from many simple sugar units.

Joining many sugar units produces a molecule suited to storage, and breakdown can later release smaller sugars.

Plants store carbohydrate as starch, while animals, including humans, store it as glycogen in the liver and muscles. Both are long chains of glucose units. A large insoluble molecule is a compact store, because it does not dissolve and affect the water balance of the cell the way a high concentration of small sugar molecules would.

Synthesis joins small units into a larger molecule, and digestion separates them again. This pattern, small units joined into large molecules, is shared by carbohydrates, proteins and fats, and the next three sections give the units for each. The same names appear in the food tests later in this unit.

### Proteins from amino acids

Proteins are built from amino acids linked in chains.

The amino-acid sequence affects how the chain folds and therefore its resulting shape and function.

Twenty different amino acids occur in proteins. Joining them in different orders gives different proteins, in the way that twenty letters can make very many different words. Glycerol and fatty acids are the building blocks of fats, not proteins. A protein also contains nitrogen, which is why plants need nitrate and why animals must eat protein.

The chain folds into a three-dimensional shape that depends on its sequence. That shape is what lets an enzyme fit its substrate, or an antibody fit the surface of a pathogen.

### Building a fat or oil molecule

Fats and oils are made by joining fatty acids to glycerol.

Their component building blocks differ from the simple sugars of storage carbohydrates and the amino acids of proteins.

A fat or oil molecule has one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acids. Fats are solid at room temperature and oils are liquid, but both are lipids with the same building blocks. They are a concentrated energy store: gram for gram, a lipid releases more energy in respiration than a carbohydrate does. Fats and oils do not dissolve in water, which is why a different test and different digestion steps are needed.

### Roles of carbohydrates

Carbohydrates provide respiratory substrates and storage material, and cellulose provides structural support in plant cell walls.

Glucose can be used in respiration, while starch or glycogen can be stored and later broken down.

Cellulose is a carbohydrate with a structural role in plants, whereas animals cannot use it as a respiratory substrate. Starch and glycogen store chemical energy; they are not energy themselves, and the energy is released when the stored carbohydrate is broken down to glucose and used in respiration.

### Roles of fats and oils

Fats and oils provide concentrated energy stores and can provide insulation or physical protection.

Lipids are also part of cell membranes, and a fat layer around some organs protects them.

A layer of fat under the skin reduces heat loss, which matters for animals in cold surroundings. Fat around some organs, such as the kidneys, protects them from knocks. A fat store can be used in respiration when other supplies run low, and it holds more energy per gram than carbohydrate. Fats and oils are also needed to make cell membranes, so a diet with no fat would still not be healthy.

### Roles of proteins

Proteins supply structural material for growth and repair and perform specialised functions such as enzymes, antibodies and some transport roles.

The shape of a particular protein enables its function; proteins are not simply a single generic building material.

Enzymes speed up reactions and antibodies help defend against pathogens. Haemoglobin is a protein that carries oxygen. Muscle and skin contain structural proteins. A protein can also be used in respiration if there is too little carbohydrate and fat, but its main roles are to build and run cells.

### Water as a solvent

Water dissolves many substances, providing a medium for reactions and movement of dissolved materials.

Dissolved digestion products, wastes and transported substances can be carried in aqueous fluids to their destinations.

Reactions in cells take place in solution, because the substances must be dissolved to meet. Blood plasma carries dissolved glucose, amino acids, ions and urea. Urine is a solution that carries dissolved waste out of the body. Water does not dissolve every biological material: fats do not dissolve in water, so they need different handling in digestion. A solvent is the liquid that does the dissolving, and the solute is the substance dissolved in it.

### Sequence, folding and protein shape

Different amino-acid sequences can produce different folding interactions and therefore different protein shapes.

A changed shape can alter a protein’s function, for example whether an enzyme’s active site accommodates a substrate.

The sequence is the order of the amino acids, not just how many there are. Two chains of the same length but a different order need not fold in the same way. A single change in the sequence can alter the shape enough to stop a protein working, although a change in some regions has little effect, so the consequence depends on where the change is.

Sequence gives folding, folding gives shape, and shape gives function. Enzyme specificity is developed in 3.5.

### DNA structure

DNA consists of two strands coiled together as a double helix. Each strand contains bases, and the complementary pairings are A with T and C with G.

The base order on one strand determines the matching order on the other through those pairings; the two strands are complementary, not identical.

For the sequence A C T G, the matching strand is T G A C, because A pairs with T and C pairs with G. In a double helix the pairs of bases link the two strands like the rungs of a twisted ladder. The bases are known by their letters, A, T, C and G.

A worked example: a strand reads T T A G C. The matching strand reads A A T C G. If a strand has 30% A, then the matching strand has 30% T, so the two strands together have equal amounts of A and T and equal amounts of C and G. DNA stores the instructions for making proteins in the order of its bases; inherited information is developed in 4.4.1.

### Reducing sugars

Benedict’s solution tests for reducing sugars when the mixture is heated safely in a hot-water bath.

A positive test forms a coloured precipitate; the blue reagent becomes green, yellow, orange or brick-red depending on the amount of sugar.

Add equal volumes of food solution and Benedict’s solution, heat in a water bath at about 80 °C for a few minutes, and record the colour. Blue stays blue when no reducing sugar is present. Green means a little, and orange or brick-red means a lot. Compare with a known positive and a known negative sample if they are available. Sucrose does not give a positive result until it has been broken down, so the test is for reducing sugars, not for every sugar.

### Starch test

Iodine solution is used to test for starch.

A blue-black colour is a positive starch response, compared with the starting brown or orange iodine colour.

Add a few drops of iodine solution to the food sample. Starch turns the iodine from brown to blue-black, and a sample with no starch stays brown. Write down the colour seen, not just “positive”. Iodine detects starch and does not detect sugars, so a sample can give a positive Benedict’s test and a negative iodine test.

### Protein test

The Biuret test is used to detect proteins.

A positive result is purple or lilac, compared with the blue appearance of a negative test.

Add sodium hydroxide solution to the sample, then a few drops of dilute copper sulfate solution, and shake gently. The mixture stays blue if there is no protein and turns purple or lilac if protein is present. Egg white and milk give a positive result. The solutions are corrosive, so wear eye protection. A food can give positive results in several tests at once, for example milk contains protein, fat and a sugar, so each test is done on a separate portion.

### Lipid emulsion test

In the ethanol test, lipid is extracted into ethanol and the extract is added to water.

A cloudy white emulsion indicates lipid; ethanol is flammable and must be kept away from flames.

Shake a crushed sample with ethanol so that any fat or oil dissolves in it. Pour the clear liquid into a tube of water. If lipid is present, it forms tiny droplets that make the water cloudy and white, which is an emulsion. A sample with no lipid leaves the water clear. Use a separate clean sample for each test, and keep ethanol away from flames, because it is flammable.

### Vitamin C test

Vitamin C reduces blue DCPIP, causing it to lose its blue colour under the test conditions.

When comparing samples, use the same DCPIP volume and concentration and a consistent colour endpoint.

Place 1 cm³ of blue DCPIP solution in a tube and add the test juice a drop at a time, shaking after each drop, until the blue colour disappears. The volume of juice needed is the result. A juice that decolourises DCPIP after only 5 drops has more vitamin C per drop than a juice that needs 20 drops. To make the comparison fair, use the same volume and concentration of DCPIP and the same drop size for every juice. Other reducing substances can also decolourise DCPIP, so the result gives a comparison and not an exact amount of vitamin C.

### Food distribution within a seed or fruit

To compare where food substances are stored in a seed or fruit, test separate, labelled regions using the same extraction and test methods for each.

Keep sample mass, extraction volume and test conditions the same; a stronger appearance is not a fair comparison if one portion was more concentrated by preparation.

Label each region before processing it, such as the seed coat and the storage tissue of a seed, or the skin and the inner tissue of a fruit. Prepare separate extracts or portions for the starch, reducing sugar, protein and lipid tests, so that one reagent does not affect another. Use a known positive and a known negative sample as controls, and repeat each observation. A negative result may mean there is little of the substance, or that the extraction was poor, which is why the known positive sample matters.

Present the results in a table with the regions in the rows and the four substances in the columns, marking each result as positive or negative or recording the colour. Compare regions only when equal masses of tissue have been tested in equal volumes of solvent.

### Where this connects

Enzymes in 3.5 are proteins whose sequence and shape support specific action. Nutrition in 3.6.2 uses food tests and molecular breakdown; inherited information is developed in 4.4.1.

## 3.5 Catalysts and enzymes

### Key points

An enzyme is a protein catalyst. A suitable substrate binds at the active site, products form and leave, and the enzyme is unchanged. Temperature and pH affect activity: above the optimum an enzyme is denatured, and its active site changes shape, whereas cooling only slows the reaction. Use matched concentrations, conditions, endpoints and repeats in enzyme investigations.

### Catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction.

A catalyst does not supply any of the reactants, and it is still present, unchanged, when the reaction is over.

With a catalyst the same amount of product forms in less time, and because the catalyst is not used up, a small amount can act again and again.

A familiar example is hydrogen peroxide, which breaks down slowly into water and oxygen on its own, but much faster when manganese(IV) oxide or the enzyme catalase is added. Afterwards the catalyst is still present. Living things use biological catalysts, called enzymes, because the reactions of life would otherwise be too slow at body temperature.

### Enzymes

Enzymes are proteins that function as biological catalysts.

They speed particular biological reactions, and their protein shape helps determine which substrates can interact with them.

An enzyme is a molecule, not a living organism. It can be denatured but it is not “killed”. Its effect depends on factors such as temperature and pH, and different enzymes have different suitable conditions, for example pepsin works best in the acid conditions of the stomach and amylase in the mouth works best near neutral pH.

Enzymes are involved in synthesis and breakdown reactions throughout organisms. Digestive enzymes such as amylase break large molecules into small ones, whereas other enzymes in cells join small molecules together. Because every enzyme is a protein, its shape depends on its amino-acid sequence (3.4).

### Active sites and reaction products

A substrate interacts with an enzyme’s active site to form an enzyme–substrate complex, after which products are released and the enzyme can act again.

The active site’s shape and properties allow suitable substrates to bind; a substrate that cannot interact correctly is not acted on in the same way.

In a breakdown reaction, one substrate is split into smaller products, as when amylase breaks starch into maltose. In a synthesis reaction, smaller substrates are joined into a larger product. Always separate the substrate, which is present before the reaction, from the products, which are present afterwards.

The sequence is: the substrate fits the active site, an enzyme–substrate complex forms, the reaction takes place, and the products leave the active site. The enzyme is unchanged and can bind another substrate molecule. Only a substrate with the right shape fits the active site of a particular enzyme, which explains specificity. Changing the shape of the protein changes the shape of the active site, which affects how well the substrate fits and the rate of catalysis.

### Temperature and enzyme activity

Below a suitable optimum, a temperature rise increases kinetic energy and can increase successful enzyme–substrate encounters, raising reaction rate under otherwise matched conditions.

Above the optimum, high temperature can denature the enzyme, changing active-site shape so the substrate no longer binds appropriately; cooling a denatured enzyme does not generally restore its activity.

At low temperatures the enzyme and substrate move slowly and collide less often, so the rate is low, but the enzyme is not damaged. Warming increases the rate up to the optimum. Above the optimum the rate falls steeply, because the enzyme is denatured and the active site no longer fits the substrate. The optimum depends on the enzyme: many human enzymes work best at about 37 °C.

In an experiment, a shorter time to reach the same endpoint means a faster rate. A rate can be worked out as 1 divided by the time: an endpoint reached in 50 s gives a rate of 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 per second, and one reached in 25 s gives 0.04 per second, twice as fast. On a graph of rate against temperature, the curve rises to a peak at the optimum and then falls sharply.

### pH and active-site fit

Each enzyme works over a range of pH and works fastest at its optimum pH; changing the pH can change the shape of the active site.

At sufficiently unsuitable pH an enzyme can be denatured, so substrate binding and reaction rate decrease. Different enzymes need not have the same optimum pH.

Pepsin works in the stomach at about pH 2, amylase in the mouth works near pH 7, and enzymes in the small intestine work in slightly alkaline conditions. Moving away from the optimum in either direction lowers the rate, and a large change denatures the enzyme. A low rate at an unsuitable pH does not mean that the substrate has disappeared: the enzyme is simply less able to bind it.

To interpret an experiment, compare the pH used with the optimum of that enzyme. The same pH can be close to the optimum for one enzyme and far from the optimum for another. Keep pH separate from temperature and from the amount of enzyme, because each affects the rate in a different way. A graph of rate against pH rises to a peak at the optimum and falls on both sides.

### Investigating amylase and lipase

To investigate an enzyme factor, vary temperature or pH while keeping enzyme and substrate concentrations, volumes and other relevant conditions the same, then measure time or rate using a consistent endpoint.

For amylase acting on starch, the disappearance of the blue-black iodine colour can define an endpoint. For lipase acting on lipid, the fatty acids produced lower the pH, which can be followed with a pH indicator.

A standard amylase investigation uses spotting tiles. Mix starch solution and amylase solution in a tube in a water bath. Every 30 s, take a drop of the mixture to a tile and add iodine. The time at which the drop stays brown, instead of turning blue-black, is the endpoint. Repeat at different temperatures, for example 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C, bring the solutions to the temperature before mixing them, and keep the volume and concentration of the starch and amylase the same.

Use buffer solutions to compare pH values. A control with boiled amylase should not remove the starch, which shows that the enzyme is the cause. Repeat each condition and calculate a mean time, or a rate (1 ÷ time). For lipase, mix milk or cream with sodium carbonate solution and drops of phenolphthalein, which is pink in alkaline conditions. Bring this mixture and the lipase solution to the temperature in a water bath, then mix them and time it. As lipase breaks fat into fatty acids and glycerol, the fatty acids neutralise the alkali and the pink colour disappears; record the time taken. Repeat at a range of temperatures, with the same volumes each time, with a boiled-lipase control. To compare pH values, a fixed indicator colour is not a fair endpoint, because each mixture starts at a different pH; measure the change in pH over a fixed time with a pH meter or probe, with no buffer added.

### Enzymes in germination

During germination, enzymes break stored food into soluble substances that the embryo can use.

Amylase can break stored starch into sugars, which can be transported to growing regions and used in respiration.

Water taken up by the seed activates its enzymes, and a suitable temperature lets them work at a useful rate. In a dry seed the enzymes are inactive, which is why it can be stored for years. A germinating seed cannot photosynthesise until it has green tissue, so its first supply of usable food comes from the store in the seed. The enzyme breaks the store down into soluble products, and the enzyme itself is not the food.

### Biological washing powders

Biological washing powders contain enzymes that break particular stain materials into smaller, more soluble products.

Proteases act on protein stains and lipases on fats; effective washing depends on a suitable temperature and pH for the enzymes.

Proteases break protein stains such as blood or egg into amino acids and small chains that dissolve in water. Lipases break fats and oils into fatty acids and glycerol, which wash out more easily. The enzymes work at low temperatures, such as 30 to 40 °C, which saves energy and protects fabrics, but a very hot wash denatures them. An enzyme that acts on protein will not remove every coloured stain.

### Enzymes in food production

Enzymes can be used to change food materials by catalysing a desired reaction under controlled conditions.

Examples include pectinase helping release juice from fruit tissue and lactase splitting lactose into smaller sugars.

Pectinase breaks down pectin, which holds plant cells together, so crushed fruit releases more juice and the juice is clearer. Lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose, which is used to make lactose-free milk for people who cannot digest lactose. The enzyme must match the material: a protease would not change the sugar in milk. In industry the temperature and pH are controlled to keep the enzyme at its optimum, and adding more enzyme does not keep increasing the output once the substrate or another condition is limiting.

### Testing biological washing powders

Compare washing treatments using equally stained fabric, equal solution volumes and controlled washing time, agitation and temperature.

An enzyme contribution needs a suitable comparison such as a matched preparation with inactive enzyme; different commercial powders can also differ in non-enzyme ingredients.

Decide how stains will be scored before washing, for example by comparing the colour left on each cloth with a set of standard samples, in the same lighting and by an observer who does not know which treatment each cloth had. Cut the fabric into equal squares, stain each with the same amount of the same stain, and repeat the treatment on several squares. Use the same volume of solution, the same time, the same movement of the fabric and the same temperature.

To test the enzyme itself, compare a solution of the powder with a matched solution in which the enzyme has been denatured by boiling and cooled again. To test the effect of temperature, keep the powder concentration the same, because hot water also helps to dissolve stains without any enzyme. One powder on one stain does not show how well it works on every stain. Use only safe materials chosen by the teacher.

### Where this connects

The experimental planning, variables and uncertainty methods are in 1.2.1 to 1.2.6. Digestion in 3.6.2 uses enzyme specificity; germination and growth are developed in 4.3.5.

## 3.6 Autotrophic and heterotrophic nutrition

### Key points

| Mode | Source of organic nutrients | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Autotrophic | Made from inorganic materials using an energy source | A green plant photosynthesising |
| Heterotrophic | Obtained ready-made from other organisms or their products | A person eating beans |

Mineral uptake is not feeding on organic nutrients. Plants photosynthesise and also respire.

### Two ways of obtaining nutrients

Autotrophic nutrition makes organic nutrients from inorganic substances, using an energy source such as light.

Heterotrophic nutrition obtains ready-made organic nutrients from other organisms or their products.

A green bean plant takes carbon dioxide from the air and water from its surroundings. With light energy it builds sugar: it does not obtain this sugar by absorbing it from the soil. Mineral ions absorbed from soil are necessary materials, but they are not the plant’s food or an energy supply. A person eating beans takes in organic material made by another organism and is a heterotroph.

Nutrition concerns obtaining materials for growth, repair and respiration. Autotrophs also respire: making nutrients and releasing energy from nutrients are different processes. Heterotrophs include animals, fungi and many bacteria; they need not eat living prey. A fungus absorbing soluble substances from dead leaves is also obtaining ready-made organic nutrients. Classify the mode by where the organic nutrients come from rather than by whether the organism moves. Respiration then releases the energy stored in these nutrients, and it is the same process in both kinds of organism.

## 3.6.1 Photosynthesis and plant nutrition

### Key points

A leaf is the main organ of photosynthesis. This unit follows the process from leaf structure, through the mineral ions a plant needs, to the photosynthesis equation, the factors that limit its rate and the investigations used to study it.

| Topic | Essential connection |
|---|---|
| Leaf tissues | Epidermis admits light; palisade absorbs it; air spaces and stomata permit gas exchange; veins transport materials |
| Minerals | Iron supports chlorophyll formation; magnesium is in chlorophyll; phosphate supports DNA and ATP; nitrate supplies nitrogen for amino acids |
| Photosynthesis | Carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using light and chlorophyll |
| Products | Sugar is respired, transported as sucrose, or converted into cellulose, starch and other materials |
| Limiting conditions | Raising an input helps while that factor is limiting; a plateau does not uniquely identify the next limit |
| Investigation | Change one factor, measure an appropriate response, control others, repeat and evaluate error |
| Indicator | Light can give net carbon dioxide uptake; darkness gives respiratory release; respiration occurs in both |

### Leaf structure in cross-section

A typical dicotyledonous leaf has upper and lower epidermis surrounding palisade and spongy mesophyll.

Veins contain xylem and phloem, and stomata are pores bordered by guard cells.

In a leaf cross-section, follow the tissues from the upper surface downwards: waxy cuticle, upper epidermis, closely packed elongated palisade cells, irregular spongy cells with air spaces, and lower epidermis. A vein cuts across these layers. Within a typical vein, xylem lies towards the upper side and phloem towards the lower side. Guard cells surround an opening called a stoma, so a stoma is a pore and not a solid cell. Colours in prepared slides may come from stains, so identify tissues from their position and structure rather than their colour.

### Chloroplast distribution

Palisade mesophyll cells contain many chloroplasts near the illuminated upper surface of a typical leaf.

Chlorophyll in chloroplasts absorbs light energy for photosynthesis.

Spongy mesophyll also photosynthesises, but its looser arrangement serves gas movement. The ordinary epidermal cells are usually transparent and lack chloroplasts, allowing light to reach the mesophyll. Guard cells are an exception to this usual epidermal pattern. Location matters as well as number: chloroplast-rich cells near incoming light can absorb much of it before it passes through the leaf.

### Gas exchange within the leaf

Open stomata allow gases to diffuse between the atmosphere and the leaf air spaces; closure reduces this exchange.

Air spaces and moist mesophyll surfaces provide a large internal surface for gas exchange.

Carbon dioxide entering a stoma moves through air spaces, dissolves in the moist surface film and diffuses into cells. Oxygen can move out in the opposite direction when photosynthesis produces it faster than respiration uses it. The direction of net movement depends on concentration differences, not on the pore pumping gases. Closing stomata can conserve water but also restrict carbon dioxide entry.

### Transport through leaf veins

Xylem supplies a leaf with water and mineral ions from the roots.

Phloem transports sucrose and amino acids between sources and regions of demand or storage.

Water is a reactant in photosynthesis; ions support the cell structures and reactions that make it possible. Sugar made in a leaf can be converted to sucrose for transport to growing roots or storage organs. This removal also supplies parts that cannot photosynthesise. A vein is a transport bundle, not an air passage. Its xylem and phloem have different contents and functions.

### Structure linked to photosynthesis

The thin leaf shortens diffusion paths, while transparent surface layers admit light to chloroplast-rich mesophyll.

Connected air spaces let carbon dioxide reach a large area of mesophyll surface, and veins supply water.

An adaptation explanation connects a visible structure to a particular need. “Many chloroplasts” needs the link “absorb light”; “air spaces” needs “permit gases to reach mesophyll cells”. A broad lamina intercepts light, but the internal arrangement distributes that light and the raw materials. Think through a damaged leaf: if air spaces become water-filled while chlorophyll remains unchanged, carbon dioxide moves through the tissue more slowly because diffusion in water is slower than in air. Green colour alone therefore does not establish a normal rate. Each feature helps with one part of the process, and photosynthesis still needs light, carbon dioxide and water together.

### Preparing epidermal impressions

A dried clear nail-varnish impression can reproduce a leaf surface for viewing stomata with a microscope.

Comparing stomatal distributions requires equal sampled areas and several fields on each surface.

Apply a thin patch of clear varnish to upper and lower surfaces of a suitable leaf, away from major veins. Let it dry; lift the film with clear adhesive tape and put it flat on a labelled slide. Start with low power, focus, then choose a useful higher power. Count stomata in equal-sized fields without selecting unusually crowded areas. Use several fields and several leaves; calculate the mean count per equal area for each surface. Keep magnification and leaf type consistent. Varnish is flammable, so work with ventilation and keep it away from flames.

A worked example: counts of 18, 22 and 20 stomata in three fields on the lower surface give a mean of 20; counts of 4, 6 and 5 on the upper surface give a mean of 5. Many dicotyledonous leaves have more stomata on the lower surface, where less water is lost to the sunlit air.

### Drawing and interpreting a section

A biological drawing records structures actually visible in the specimen with clear outlines and appropriate proportions.

Leaf tissues should be identified using their arrangement and cell shapes, with accurate label lines.

Begin at low magnification to orientate the whole section, then inspect detail. Draw a large view, use continuous pencil lines without decorative shading, and separate individual cells where visible. Rule label lines to the named structure without crossing them. Include upper and lower epidermis, palisade and spongy tissues, a vein and a stoma where the chosen field shows them. Draw only what the section shows. A layer of elongated cells and a region of irregular cells can be distinguished even when staining hides chlorophyll.

To find the magnification of a drawing, measure the length of a structure on the drawing and divide by its actual length, with both in the same unit. A cell drawn 30 mm long that is really 0.06 mm long is magnified 500 times.

### Iron in plants

Iron ions are needed for chlorophyll formation.

A plant requires iron in small amounts for healthy green growth.

Iron is not the central atom of chlorophyll; magnesium has that role. Availability can matter as much as the amount in soil: a root must be able to absorb the ions. Nutrient solutions allow a plant to be supplied with a known mixture.

### Magnesium in plants

Magnesium is a constituent of chlorophyll.

A shortage of magnesium can reduce the amount of chlorophyll in leaves.

Magnesium is a mineral ion rather than an organic food. A plant with enough light still needs the raw materials to build its photosynthetic machinery.

### Phosphate in plants

Phosphate ions are needed to make DNA and energy-transfer molecules such as ATP.

Phosphate supply supports cell division and growth of roots and other tissues.

ATP transfers energy within cells; phosphate fertiliser does not supply light energy. DNA is the genetic material that must be copied when cells divide. These roles connect phosphate supply to production of new cells.

### Nitrate in plants

Nitrate ions provide nitrogen for making amino acids.

Amino acids are assembled into proteins needed for growth.

Proteins include enzymes and structural materials. Nitrate uptake and amino-acid synthesis allow a plant to turn some carbohydrate products into nitrogen-containing molecules. Nitrogen gas in the air is not directly usable by an ordinary plant root in the same way as dissolved nitrate.

### Iron deficiency

Iron deficiency can cause chlorosis because insufficient chlorophyll is formed.

Lower chlorophyll content can reduce photosynthesis and restrict growth when light capture becomes limiting.

Chlorosis means yellowing caused by a shortage of green chlorophyll. Iron-related chlorosis often shows first in young leaves. Several mineral deficiencies and other stresses can also make leaves yellow, so a controlled nutrient comparison is stronger evidence than colour alone. If adding iron restores greenness while matched untreated plants stay pale, that supports iron shortage as the cause.

### Magnesium deficiency

Magnesium deficiency can cause chlorosis because chlorophyll contains magnesium.

Reduced chlorophyll can restrict photosynthesis and leave less carbohydrate for growth.

Magnesium shortage often shows first in older leaves, because the plant moves magnesium from old leaves to new growth. Separate a role from an effect: “magnesium is part of chlorophyll” names the role; “less chlorophyll leads to less light capture and slower growth” explains the effect. A yellow plant still respires; it is photosynthesis that is reduced.

### Phosphate deficiency

Phosphate deficiency can restrict growth because cells lack sufficient material for DNA and ATP.

Poor phosphate supply can result in weak root development and reduced overall growth.

A stunted seedling may be limited by water, temperature or another nutrient. Comparing a complete solution with a solution lacking only phosphate, while controlling everything else, links a difference to phosphate. Phosphate is needed by roots as well as shoots, so root length is a useful measurement as well as leaf colour. For example, mean root lengths of 8.2 cm with phosphate and 3.4 cm without give a difference of 4.8 cm.

### Nitrate deficiency

Nitrate deficiency restricts amino-acid and protein production.

Reduced protein synthesis can cause stunted growth, often with yellowing leaves.

Nitrogen is needed for new cytoplasm and enzymes. Adding more sugar cannot replace the missing nitrogen, even though a plant needs both carbon-containing materials and nitrogen-containing materials to grow. Nitrate shortage usually shows first as yellowing of the older leaves, because the plant moves nitrogen from old leaves to new growth. Growth slows across the whole plant, and stems are often thin and weak.

### Investigating mineral shortage

A mineral-deficiency investigation compares a complete nutrient solution with one lacking the named mineral.

The comparison requires similar seedlings, matched growing conditions, repeated measurements and replication.

Germinate seedlings of the same species and similar initial size. Put replicated groups into complete solution and into otherwise identical solution without nitrate, supporting the roots and shielding solution from light to discourage algae. Keep solution volume, aeration, temperature and illumination alike. Record initial and later shoot height, leaf colour and, if appropriate, final dry mass; use the same growth interval. Replace or top up solutions consistently. Label units and calculate means for each group. Differences in initial plant size, unnoticed contamination or unequal light can weaken the comparison. Random allocation and rotating positions reduce position bias. Removing only the named mineral, and keeping the rest of the solution the same, is what links a difference in growth to that mineral.

### The photosynthesis word equation

Photosynthesis uses light energy absorbed by chlorophyll to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen.

The word equation is carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, with light and chlorophyll as conditions.

Write the conditions above the arrow or alongside the equation. Light supplies energy and chlorophyll absorbs it; neither is a raw material that is turned into sugar. Carbon enters in carbon dioxide, not in soil minerals. Water comes through the xylem. The glucose stores chemical energy that can later be released in respiration. Photosynthesis and respiration can occur in the same illuminated plant.

### A balanced photosynthesis equation

The balanced summary equation is 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.

The coefficients conserve carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms; light energy and chlorophyll are required conditions.

Count atoms to check the equation: six carbon atoms on each side, twelve hydrogen atoms on each side, and eighteen oxygen atoms on each side. A coefficient multiplies an entire molecule, so balance an equation by changing coefficients and never the formulae. This is an overall equation that summarises many separate reactions. Its glucose product contains chemical energy.

### Testing requirements for photosynthesis

A destarched leaf can be tested for newly accumulated starch after a controlled light treatment.

Light, chlorophyll and carbon dioxide can be investigated separately by suitable comparisons.

Destarch suitable plants in darkness beforehand so old starch is less likely to confuse the result. For light, cover part of a leaf on both sides with opaque material and expose the plant to light; the uncovered region is the comparison. For chlorophyll, mark green and non-green regions of a variegated leaf before testing it. For carbon dioxide, use matched destarched plants in sealed transparent containers: one has an absorber kept away from the plant, while the comparison lacks the absorber. Match illumination, temperature and duration.

For the starch test, kill the leaf in hot water, decolourise it in ethanol warmed in a hot-water bath, rinse to soften, then add iodine solution. Blue-black indicates starch; yellow-brown indicates no detected starch. Ethanol is flammable: use teacher supervision, eye protection and no naked flame. A green illuminated region in the same leaf helps show that the starch test and treatment worked.

### Light intensity and rate

Increasing light intensity raises photosynthesis rate while light is the limiting factor.

A plateau can occur when another factor limits the rate, so further light then gives no increase.

At a constant suitable temperature and carbon dioxide supply, a dimly lit plant has little light energy available. Increasing illumination can increase sugar production. This relationship does not continue without limit: carbon dioxide supply or temperature may then constrain it. On a graph of rate against light intensity, the rising part of the curve shows light limiting the rate and the flat part shows another factor limiting it.

### Carbon dioxide concentration and rate

Increasing carbon dioxide concentration increases photosynthesis rate while carbon dioxide is limiting.

At a plateau another factor, such as light intensity, prevents further increase.

Carbon dioxide is a reactant and carbon source. Its availability differs from the availability of light energy. In a closed greenhouse it may be used faster than it enters, particularly in bright conditions. Raising its concentration can help then, but cannot make up for darkness. On a graph of rate against carbon dioxide concentration, the curve rises and then flattens in the same way as for light. The units on each axis show what was measured, for example rate in cm³ of oxygen per minute.

### Temperature and rate

When temperature limits photosynthesis, warming towards an optimum can increase enzyme-controlled reaction rates.

Above a suitable range, photosynthesis can fall as enzymes are damaged and other heat stresses occur.

At low temperatures molecules move more slowly and enzyme reactions tend to proceed more slowly. Warming helps only if other needs are met. Beyond an enzyme’s optimum, its active site may change shape through denaturation, reducing activity. Cooling does not reverse denaturation. Colder conditions slow enzymes without killing them, and warming raises the rate only up to the optimum. The optimum depends on the plant and the conditions, so it differs between species.

### Building carbohydrates

Photosynthesis produces glucose, a simple carbohydrate.

Glucose units can be joined to make larger carbohydrates such as starch and cellulose.

Joining many simple sugar units forms a larger molecule with different properties. Starch makes a compact insoluble reserve, while cellulose forms strong cell walls. Plants also convert some sugar into sucrose for transport. These are chemical changes made by cells, not the absorption of ready-made starch from soil. A growing leaf uses its products as well as storing some.

### Using and storing photosynthetic products

Glucose can be respired, converted to cellulose or sucrose, or stored as starch.

Carbohydrate products can also provide material for fats and, with nitrogen, amino acids.

Respiration transfers energy for cell processes; it does not merely occur in darkness. Cellulose supports new walls, sucrose travels in phloem, and starch reserves can later be converted back to soluble sugars. Starch is insoluble, so storing it has a smaller direct osmotic effect than storing an equivalent amount as dissolved glucose. Seeds may store oils as well as starch. The product made and stored depends on the tissue and species.

### Limiting factors

A limiting factor is something present in the environment in short supply that restricts life processes.

A limiting factor can change when conditions change.

A plant with insufficient carbon dioxide cannot increase its photosynthesis merely because more light is provided. To test a suspected limit, increase that factor while keeping others constant. If rate rises, the intervention supports that factor having limited the earlier rate. A plateau alone does not uniquely identify what is limiting: several possible factors require further evidence.

### Identifying the active limit

A factor is supported as limiting when increasing it raises rate under otherwise matched conditions.

A lack of response to extra light alone does not distinguish carbon dioxide shortage from unsuitable temperature.

For example, at a fixed temperature a plant releases 4 cm³ oxygen per minute at both moderate and high illumination. Adding carbon dioxide at the higher illumination raises output to 7 cm³ per minute. The intervention supports carbon dioxide limiting the original plateau. Temperature was not ruled out merely by seeing a flat curve; it was the positive carbon dioxide response that supplied the extra evidence. On a graph, a curve that is flat at high light intensity and rises when carbon dioxide is added shows that carbon dioxide was limiting at the plateau.

### Investigating photosynthesis rate

Oxygen volume released by a submerged plant over a measured time can estimate its net photosynthetic output.

Light, carbon dioxide and temperature should be varied separately, with the other conditions controlled.

Place a healthy aquatic shoot in a suitable transparent vessel and collect its oxygen into a graduated system. Allow it to adjust after changing a condition, then measure gas volume over a fixed period. For light, change lamp distance or use measured illumination while a water bath prevents heating. For carbon dioxide, use defined hydrogencarbonate concentrations at constant temperature and illumination. For temperature, use water baths, keeping light and solution conditions fixed. Repeat each level and compare mean volume per minute. Bubble counts are less reliable because bubbles vary in size. Gas may dissolve before collection, and leaking joints lose gas; these limit the estimate. Respiration also consumes oxygen, so the collected amount represents net output.

### Greenhouse productivity

Carbon dioxide enrichment, suitable illumination and suitable temperature can increase photosynthesis when they remove a limiting factor.

Productivity depends on growth over time, not on maintaining the maximum level of each input.

Enrichment supplies a reactant; lamps provide energy; heating helps enzyme reactions in cold weather, while ventilation can avoid excessive heat. Increasing one input may shift the limitation to another, so there is no guarantee that doubling an input doubles growth. A greenhouse can waste energy by heating beyond a useful temperature or adding carbon dioxide when light is inadequate. Adequate water and minerals are still needed to turn the products of photosynthesis into new tissue.

### Gas exchange in light and darkness

Hydrogencarbonate indicator changes colour with carbon dioxide concentration: yellow at high levels, red/orange near atmospheric levels and purple at low levels.

An illuminated aquatic plant can remove carbon dioxide faster than respiration releases it, whereas in darkness respiration releases carbon dioxide without photosynthetic uptake.

Use equal masses of similar aquatic plant in equal volumes of indicator in sealed tubes. Illuminate one, wrap another fully in opaque material, and include a no-plant tube under each condition. Keep temperature, starting indicator colour and duration matched; use cool lighting to avoid warming one treatment. Repeat tubes. A purple light tube supports net uptake and a yellow dark tube supports net release, compared with stable no-plant controls. Light does not stop respiration: the colour indicates the balance of two processes. An unchanged colour can mean little net change rather than no reactions. Air leakage, handling with exhaled breath near the indicator or unsuitable plant material can alter the outcome.

## 3.6.2 Human nutrition

### Key points

Nutrition is how an organism takes in food and uses it. For people, a balanced diet supplies energy and the materials the body needs. This unit covers what happens when a nutrient is lacking, how needs change with age, activity and pregnancy, what happens to food in the alimentary canal, and how digested nutrients are absorbed and used.

| Process or need | Essential knowledge |
|---|---|
| Balanced diet | Suitable energy plus protein, fat, carbohydrate, vitamins, minerals, fibre and water |
| Deficiencies | Vitamin A: dim-light vision; D: rickets; C: scurvy; iodine: goitre; iron: anaemia |
| Changing needs | Growth, activity, pregnancy and milk production change requirements; averages do not determine individuals |
| Malnutrition | Includes shortage, excess and imbalance; energy sufficiency does not guarantee micronutrient sufficiency |
| Ingestion to egestion | Mouth entry → food breakdown → absorption → passing undigested remains through anus |
| Enzymes | Amylase: starch to maltose; maltase: maltose to glucose; proteases: proteins ultimately to amino acids; lipase: fat to fatty acids and glycerol |
| Bile | Helps neutralise acidic contents and emulsifies fat; not an enzyme |
| Absorption | Small-intestinal villi provide large area; capillaries carry sugars/amino acids, lacteals carry lipid material |
| Liver | Portal blood delivers absorbed nutrients; excess glucose forms glycogen; excess amino acids are deaminated to form urea |
| Water | Absorbed in small intestine and colon |

### Vitamin A deficiency

Insufficient vitamin A can impair vision in dim light and damage healthy epithelial surfaces.

Animal foods such as eggs and liver supply vitamin A; orange vegetables and dark green leaves supply precursors the body can convert.

A deficiency is an inadequate supply for the body’s needs, not necessarily complete absence. A restricted diet without suitable vitamin A sources can lead to night blindness. Variety matters: a filling meal may still lack a micronutrient. Night blindness has other possible causes, so a doctor confirms a deficiency with tests. Eating a variety of foods prevents shortage; very large doses of vitamin A supplements can be harmful.

### Vitamin D deficiency

Vitamin D supports calcium absorption and healthy bone mineralisation.

Insufficient vitamin D can cause rickets in children, with soft or deformed bones.

Vitamin D can come from foods such as oily fish and fortified foods and can be formed in skin exposed to sunlight. A shortage may arise from insufficient dietary supply together with limited skin production. Calcium is needed as well: vitamin D does not replace the mineral. In growing children, poor mineralisation may cause bowed legs under body weight. Calcium is found in milk, small fish eaten with their bones, and green leafy vegetables, so a child needs a diet that supplies both calcium and vitamin D.

### Vitamin C deficiency

Vitamin C supports formation and maintenance of connective tissues.

Long-term deficiency can cause scurvy, including bleeding gums and poor wound healing.

Fresh fruits and vegetables provide vitamin C. A very restricted diet with little of these foods can supply insufficient vitamin C even if energy intake is adequate. Heating and long storage can reduce the vitamin content of some foods. A mixed diet with suitable fruit or vegetables supports tissue maintenance; vitamin C is not itself an energy source.

### Iodine deficiency

Iodine is required for thyroid hormone production.

Iodine deficiency can cause enlargement of the thyroid gland, called goitre.

Iodised salt can supply iodine, but this does not justify consuming large quantities of salt. Seafood is another possible dietary source. Goitre is an enlargement in the neck associated with the thyroid, not an enlargement of red blood cells. Keep iodine and iron separate: iodine is linked to thyroid function; iron is linked to haemoglobin.

### Iron deficiency in people

Iron is needed to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in red blood cells.

Iron deficiency can cause anaemia, with tiredness and reduced capacity for activity.

Meat, pulses and some green vegetables can contribute dietary iron. Insufficient intake or increased losses may lead to shortage. Less haemoglobin reduces oxygen-carrying capacity, so aerobic respiration during activity can become more difficult to support. Tiredness alone does not identify iron deficiency because it has many possible causes. Iron in food is not the same as the whole red blood cell; cells also require other materials.

### A balanced diet for children

A balanced diet provides adequate energy and appropriate amounts of carbohydrate, protein, fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre and water.

Children need protein for growth and calcium with vitamin D for developing bones and teeth.

Balanced means suited to needs, not equal masses of each nutrient. A meal of pearl-millet porridge, beans, vegetables and a suitable calcium source can combine energy, protein and micronutrients; no one dish guarantees the complete daily balance. A growing child may need less total energy than a large active adult but more growth materials relative to body size. Fibre supports bowel function and water supports transport and reactions. Variety across meals helps avoid shortages.

### Adults and workers

Adults require nutrients for maintenance and repair as well as energy for metabolism and activity.

Physically demanding work generally increases energy needs compared with sedentary work in otherwise similar adults.

A road worker carrying materials may respire more fuel during the working day than a worker seated at a desk. The comparison depends on body size, duration and actual effort; a job title alone does not determine an exact energy requirement. Adults still need protein, micronutrients, fibre and water even after height growth has ended. Persistent excess energy intake can lead to increased fat stores, while insufficient intake forces use of reserves.

### Nutrition during nursing

Producing breast milk requires energy and materials such as protein, calcium and water.

A nursing mother needs an adequate varied diet to support her own tissues as well as milk production.

Milk transfers nutrients to an infant, so ongoing supply matters as well as the mother’s existing reserves. Foods providing protein and calcium, sufficient energy and adequate drinking water can support these demands. Eating a varied diet with enough energy helps more than eating much more of one food. Requirements vary with the amount of milk produced and other individual factors. Family access to sufficient food can therefore affect two people at once.

### Athletes and training

Sustained physical training increases energy use through muscle respiration.

Athletes also require water, mineral replacement and protein for tissue repair as part of a balanced diet.

Carbohydrate is a useful fuel source during demanding activity; some is stored as glycogen before use. Protein supports repair and adaptation but is not a substitute for sufficient overall energy. Sweating increases water loss; replacement requirements depend on weather, duration and intensity. An athlete’s diet still includes fruit, vegetables and other micronutrient sources. More protein by itself does not guarantee better performance.

### Nutrition for people living with HIV

Adequate nutrition supports tissue maintenance and immune function in people living with HIV.

Illness, reduced appetite or diarrhoea can make adequate nutrient intake and absorption more difficult.

A varied diet with enough energy and protein, and safe food and water, supports health. Needs vary with the person’s health and treatment. Good nutrition supports general health and recovery; it does not remove HIV, and antiretroviral treatment is still needed.

Difficulty getting enough food can come from illness, household resources or access to food. Careful food hygiene matters because infections are more dangerous when immunity is reduced. People living with HIV can safely share meals and normal social contact.

### Age and dietary needs

Growth raises requirements for building materials relative to body size.

Energy requirements depend on body size and activity as well as age.

A rapidly growing adolescent may require substantial energy and protein, while an older sedentary adult may need less energy but still needs adequate micronutrients. A shorter person does not necessarily need less of every nutrient, and older people still need protein to maintain their muscles.

### Sex-related needs and individual variation

Average differences in body size and muscle mass can contribute to different average energy requirements between sexes.

Menstrual blood loss can increase iron requirements, while pregnancy and lactation add other demands.

On average, males and females differ in body size and muscle mass, and the biological reasons concern body composition and reproduction. An average does not decide one person’s exact need. Two people with the same activity and body size can have similar energy demands, while menstrual blood loss may still change iron needs. When comparing the needs of two people, say which need differs: energy, protein or a particular mineral such as iron.

### Activity and energy demand

Working muscles increase respiration to supply energy for contraction.

Greater duration or intensity of activity usually increases energy requirements.

Compare actual activity rather than labels such as “busy”. Walking a long distance, carrying loads or repeated vigorous exercise consumes energy, whereas concentrated mental work does not necessarily require a similar increase. If energy expenditure falls but intake stays high for a long time, surplus energy can be stored as fat. A single meal or day does not establish a long-term imbalance.

### Pregnancy and dietary needs

Pregnancy requires materials and energy for fetal development and maternal tissue changes.

Protein, iron and calcium are among the nutrients needed in adequate amounts during pregnancy.

A developing fetus produces new cells, while the pregnant person also develops tissues and a larger blood supply. Protein supports new tissue, calcium supports developing bones and iron supports haemoglobin. The extra energy needed is much less than double, and specific requirements vary during pregnancy and with the person’s existing diet.

### Breastfeeding changes nutrient balance

Lactation transfers water and nutrients from mother to infant through milk.

Milk production can increase energy and material requirements beyond maintenance.

Milk contains fat, sugar, protein, mineral ions and water. The mother’s body must obtain these materials or draw on reserves, and synthesis also costs energy. Extra energy alone does not replace calcium or protein. The needs of a person producing milk differ from those of the same person after lactation ends, although activity and body size also influence the comparison.

### Starvation

Starvation occurs when food supplies insufficient energy and nutrients for a prolonged period.

The body uses stored materials and eventually body tissues, causing loss of mass, weakness and impaired function.

Malnutrition includes too little, too much or an unsuitable balance of nutrients. Starvation is a severe shortage, not a label for missing one meal. Fat reserves can provide fuel, but continued shortage also leads to protein breakdown and tissue loss. Children may stop growing normally, and resistance to infection can fall. Drought can reduce food production, while poverty or disrupted distribution may prevent access even where food exists elsewhere.

### Constipation

Insufficient fibre or water intake can contribute to constipation.

Hard stools that are difficult or infrequent to pass are characteristic of constipation.

Fibre is not digested by human digestive enzymes but helps retain water and adds bulk to bowel contents. Water intake supports softer stools. The colon absorbs water, so contents that remain there longer may become harder. Whole grains, pulses, fruit and vegetables contribute fibre. Some cases of constipation have other causes, such as illness or medicines.

### Diet and coronary heart disease

A diet high in saturated fat can increase the risk of fatty deposits narrowing coronary arteries.

Narrowed coronary arteries can restrict oxygen supply to heart muscle and contribute to coronary heart disease.

Risk is not certainty. Smoking, inherited susceptibility, activity and other factors also affect coronary disease. If heart muscle receives insufficient oxygen, its aerobic respiration is restricted; severe blockage may kill part of the muscle. Excess salt can contribute to raised blood pressure, adding cardiovascular strain. Distinguish the artery supplying the heart muscle from the cavities through which the heart pumps blood.

### Obesity and energy balance

Long-term energy intake exceeding energy expenditure can increase fat stores and lead to obesity.

Obesity is associated with increased risks of conditions including cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes.

Both intake and expenditure matter. High-energy foods can make surplus intake easier, but body mass is influenced by several factors, and judging a person’s habits from appearance is unreliable. Health investigations need sensitive, voluntary participation and protection of personal information. A change in a few days can reflect water as well as fat, so a sustained pattern is more informative about energy imbalance.

### Scurvy as malnutrition

Scurvy results from a sustained shortage of vitamin C.

Bleeding gums and poor wound healing reflect impaired connective-tissue maintenance.

A person can consume adequate energy yet develop scurvy if the diet lacks vitamin C. This distinguishes a specific micronutrient deficiency from starvation. Treating a deficiency needs the missing nutrient itself, not just a larger serving of the same foods. Scurvy is treated by supplying vitamin C, for example through fresh fruit and vegetables.

### Investigating dietary health locally

A dietary survey can investigate associations between food intake and health indicators but does not by itself establish causation.

Health data collection requires voluntary consent, confidentiality and consistent non-judgemental questions.

For a school investigation in Namibia, use anonymous volunteer records or an anonymised dataset prepared by the teacher. The aim is to look for patterns in groups, not to diagnose individuals. Define a focused question, for example whether frequency of sugary drinks is associated with a recorded dental-decay indicator. Record portions consistently over the same interval. Investigate fat, meat and salt through separate defined measures: frequent fatty meat may raise saturated-fat intake, while processed salty foods can raise salt intake. Meat also provides useful protein and iron, so “meat is harmful” is not a sound conclusion. Sugar frequency is relevant to tooth decay and surplus energy; high salt intake can contribute to high blood pressure. Match or record age, activity and other confounders where feasible. Self-report can be inaccurate, samples may be unrepresentative, and an association cannot distinguish all alternative explanations.

### Why famine occurs

Drought and flooding can reduce harvests or damage food stores and transport routes.

Unequal access and distribution can prevent people obtaining food even when supplies exist elsewhere.

Population growth can increase demand faster than food production and distribution expand.

Famine is a severe widespread shortage of accessible food. It is not explained by a single event in every place. Drought reduces water available for crops and livestock, while flooding may destroy crops and roads at the same time. Damaged roads can prevent remaining supplies reaching households. Low income or disrupted trade can also limit access to food. Increasing demand adds pressure when land, water, harvests or infrastructure cannot keep pace. A district with a reasonable total grain supply may still have hungry households if supplies and purchasing power are uneven. Both production and access matter, because a national total does not guarantee food for every family.

### Ingestion

The taking in of substances into the body through the mouth.

Food is ingested before digestion.

The term describes entry, not the breakdown of a molecule. Drinking water is also ingestion, even though water does not need enzyme digestion.

### Mechanical digestion

The breakdown of food into smaller pieces without chemical change to the food molecules.

Chewing breaks a piece of food into smaller pieces.

Its starch molecules remain starch molecules at this stage. The increased exposed surface lets enzymes act more readily; mechanical processing and enzyme action therefore complement one another.

### Chemical digestion

The breakdown of large, insoluble molecules into smaller, soluble molecules.

Enzymes catalyse the chemical change.

Small soluble products can cross the intestinal lining more readily than the large food molecules. Dissolving a substance or grinding it finely is not itself the same as breaking its molecules into smaller ones.

### Absorption

The movement of small food molecules and ions through the wall of the intestine into the blood.

Absorption follows digestion for nutrients that began as large insoluble molecules.

It is a movement into the body’s transport system, not the conversion of starch to sugar. Water, ions and some nutrients already small enough can be absorbed without prior digestion.

### Egestion

The passing out of food that has not been digested or absorbed through the anus.

The rectum stores faeces before egestion.

Egestion differs from excretion: undigested food has not become a waste product of cell metabolism. Urine and carbon dioxide are examples of excretory outputs, whereas undigested fibre leaves in faeces.

### The alimentary canal as a sequence

The mouth ingests and mechanically processes food, while the stomach and small intestine carry out further digestion.

The small intestine absorbs digested nutrients; the colon absorbs water and the rectum stores faeces before egestion.

Trace a meal through the alimentary canal, a continuous tube from mouth to anus. Waves of muscle contraction called peristalsis push food along it.

| Part | Main functions here |
|---|---|
| Mouth and salivary glands | Teeth break food; saliva moistens it and supplies amylase |
| Oesophagus | Muscular contractions move the swallowed bolus towards the stomach |
| Stomach | Mixes food with acid and protease-containing gastric juice |
| Duodenum | Receives bile and pancreatic digestive juice; chemical digestion continues |
| Ileum and remaining small intestine | Completes digestion and absorbs products through villi |
| Colon | Absorbs water from remaining contents |
| Rectum and anus | Store faeces and control their release |

The liver makes bile, the gall bladder stores and releases it, and the pancreas supplies digestive enzymes. Food passes along the gut and does not pass through the liver or pancreas.

### Cholera

Cholera is an infectious disease caused by a bacterium.

Contaminated water or food can carry the bacterium into the alimentary canal.

The bacterium affects the intestine; the resulting severe watery diarrhoea can rapidly remove water and salts. Clean drinking water, sanitation and hygienic food preparation reduce transmission. A pathogen causes the disease, while poor sanitation increases the chance of exposure. Cholera is an infection and not an inherited condition. Treatment replaces the lost water and salts, using a drinking solution of water, salts and sugar.

### How cholera causes fluid loss

Cholera bacteria produce a toxin that stimulates chloride-ion secretion into the small intestine.

The increased gut solute concentration draws water into the intestine by osmosis, causing diarrhoea and losses of water and salts.

Follow the direction: ions move into the gut lumen, then water follows from body fluids across the intestinal lining. Severe diarrhoea therefore dehydrates the person rather than washing excess body water away harmlessly. Fluid can continue entering the intestine even if the person has drunk little, because body fluids supply it. Treatment therefore has to replace both the water and the salts that were lost. Osmotic reasoning connects this to the movement of water across membranes taught in 3.3.2.

### Digestive enzymes and their conditions

Digestive enzymes catalyse breakdown of large food molecules into smaller products.

Each enzyme works best within a suitable pH and temperature range; excessive heat can denature it.

An enzyme’s substrate must fit its active site. Unsuitable pH can alter the interactions needed for activity; excessive temperature changes the protein’s shape. Below an optimum, cooling generally slows reactions without the same permanent damage as denaturation. Different enzymes work in different parts of the canal: an acidic stomach suits its protease, while intestinal enzymes operate in less acidic conditions. Each enzyme has its own optimum pH.

### Why chemical digestion is needed

Large insoluble food molecules cannot readily be absorbed through the intestinal wall.

Chemical digestion produces smaller soluble molecules suitable for absorption.

Ingesting protein does not mean whole protein molecules normally move straight into blood. Proteases break them into smaller products, ultimately amino acids, which can cross the lining and be used to build the body’s own proteins. A body therefore digests food and then constructs its own tissues, rather than directly inserting pieces of a meal into a muscle. Nutrients such as water and ions do not need this molecular breakdown.

### The digestion of starch

Salivary amylase begins converting starch to maltose in the mouth.

Pancreatic amylase continues starch digestion in the small intestine, and maltase converts maltose to glucose.

Chewing exposes more food surface to saliva. Swallowed starch may continue to meet salivary amylase briefly, but acid conditions in the stomach inhibit its action. In the small intestine, pancreatic juice supplies more amylase in suitable conditions. Maltase associated with the intestinal lining acts on maltose, producing glucose for absorption. Starch → maltose → glucose separates the two steps and enzymes. Stomach acid is not an enzyme: it only changes the pH.

In a simple test, starch solution mixed with saliva at body temperature stops giving a blue-black colour with iodine after a few minutes, while a control made with boiled saliva still does. Boiling denatures amylase, so the boiled-saliva tube shows that the starch disappeared in the first tube because of active enzyme, not because of anything else in saliva or the conditions.

### Amylase

Amylase acts on starch, producing maltose.

The smaller soluble sugar product differs chemically from its starch substrate.

Substrate names identify what an enzyme acts on; product names identify what leaves the reaction. A typical amylase does not digest protein because its active site is suited to a different substrate. In digestion, amylase is one stage rather than the complete pathway to glucose. The mouth and pancreas provide amylase, but the reaction depends on local conditions.

### Proteases

Proteases break down proteins, with amino acids as the eventual products of complete protein digestion.

Different proteases can act in the stomach and small intestine under different suitable conditions.

Some protein digestion passes through shorter chains before free amino acids are produced. Give protein as the substrate and amino acids as the overall end-products. The stomach’s acid provides a suitable environment for its protease, whereas intestinal proteases have different conditions.

### Lipase

Lipase digests fats to fatty acids and glycerol.

Emulsification increases the fat surface accessible to lipase without itself chemically digesting the fat.

A large oil drop has less exposed surface than the same amount divided into many small drops. Since lipase acts where fat meets the watery contents, division into droplets can increase reaction rate under suitable conditions.

### Stomach acid and bacteria

Hydrochloric acid in gastric juice helps kill many bacteria swallowed in food.

The stomach’s acid is a defence but does not make contaminated food harmless.

Acidic conditions damage many microorganisms. Some pathogens or their effects can survive this barrier, so food hygiene remains necessary. Acid is not an enzyme: it changes conditions and can affect bacteria without being a biological catalyst. Distinguish this protective role from the separate effect on the pH needed by stomach protease.

### Stomach acid and protease activity

Hydrochloric acid creates an acidic environment in the stomach.

This acidic environment is suitable for the stomach’s protease activity.

An enzyme’s suitable pH is linked to the structure and interactions of its active site. Acid helps create the right conditions for stomach protease; other digestive enzymes work best at a higher pH. When acidic stomach contents enter the small intestine, their pH must be adjusted for intestinal enzyme activity. That is a different stage of digestion.

### Bile and acidity

Bile is produced by the liver, stored in the gall bladder and released into the small intestine.

Its alkaline components help neutralise acidic material arriving from the stomach.

Pancreatic secretions also contribute to neutralising the mixture. The resulting less acidic environment suits intestinal enzymes better. Bile is not hydrochloric acid and does not make the stomach more acidic. Keep the locations straight: stomach acid lowers pH before the material reaches the intestine; alkaline secretions then raise pH. Food stays in the gut while bile arrives through a duct.

### Bile and fat droplets

Bile salts emulsify fat, dividing it into small droplets.

Emulsification increases total surface area for lipase and is not chemical digestion of fat molecules.

The molecules remain fat molecules after emulsification. Lipase subsequently catalyses their chemical breakdown. Shaking oil with water may briefly disperse it, but droplets tend to join again; bile salts help maintain a finer dispersion. Thus bile helps enzyme action without being a digestive enzyme itself. Think “droplet size” for emulsification and “molecular products” for lipase.

### Investigating bile salts

A matched oil-and-water comparison with and without bile salts can demonstrate emulsification.

To isolate emulsification, keep oil amount, water volume, shaking and observation time the same.

Put equal oil and water volumes into identical transparent tubes. Add a measured bile-salt solution to one and an equal volume of water to the comparison; shake both for the same time and leave them together. Observe separation or the persistence of a cloudy dispersion at fixed times. Use replicated tubes. Without lipase, this tests physical dispersion rather than digestion; cloudiness is not evidence that fatty acids have formed. A separate lipase investigation could test rate, but pH needs careful control because bile’s alkalinity can itself change an indicator. Keep tubes stoppered for shaking, avoid contact with reagents and follow teacher instructions.

### Where digested nutrients enter the body

The small intestine is the main region for absorption of digested food.

Absorption moves products across the intestinal lining into transport fluids.

The folded lining bears villi, increasing the area through which materials can pass. Most sugars and amino acids enter blood capillaries; absorbed lipid products take a different route through lacteals after processing within the lining cells. The stomach mainly stores, mixes and begins digestion of a meal rather than carrying out most nutrient absorption. The colon absorbs water, not the bulk of digested food.

### Why villi increase absorption

Villi are numerous projections of the small-intestinal lining that increase its internal surface area.

A larger surface permits more nutrient transfer at the same time under suitable conditions.

A long tube gains still more area from folds and projections. Surface area is different from the volume of food inside it: a lining can have many more absorbing cells without a proportionate increase in tube volume. A villus has a thin epithelial covering and a transport supply beneath it, so the enlarged surface is connected to routes that carry absorbed nutrients away. Damaged villi can therefore impair absorption even when digestion has produced normal small molecules.

### Structure of a villus

A villus has a thin epithelial covering, blood capillaries and a central lymph vessel called a lacteal.

Blood capillaries take up sugars and amino acids, while lacteals receive absorbed lipid material.

The thin covering shortens the transfer distance. Flowing blood carries absorbed products away, helping maintain concentration differences. The lacteal leads into the lymphatic system, which later returns material to the blood circulation. A villus is not a single cell, and its blood capillaries are not the same tube as its lacteal. In a drawing of a villus, label the epithelium, the blood capillaries and the lacteal separately.

### The hepatic portal route

The hepatic portal vein carries nutrient-rich blood from the intestines to the liver.

The liver processes absorbed nutrients before much of this blood returns to the general circulation.

“Hepatic” refers to the liver. The portal vein links intestinal capillaries to the liver and does not carry absorbed food straight to the heart. The hepatic artery supplies oxygenated blood to the liver; blood leaving the liver enters hepatic veins and then the vena cava. These are different directions and functions. Most absorbed lipids enter the lymph in the lacteals first, not the intestinal blood capillaries.

### Excess glucose in the liver

The liver can convert excess absorbed glucose into glycogen for storage.

Glycogen can later be broken down to glucose when blood glucose supply needs support.

After a carbohydrate-rich meal, absorption raises glucose supply. Storing some as glycogen helps prevent an excessive rise and provides a reserve between meals. The reverse change makes glucose available when it is needed. Glycogen is an animal storage carbohydrate; it is not the plant reserve starch. The hormones insulin and glucagon control this storage and release.

### Excess amino acids

Excess amino acids are deaminated in the liver rather than stored as a separate amino-acid reserve.

The nitrogen-containing portion is converted into urea for excretion, while the remaining material can be used as fuel or converted for storage.

Deamination means the removal of the nitrogen-containing part of amino acids to form urea. Blood carries urea to the kidneys, which remove it in urine. The liver forms it; the kidneys excrete it. The process differs from protein digestion: digestion produces amino acids in the gut, whereas deamination processes surplus amino acids already absorbed into the body. A diet containing protein therefore does not simply fill a store of unchanged amino acids.

### Fat as a reserve

Fat stores a concentrated supply of chemical energy for later respiration.

Stored body fat also contributes insulation and cushioning.

A long-term energy surplus can increase fat storage; a sustained deficit can mobilise reserves. Lipids store much energy relative to mass and are largely insoluble, making them useful reserves rather than dissolved solutes. Too much stored fat increases the risk of conditions such as heart disease.

### Water absorption

Water is absorbed in both the small intestine and the colon.

Normally, as digested food and ions are absorbed, the gut contents become more dilute than the blood, so water follows by osmosis; if solutes build up in the gut, as in cholera, water moves the other way.

Much water is absorbed in the small intestine, including water from drinks and digestive secretions. The colon recovers additional water from the remaining contents, helping form faeces. It is therefore wrong to give the colon as the sole site of water absorption. Excessive fluid loss in diarrhoea can disrupt this recovery and deplete body water and salts. The amount of water in the gut is not simply the amount just drunk because secretions add further fluid.

## 3.7 Transport in plants

### Key points

| Route or process | Essential idea |
|---|---|
| Root | Outer epidermis and root hairs; cortex; central xylem with phloem between its arms |
| Stem | Vascular bundles in a ring; xylem inside each bundle, phloem outside |
| Water uptake | Osmosis into root cells when the soil-to-cell water-potential gradient favours entry |
| Xylem stream | Evaporation from leaves pulls connected water upwards |
| Transpiration | Evaporation at mesophyll surfaces, then vapour diffusion through stomata |
| Environment | Temperature, wind, humidity and stomatal opening change water loss under stated conditions |
| Wilting | Loss of cell turgor when water loss exceeds replacement |
| Translocation | Phloem carries sucrose and amino acids from sources to receiving regions |

### Root structure

A young dicotyledonous root has an outer epidermis, some of whose cells extend into root hairs, surrounding a broad cortex.

In a typical young dicotyledonous root cross-section, xylem is central and star-shaped, with phloem between its arms.

A root hair is an extension of one epidermal cell, not a separate root. Its thin wall and large surface area bring the cell close to soil water. The cortex consists of many cells between the epidermis and the transport tissues. Water crosses this region on its way to the xylem. Xylem transports water and mineral ions; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids.

Start with the whole organ before identifying individual cells. In a transverse section, the epidermis forms the outside boundary and the cortex is the wide band inside it. At the centre, the xylem forms a star shape made of large, thick-walled cells, and the smaller cells in the gaps between the arms of the star are phloem. Root hairs grow only near the growing tip, so a section cut further back may show none; the central xylem star still identifies the organ as a root.

### Recognising a dicotyledonous stem

A young dicotyledonous stem has an epidermis surrounding a cortex, with vascular bundles arranged in a ring.

Within a typical young dicotyledonous stem vascular bundle, xylem faces the centre and phloem faces the outside.

The epidermis protects the surface. The cortex lies beneath it. Each vascular bundle contains transport tissues, but their arrangement differs from that in the young root: the bundles form a ring near the edge of the stem instead of a central star. In a stem cross-section, follow the circle of bundles and pick out the inner xylem and the outer phloem in one bundle before identifying them elsewhere. Tissue position is more reliable than colour, because different stains colour tissues differently.

A longitudinal section shows vessels running along the stem rather than a ring of bundles. Section direction therefore changes the pattern seen, without changing the function of the tissue. To tell a root from a stem in an unlabelled transverse section, look at the arrangement: a central star of xylem means root, a ring of separate bundles means stem.

### Drawing root and stem sections

A low-power biological plan drawing records the relative sizes and positions of tissues visible in a specimen, without adding invented cells.

A detailed drawing of transport tissue should show observed cell outlines and label recognisable xylem vessels and phloem accurately.

Place a prepared slide on the microscope stage and locate the section at low power. Adjust focus and illumination before drawing; then use higher power for a small region if individual transport cells are needed. Use a large drawing with single clear lines, no decorative shading and labels ruled to the exact structure. Keep relative proportions faithful to the section. State what was drawn and the section direction. Draw only what you can see in the specimen, not structures remembered from a textbook diagram.

Xylem vessels have conspicuous thick walls and open spaces inside. Phloem is identified from its position and the cells you can observe, not drawn as an invented large empty tube. An outline of the outside of a root cannot replace a drawing of a tissue section. The microscope procedure builds on the skills in 1.2.4.

### Water from soil to atmosphere

Water enters a root hair cell by osmosis when soil water has a higher water potential than the cell contents, crossing a partially permeable membrane.

Water crosses the root through cell walls and through cells, enters xylem, and moves upwards in a continuous column pulled by evaporation from leaves.

In a leaf, water moves from xylem to mesophyll tissues, evaporates from moist cell surfaces, and diffuses as vapour through stomata.

Osmosis describes the parts of the route where water crosses a partially permeable membrane; it does not describe bulk flow along an open xylem vessel. Water moving through root cell walls need not cross a membrane at each successive wall. The route includes membrane crossings before the water enters the xylem. The cortex is a band of many cells, not a single membrane, and root cells do not pump water up the stem.

Evaporation from mesophyll surfaces removes liquid water from the leaf. Replacement water is drawn from the xylem, producing a pull transmitted down the connected water column. Attraction between water molecules helps the column stay continuous. Xylem vessel walls are strengthened so they resist collapse under this pull.

Water supply depends on the soil-to-root gradient and an intact conducting route. A plant can therefore wilt in wet soil if root uptake or xylem transport is damaged. Mineral-ion uptake is a separate process from water movement, because ions are often taken up by active transport (see 3.3.3).

### Tracing the water pathway with dye

A cut leafy shoot placed in a suitable aqueous dye can show the water-conducting pathway when coloured tissue is examined in stem and leaf sections.

Dye location indicates where the solution travelled under the investigation conditions, but does not by itself measure water-transport speed.

Choose fresh similar shoots and use a suitable dilute stain selected by the teacher. Cut the stem under water to reduce the entry of air into the conducting tubes, then place it promptly in the stain. A similar shoot in water without dye shows what natural colour looks like. Keep exposure time, shoot size and environment comparable. Examine fresh transverse sections at measured heights and inspect leaf veins after a fixed interval. Handle the cutting tool using the laboratory's supervised procedure.

Record which tissues are coloured and repeat with several shoots. Coloured xylem, rather than an evenly coloured stem surface, supports a transport-pathway conclusion. Rinse the outside before sectioning to reduce contamination. If no colour appears, the likely causes are too short a time, air in the xylem or an unsuitable stain; it does not show that the plant has no xylem.

### The meaning of transpiration

Transpiration is loss of water vapour from plant leaves by evaporation of water at the surfaces of mesophyll cells followed by diffusion of water vapour through the stomata.

The site of evaporation is the moist mesophyll surface, whereas a stoma is a route by which the vapour leaves the leaf.

The definition contains two physical processes in order. Evaporation changes liquid water to vapour inside the leaf. Diffusion then moves vapour from the air spaces towards the outside air when the vapour concentration is lower outside. Water does not first become vapour inside the stoma. A stoma is a pore, not a water-producing cell. Root absorption supplies replacement water but is not itself transpiration.

### Describing the transpiration stream

Transpiration draws replacement water from leaf xylem as mesophyll surfaces lose water by evaporation.

The stream through xylem supplies water for cells and transports dissolved mineral ions from roots.

Trace liquid water upwards through root, stem and leaf veins, then distinguish liquid from vapour at the mesophyll. The water reaching the leaf can support cell turgor or supply reactions such as photosynthesis as well as being lost as vapour. The stream is a flow through connected vessels, not the movement of a series of separate water-containing cells. Transpiration continues according to environmental conditions and stomatal opening, whether or not the leaf is actively photosynthesising at that moment.

### Temperature and water loss

With adequate water supply and similar stomatal opening, a higher temperature increases evaporation from mesophyll surfaces.

Faster vapour diffusion and evaporation can increase transpiration and replacement water uptake when other conditions are comparable.

A temperature comparison must keep light, humidity and air movement as similar as possible. Heating with a lamp may change temperature and light together, so it would not isolate one factor. Higher temperature increases transpiration as long as the stomata stay open and water is available. Severe heat or water shortage may close the stomata and reverse the expected increase. A greater uptake reading supports increased water demand, but some absorbed water is retained in growth and cell contents.

Worked example: a shoot takes up 4.0 cm³ of water in 20 min at 20 °C and 7.0 cm³ in 20 min at 30 °C. The rates are 0.20 and 0.35 cm³/min, so uptake is 0.15 cm³/min greater at the higher temperature.

### Wind and the boundary layer

Air movement removes humid air from near a leaf surface.

Where stomata remain open and water supply is adequate, removal of humid air steepens the vapour concentration gradient and tends to increase transpiration.

In still air, vapour leaving the stomata accumulates close to the leaf. A gentle air current replaces this with drier air. The effect is on the air next to the stomata; wind does not push liquid water out of the leaf. Strong drying may cause the stomata to close, so uptake does not keep rising with wind speed. Keep the fan far enough away to avoid heating or physically damaging the shoot and measure over a fixed time.

### Humidity and the vapour gradient

Humidity describes the water vapour in air; more humid air reduces the vapour concentration difference between leaf air spaces and the atmosphere.

With other conditions held similar, higher humidity tends to reduce transpiration and hence replacement water uptake.

The mechanism is diffusion, not absorption of air into roots. Saturated air outside a leaf leaves little gradient for net outward diffusion, even if liquid water can still evaporate and condense locally. Humidity and temperature must be considered separately: changing temperature can change relative humidity as well as evaporation. To test humidity, keep air temperature and movement matched and let the shoot adjust to the new conditions before timing uptake.

### Light and stomatal opening

In many plants, illumination promotes stomatal opening, allowing water vapour to diffuse out of the leaf.

If the plant is adequately watered and temperature, humidity and wind are comparable, increased light can increase transpiration and water uptake.

Light does not supply a pushing force inside xylem. Its effect is commonly mediated by opening stomata for gas exchange. Other causes of closure, including water stress, can limit the effect. A lamp also heats leaves; a heat screen, distance adjustment and temperature measurement help separate the effects. Some desert succulents open their stomata mainly at night, so their water loss is greatest then.

### Counting stomata with impressions

A clear varnish impression can reproduce stomatal outlines from a leaf surface for counting under a light microscope.

Upper and lower epidermis must be compared over equal areas with the same magnification and representative repeated fields.

Use a thin layer of teacher-approved clear water-based varnish on a marked area of each side of a fresh leaf. Let it dry, lift the impression with transparent tape and mount it flat on a labelled slide. Focus on the impression rather than pressing a leaf into the microscope. Count stomata in several non-overlapping fields on each surface. Keep magnification, field area and the counting rule at field boundaries constant. Average the counts separately. Use several leaves of the same species and similar age, preferably paired surfaces of each leaf.

For example, equal-area fields with lower-surface counts 14, 16 and 15 give a mean of 15; upper-surface counts 2, 1 and 3 give a mean of 2. If each field has an area of 0.20 mm², the lower surface has 15 ÷ 0.20 = 75 stomata per mm² and the upper surface 2 ÷ 0.20 = 10 per mm². This sample supports more stomata below, but other plants differ: many floating leaves have stomata only on the upper surface. Distorted impressions or views crossing a large vein may bias the comparison.

### Using a potometer

A potometer estimates water uptake by measuring movement of a marker in a capillary connected to a leafy shoot in an airtight water-filled apparatus.

Water uptake is an indirect estimate of transpiration because some absorbed water is retained or used in the plant.

Fill the apparatus with water and attach a freshly cut shoot under water. Ensure joints are sealed and the reservoir can reset a small marker bubble. Dry the outside of the leaves. Let the shoot settle under each condition before starting a fixed timing period. Measure bubble displacement along the same capillary; a larger distance per minute means greater uptake if bore area is constant. For a displacement of 18 mm in 3 min, rate = 18 ÷ 3 = 6 mm/min. To calculate volume per time, multiply distance travelled per time by the known capillary cross-sectional area. For a capillary of radius 0.5 mm the area is 3.14 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.79 mm², so 6 mm/min corresponds to about 4.7 mm³/min.

Investigate temperature and humidity in separate series: vary one while holding light, wind and the other factor steady. Repeat each setting, reset the bubble and calculate means. Do not count movement caused by leaks as plant uptake. Never allow an air bubble to enter the stem, because it blocks the xylem and stops water uptake.

### Wilting

Wilting occurs when cells lose enough water that reduced turgor no longer supports soft plant tissues effectively.

Water loss can exceed uptake because of dry soil, rapid transpiration or impaired root and xylem function.

A flaccid cell presses less firmly against its wall than a turgid one. Many such cells together make a leaf or soft stem droop. Wilting can reduce the leaf area directly exposed to sun and air, but it is a result of water imbalance and not a deliberate muscular movement. Early wilting may be reversed if water supply recovers before permanent damage.

Check both sides of the balance: adding water will help dry soil but will not cure disease-damaged roots or blocked xylem. A plant in waterlogged soil may have little oxygen for root respiration and so take up water poorly despite the abundant water around it. A wilted plant therefore shows that too little water is reaching its cells, which can happen in wet soil as well as dry soil.

### Xerophytes and local examples

Xerophytes possess features that reduce water loss or help retain a water reserve in dry habitats.

Many local aloes have thick waxy leaf surfaces and fleshy leaves; the quiver tree stores water in its stem and branches.

Sunken stomata and a small exposed surface area can reduce water loss by keeping humid air near the pores and reducing the evaporating surface.

A thick waxy cuticle reduces evaporation through the outer surface, and a small exposed surface area reduces the area from which water is lost. Sunken or sheltered stomata keep humid air near the pores, reducing the vapour gradient. Different xerophytes use different combinations of these features.

In a local aloe, fleshy leaves hold a water reserve and a waxy surface helps conserve it. The quiver tree, a southern African succulent, stores water in its stem and branches. Storage helps the plant survive a period when uptake is restricted; it does not itself reduce transpiration.

To compare two plants, link each feature to its effect: for example, a waxy cuticle reduces evaporation from the surface, and sunken stomata trap humid air so the diffusion gradient is smaller. A spiny plant may have a small leaf area, but its stomatal depth can only be judged from a section of the leaf.

### Translocation

Translocation is the movement of sucrose and amino acids from the region of production or of storage to a region of utilisation or demand in respiration or growth.

Phloem transports these substances from sources to sinks, which can lie above or below the source.

A mature photosynthesising leaf can supply sucrose to growing roots, buds, flowers or fruits. A stored reserve can later supply new shoots. Source and sink describe current roles, not permanent labels for an organ: a developing storage organ may receive materials, while the same organ later releases stored products. Separate phloem pathways can therefore carry materials in different directions at the same time. Xylem supplies water and mineral ions; it is not the principal route for distributing sucrose.

### Systemic pesticide movement

An absorbed systemic pesticide can be transported internally to tissues beyond the place where it was applied.

For a pesticide transported in phloem, translocation distributes it with phloem contents towards receiving tissues.

A systemic pesticide is absorbed into the plant and then carried through it, so tissues far from where it was applied are also protected. A pesticide carried in the phloem travels with the sucrose and amino acids from the leaves towards growing shoots, roots and other sinks. Insects such as aphids, which feed on phloem sap, then take it in. A contact pesticide acts only where it touches the pest and is not carried through the plant.

Leaf anatomy and photosynthesis, which makes the sucrose moved in the phloem, are in 3.6.1 Photosynthesis and plant nutrition.

## 3.8.1 The heart and circulation

### Key points

| Structure or process | Essential role |
|---|---|
| Atria and ventricles | Receive blood and pump it out respectively |
| Valves and septum | Limit backflow and separate right from left circulation |
| Arteries | Strong elastic walls carry blood away from the heart |
| Veins | Low-pressure return, wide lumens and valves |
| Capillaries | Thin exchange walls near body cells |
| Double circulation | Heart–lungs–heart and heart–body–heart |
| Coronary disease | Restricted oxygen delivery to heart muscle; risk is not certainty |
| Lymphatics | Return excess tissue fluid and support defence |

### Heart chambers and valves

The mammalian heart has right and left atria receiving blood and right and left ventricles pumping blood out.

Valves keep blood flowing forwards, while the septum separates the right and left sides and the thicker left ventricle supplies the body circulation.

Follow the route rather than memorising a list of names. Blood from the body enters the right atrium through the vena cava, passes to the right ventricle and leaves through the pulmonary artery. Blood from the lungs enters the left atrium through the pulmonary veins, passes to the left ventricle and leaves through the aorta. Atrioventricular valves lie between atria and ventricles; valves at the arterial exits prevent blood returning to the ventricles. Valves open and close according to blood-pressure differences.

The left ventricular wall is thicker because it must generate the force to send blood round the whole body, while the right ventricle only sends blood to the nearby lungs. Both sides pump the same volume over time in a healthy closed circulation. The septum keeps oxygen-rich blood on the left separate from oxygen-poor blood on the right.

### The external heart

An external mammalian heart view can show the major vessels, the ventricular outline and coronary vessels running across the surface.

The anatomical right of a heart faces the viewer’s left in a standard front view, so orientation must be checked before labelling.

Locate the pointed lower region and the large vessels near the upper region. Distinguish the muscular wall from the coronary vessels on its surface. A drawing of the external surface should show what can be seen externally; do not add internal valve flaps as if the heart were transparent. Draw a clear outline at useful size, keep the major vessel positions accurate and rule label lines to the correct structures. Use the orientation of the specimen you are looking at rather than reversing labels from memory.

### Arteries

Arteries carry blood away from the heart and have thick muscular and elastic walls suited to relatively high, pulsatile pressure.

Elastic recoil helps maintain arterial blood movement between heartbeats; a relatively narrow lumen accompanies the strong wall.

The defining feature of an artery is the direction of flow, away from the heart, and not the oxygen content of its blood. The pulmonary artery carries blood with relatively little oxygen to the lungs. Elastic tissue stretches as blood is forced out and recoils afterwards, which keeps blood moving between beats. A thick wall withstands the pressure; smooth muscle can alter the vessel diameter.

Link each feature to a job: thick wall, so it does not burst under pressure; elastic tissue, so the pressure pulse is smoothed; smooth muscle, so the diameter and blood flow to an organ can be adjusted. The wall and the tissues in it count as separate features only when each is linked to a different job.

### Veins

Veins carry blood towards the heart at lower pressure than comparable arteries and usually have thinner walls and wider lumens.

Valves in many veins help prevent backflow, while contraction of nearby skeletal muscles assists venous return.

A wide lumen offers a low-resistance route for returning blood. Thick high-pressure walls are less necessary, so the muscular and elastic layers are thinner than in an artery of similar size. Valves are particularly useful in limbs, where blood may return against gravity. Veins are not defined as vessels carrying deoxygenated blood: the pulmonary veins return oxygen-rich blood from the lungs.

When leg muscles contract, they squeeze the veins and push blood towards the heart; the valves stop it from flowing back down again. This is why standing still for a long time can make blood collect in the legs, while walking helps blood return.

### Capillaries

Capillaries form extensive networks connecting small arteries to small veins near body cells.

A capillary wall is one cell thick and its narrow lumen brings blood close to the exchange surface.

Many branching capillaries provide a large total exchange area. Short distances between blood and cells favour diffusion, and the thin wall shortens that path further. Oxygen and dissolved nutrients can leave the blood for tissue fluid, while carbon dioxide and other products move towards the blood. A capillary is an exchange vessel, not a tiny artery with a thick muscular coat.

### Double circulation

In double circulation, blood passes through the heart twice during a complete journey through lungs and body.

The pulmonary circuit runs from heart to lungs and back; the systemic circuit runs from heart to body tissues and back.

The right ventricle supplies the pulmonary circuit and the left supplies the systemic circuit. At the lungs the blood gains oxygen and loses carbon dioxide; in most tissues the reverse net exchange occurs. Separation allows the body to receive oxygen-rich blood at a suitable pressure while the lung circuit operates at a lower pressure, which protects the delicate capillaries in the lungs. The circuits are joined in series, so the whole output of one side becomes the input of the other.

### Pulse and activity

A pulse is an arterial pressure wave associated with ventricular ejection and can estimate heart rate by counting beats over time.

Moderate exercise commonly increases pulse rate, helping deliver more oxygen and nutrients and remove respiratory products from active tissues.

In a supervised voluntary investigation, let a healthy participant rest before counting the wrist pulse for a full minute. Record beats/min. Use a defined comfortable activity such as stepping at a set pace for a short time; measure promptly afterwards and at fixed recovery times. Keep activity duration, timing and posture consistent. Repeat after full recovery rather than comparing a fresh first trial with a fatigued second trial. Participation must be optional, within school safety arrangements, and stopped if discomfort occurs.

A resting count of 72 beats/min and an immediate post-activity count of 108 beats/min show a rise of 36 beats/min. A reading taken two minutes later measures recovery, not the immediate response. Measurements from one student cannot give a population norm or diagnose disease.

### Diet and coronary disease

A diet high in saturated fat can increase the risk of fatty deposits narrowing coronary arteries.

A coronary artery blockage deprives part of the heart muscle of oxygen and may cause a heart attack.

Narrowing limits blood supply; a clot forming at a diseased area can reduce it sharply. The problem lies in the coronary vessels that supply the heart muscle, not in a blocked heart chamber. Diet affects risk over a long time, so one meal does not cause an attack. Risk also depends on other factors. A balanced diet can reduce risk, though it cannot guarantee prevention.

### Smoking and coronary disease

Smoking increases coronary heart disease risk through effects including blood-vessel damage and a greater tendency for clots to form.

Carbon monoxide in smoke reduces oxygen transport, while nicotine increases cardiovascular workload.

Smoking raises the chance of coronary disease but does not make it certain for any one person. Less oxygen carried in the blood and a reduced coronary supply act together. Nicotine can raise heart rate and cause vessels to constrict. These effects on the heart and blood vessels are different from tar damage in the airways, which is taught in 3.9.

### Stress and coronary risk

Persistent stress can contribute to coronary risk through sustained cardiovascular strain and associated behaviours.

Stress responses can raise heart rate and blood pressure, increasing the heart’s workload.

Stress is one influence among several. A short-lived stress response is normal and healthy; the risk comes from stress that is persistent, because the raised heart rate and blood pressure are then sustained. Persistent stress can also lead to behaviours that raise risk, such as smoking or eating a poor diet. Stress alone does not explain a heart attack.

### Age and coronary risk

Coronary heart disease becomes more common with increasing age, although it can occur in younger people.

Older people have generally had longer exposure to factors that damage arteries or favour fatty deposits.

Age is linked to risk but is not itself the blockage. Arterial changes accumulate over many years, so older people have on average had longer exposure to damaging factors. The extent of change differs greatly between people. Younger people can also develop coronary disease, particularly with other risk factors such as smoking or a diet high in saturated fat.

A population trend describes how risk changes for groups of people; it does not predict that every older person has narrowed coronary arteries.

### Sex-related coronary risk

Coronary disease risk differs between male and female populations, with males tending to develop it earlier on average.

Population differences in risk do not establish whether a particular individual will develop coronary disease.

This risk factor is often called gender in textbooks and examinations. The biological comparison is between male and female populations: on average, males tend to develop coronary disease earlier in life. Females can also develop coronary disease, and the difference becomes smaller with age.

### Aspirin

Aspirin can reduce platelet aggregation and hence reduce clot formation in coronary disease treatment.

Treatment choice depends on clinical assessment because aspirin also increases bleeding risk.

Aspirin is prescribed by doctors after an assessment, because it also increases the risk of bleeding. It makes platelets less likely to clump together, so clots are less likely to block a coronary artery. It does not remove fatty deposits that are already present and does not repair heart muscle that has been starved of oxygen.

### Stents

A coronary stent is a small mesh tube used to support a narrowed artery in an open position.

Keeping the artery open can improve blood delivery to the heart muscle.

A stent supports the artery wall from the inside. It keeps the narrowed vessel open so that blood can flow through it normally; it is not an alternative route around the narrowing. A stent treats the narrowed region but does not remove the causes of coronary disease, so diet, smoking and exercise still matter.

### Angioplasty

Coronary angioplasty uses an inflated balloon to widen a narrowed region of a coronary artery.

A stent may be placed during the procedure to help keep the artery open.

In angioplasty a thin tube with a small balloon is guided to the narrowed place. Inflating the balloon widens the existing route. A stent can be left in place afterwards to keep the vessel open. Angioplasty is different from by-pass surgery, which provides a new route.

### By-pass surgery

Coronary by-pass surgery creates an alternative blood route around a narrowed or blocked coronary region.

The alternative route improves delivery to the heart muscle beyond the obstruction.

In by-pass surgery a surgeon joins a piece of vessel, often taken from the leg or chest, to the coronary artery before and after the blockage. Blood then flows around the obstruction to reach the heart muscle. The operation does not by-pass the heart itself. Changes to diet, smoking and exercise are still needed afterwards to slow further narrowing.

### Lymphatic vessels and nodes

The lymphatic system includes lymphatic vessels that carry lymph and lymph nodes along their routes.

Lymph is fluid within lymphatic vessels; it originates as tissue fluid around cells.

Lymph nodes are small structures found at intervals along lymphatic vessels. They contain white blood cells and are not the same as endocrine glands, which secrete hormones. Lymphatic vessels eventually return collected fluid to the blood. The system has two roles, fluid circulation and defence, which the next two sections develop.

### Lymph nodes and defence

Lymph nodes filter lymph and bring carried foreign material into contact with immune cells.

Lymphocytes and phagocytic cells in lymphatic tissues contribute to defence against infection.

Fluid draining from tissues can carry pathogens or their components. As it passes through nodes, cells can recognise foreign material and start the appropriate response. Lymphocytes take part in specific antibody responses; phagocytes engulf material. Nodes are therefore active defence sites and not simply strainers that catch every pathogen.

### Returning tissue fluid

Some fluid surrounding body cells enters lymphatic vessels and becomes lymph.

The lymphatic system returns this fluid to the bloodstream, helping prevent its accumulation in tissues.

Tissue fluid bathes cells and allows exchange between them and capillary blood. Much returns directly to capillaries; the remaining fluid can drain into lymph vessels. Valves and body movement assist lymph flow towards its return to the blood. If drainage is impaired, fluid can build up and cause swelling. The circulation of body fluid is therefore connected with, but different from, immune recognition in nodes.

For example, a person who sits for many hours on a long flight may find their ankles swell, because without muscle movement tissue fluid is not drained and returned efficiently.

## 3.8.2 Blood

### Key points

| Blood component | Function or recognition |
|---|---|
| Red cells | Mature human cells lack nuclei; haemoglobin carries oxygen |
| White cells | Nucleated defence cells |
| Lymphocytes | Specific responses including antibody production |
| Phagocytes | Engulf and digest pathogens |
| Platelets | Help start clotting |
| Plasma | Liquid transport medium |
| Clot | Fibrinogen becomes fibrin; fibres trap cells |
| Tissue fluid | Exchange medium between capillary blood and cells |

### Recognising blood cells

Mature human red blood cells are biconcave discs without nuclei, while white blood cells have nuclei and are generally larger.

A lymphocyte typically has a large rounded nucleus occupying much of the cell; a phagocyte can have a lobed nucleus.

In a stained blood film, many small pale-centred discs are red cells. Look for a stained nucleus inside a larger cell before calling it a white cell. A pale central depression in a red cell is not a nucleus. Stain intensity and colour alone are unreliable identifiers. Use size, shape and nuclear appearance together.

The two main groups of white cell look different. A lymphocyte has a large rounded nucleus filling most of the cell, with little cytoplasm. A phagocyte has a lobed nucleus and more cytoplasm. A blood film from a healthy person contains far more red cells than white cells, so a field with no white cell is normal and does not mean the cells are absent from the body.

To identify a cell, ask in order: does it have a nucleus? If not, it is a red cell. If it does, is the nucleus rounded and large (lymphocyte) or lobed (phagocyte)?

### Red blood cell function

Red blood cells transport oxygen using haemoglobin.

The biconcave form provides a large surface area relative to volume and a short path for oxygen exchange.

Haemoglobin combines reversibly with oxygen, loading where oxygen is abundant and releasing it in tissues with lower oxygen concentration. The lack of a nucleus leaves more space for haemoglobin in mature human red cells. Oxygen transport is different from the release of energy in cellular respiration, which happens inside all living cells.

### White blood cell function

White blood cells defend the body against pathogens.

Different white cells contribute through mechanisms including phagocytosis and antibody production.

White blood cells form several groups, so it is too broad to say that every white cell produces antibodies. Lymphocytes and phagocytes show two different defence functions: lymphocytes produce antibodies, while phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens. The oxygen-carrying job belongs to red cells and is not a function of white cells.

### Platelet function

Platelets are cell fragments involved in starting blood-clot formation at a damaged vessel.

The resulting clot helps seal the damaged region and reduce blood loss.

Platelets are fragments of larger cells and are much smaller than red or white blood cells. They are not a third kind of white blood cell. At a damaged vessel they gather and help start clotting, which leads to the formation of a mesh of fibrin described later in this unit.

### Plasma transport

Plasma is the liquid component carrying blood cells and dissolved substances.

Plasma transports substances including glucose, amino acids, carbon dioxide, urea and hormones, and distributes heat.

When asked for an example of what plasma carries, choose a named cargo. Oxygen is carried mainly by haemoglobin inside red cells, whereas dissolved nutrients such as glucose and amino acids travel in the plasma, as do urea from the liver to the kidneys and hormones from glands to target organs. Plasma also spreads heat from active organs round the body. Plasma is not the same as tissue fluid, though the two fluids exchange materials at capillaries.

### Lymphocytes

Lymphocytes participate in specific immune responses, including antibody production.

A typical lymphocyte image shows a large rounded nucleus with a relatively small surrounding amount of cytoplasm.

Antibodies recognise particular antigens; this is different from engulfing a whole pathogen. Some lymphocytes form memory cells after active responses. In a stained blood film, a lymphocyte is identified by its large rounded nucleus and thin rim of cytoplasm. It can be told apart from a phagocyte, which has a lobed nucleus and more cytoplasm.

### Phagocytes

Phagocytes engulf pathogens and digest the engulfed material.

The cell membrane extends around the pathogen to enclose it before digestion occurs inside the phagocyte.

A lobed nucleus helps to identify a common phagocyte in a stained blood film. The phagocyte changes shape, extending its membrane around the pathogen until it is enclosed, and then digests it. Engulfing is different from antibody secretion, which is the job of lymphocytes, and red cells have no part in either process.

### Fibrin and clot formation

During clotting, soluble fibrinogen is converted to insoluble fibrin.

Fibrin forms fibres that make a mesh trapping blood cells and helping form a clot.

At a vessel injury, platelets gather and the clotting process leads to fibrin formation. Fibrinogen is the dissolved starting material, not the final fibre network. The fibres retain cells and platelets at the wound; the resulting plug limits further bleeding. This is different from antibody production in the immune response.

### Why blood clots

A clot reduces loss of blood from a damaged vessel.

Sealing a wound also reduces entry of pathogens through the damaged surface.

Clotting is useful at an injury, but a clot forming inside an intact coronary vessel can deprive heart muscle of oxygen. The same plugging action has different consequences at these different sites.

### Capillaries and tissue fluid

Tissue fluid surrounds body cells and provides an intermediate exchange medium between blood and cells.

Dissolved nutrients move from capillary blood into tissue fluid and then enter cells; metabolic products can move in the opposite direction.

Plasma supplies dissolved nutrients to the capillary exchange region. Cells take up nutrients from their immediate surroundings, not by direct contact with a circulating red cell. Oxygen also diffuses from blood towards respiring tissues, while carbon dioxide moves back towards the capillaries. Some tissue fluid returns to the blood and some drains through lymph vessels. Tissue fluid contains far fewer large blood components than whole blood, because blood cells and large proteins stay inside the capillaries.

### Where this connects

Use Topic 1 microscope technique before identifying prepared blood slides. The heart and vessels in 3.8.1 deliver blood to exchange sites. Diffusion in 3.3.1 underpins tissue exchange, and 3.8.3 develops immune defence.

## 3.8.3 Body defence

### Key points

| Defence | Essential distinction |
|---|---|
| Mechanical | Natural structures such as intact skin restrict entry |
| Chemical | Stomach acid can destroy swallowed pathogens |
| Phagocytosis | Engulfing and digestion by a white cell |
| Antibodies | Bind matching antigens |
| Active immunity | Antibodies produced in the body; memory can remain |
| Passive immunity | Ready-made antibodies from another individual; short-term, no transferred memory |
| Vaccination | Stimulates active protection before later exposure |
| Breast milk | Supplies maternal antibodies while infant responses develop |

### Pathogens

A pathogen is a disease causing organism.

Pathogens can enter a host and disrupt normal function; not every microorganism is pathogenic.

A pathogen is an organism that causes disease. The disease is the resulting disturbance of normal function, and the pathogen is the cause. A nutrient deficiency can also cause disease but is not a pathogen, because nothing is infecting the body. Many bacteria are harmless or useful, such as those in the gut that help with digestion.

Identify whether a question asks about a disease-causing agent, a symptom or a non-infectious condition before naming a pathogen. Examples of pathogens are the bacterium that causes cholera, the virus that causes influenza and the protozoan that causes malaria.

### Active immunity

Active immunity is defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body.

Active responses can produce memory cells that enable a faster response on later exposure to the same antigen.

Antibodies are proteins that recognise particular antigens, which are identifying molecules on foreign material. Active describes the person's own response; it has nothing to do with exercise or how severe the illness is. Infection or vaccination can stimulate this response. Antibody production takes time during a first encounter; an existing memory response is faster. Immunity can reduce the chance or severity of disease, but protection is not always complete or lifelong.

Compare a first and second infection: after the first exposure, antibodies take several days to reach a useful level, and the person may be ill meanwhile. After a second exposure to the same pathogen, memory cells start antibody production sooner and make more of it, so the person may not become ill at all.

### Mechanical barriers

Intact skin forms a physical barrier that restricts entry of pathogens into the body.

Nasal hairs trap some particles before they travel further into the air passages.

A barrier acts before a pathogen reaches internal tissues. Damage such as a cut interrupts skin protection; clotting and healing help close the entry route. Nasal hairs act by trapping particles, and they do not produce antibodies. The natural mechanical barriers of the body are different from manufactured barriers such as condoms, which reduce the spread of some infections but are not part of the body's defences.

Mechanical barriers stop pathogens physically. Chemical barriers, such as stomach acid, destroy them. When a question asks for a mechanical barrier, name a physical structure such as skin, not an acid or an enzyme.

### Chemical barriers

Acid in the stomach can destroy many pathogens swallowed with food.

Chemical defences can act before a specific antibody response develops.

Stomach acid provides a hostile chemical environment that destroys many pathogens swallowed with food or drink. It is not an antibody, and it protects only the stomach and the gut beyond it. A barrier lowers the number of living pathogens reaching the tissues but does not remove all risk. Stomach acid destroys pathogens chemically, whereas nasal hairs trap them physically.

### Antibody production

Antibodies recognise and bind particular antigens.

Binding can neutralise harmful material or help other defence mechanisms remove it.

Antibodies are specific. An antibody that binds one antigen does not usually fit a different one, so a response to one pathogen generally does not protect against another. Lymphocytes produce antibodies, unlike red cells, which transport oxygen. An antibody is a protein, not a cell, and it cannot engulf bacteria. The immune response involves cooperation between cells and soluble molecules: antibodies can also make pathogens easier for phagocytes to recognise and engulf.

### Phagocytosis

Phagocytosis involves a white blood cell surrounding and engulfing a pathogen.

The engulfed material is enclosed inside the cell and digested.

The phagocyte changes shape around the pathogen: its membrane flows round it until it is enclosed in a vesicle inside the cell. Digestion follows enclosure. This cellular defence differs from antibodies binding antigens outside cells, though antibodies can help phagocytes recognise targets. Red blood cells have no digestive role.

### Vaccination and spread

Vaccination exposes the immune system to suitable antigens without requiring the person to undergo the corresponding full disease.

An effective vaccine stimulates an active response and memory, so later exposure can be controlled more quickly and transmission may be reduced.

A vaccine can contain weakened or inactive material, or selected components, depending on its type. It is not the same as an injection of ready-made antibodies. With more people protected, a pathogen meets fewer hosts in which it can multiply and spread. How well a vaccine protects depends on the vaccine, the timing and the proportion of people vaccinated. Some vaccines need later doses to build lasting memory.

A vaccinated person can sometimes still be infected and pass the pathogen on, so health services also monitor cases of disease.

For example, imagine two similar villages. In one, 90 out of 100 people are vaccinated; in the other, 20 out of 100. When one infected person arrives, in the first village most contacts are likely to resist infection and the outbreak is likely to die out, while in the second the pathogen finds many susceptible people and spreads. A community can still contain susceptible people after a successful campaign, which is why high coverage matters.

### Passive immunity

Passive immunity is a short-term defence against a pathogen by antibodies acquired from another individual.

Ready-made antibodies can act promptly but do not by themselves establish the recipient’s immune memory.

The recipient receives an existing defensive product rather than first making it. Examples include maternal antibody transfer and medically supplied antibodies. Passive refers to the source of the antibodies, not to an inactive child or a weak response. How long the protection lasts depends on how long the supplied antibodies survive in the body, usually weeks or months.

Active and passive immunity can be compared by source and duration: in active immunity the person makes antibodies and memory cells, and protection can last for years; in passive immunity the antibodies come from someone else and protection is short.

### Establishing active immunity

On first exposure to an appropriate antigen, specific lymphocytes are activated and produce an antibody response.

Some responding lymphocytes become memory cells, allowing a faster and stronger response to a later encounter with the same antigen.

Follow the causal order: an antigen is recognised, matching cells respond, antibodies become available and memory cells remain. Antibodies bind their matching antigen; they do not become memory cells. On a second encounter the remembered antigen triggers rapid production, which can control the pathogen before substantial illness develops. The first response can come from natural infection or vaccination. A person need not experience the full disease to gain active immunity through a vaccine. The first response is slow, while the response of memory cells is much faster.

### Acquiring passive protection

Passive protection begins when antibodies made by another individual reach the recipient.

Those antibodies act against their matching targets without requiring an initial antibody-production response by the recipient.

Maternal transfer includes antibodies crossing the placenta before birth and antibodies supplied in breast milk after birth. Antibodies in milk act particularly on the surfaces of the infant's gut and airways; not all swallowed antibodies pass into the baby's blood unchanged. Medically supplied antibodies can also give rapid temporary protection, for example after exposure to a dangerous infection. In each of these examples the recipient did not make the transferred antibodies.

### Memory is not transferred

Passive immunity does not produce the recipient’s memory cells merely by supplying antibodies.

When supplied antibodies decline, that passive protection declines unless another source replaces them.

Supplying antibodies does not stimulate the recipient's lymphocytes, so no memory cells are made. The infant may develop its own active responses to other exposures, but these are separate from the antibodies received in milk. Protection from passive antibody transfer is therefore not the same as the memory response produced by vaccination.

This explains why a baby breast-fed for several months still needs its own vaccinations: the milk antibodies protect for a while but leave no memory.

### Breast milk and infant protection

Breast milk supplies maternal antibodies that can help protect a breast-fed infant against pathogens.

This passive protection is useful while the infant’s own immune responses are still developing.

Maternal antibody supply can reduce susceptibility, but feeding method alone does not decide whether an individual baby becomes ill. Hygiene, exposure, nutrition and other protective measures also matter. Breast milk does not guarantee absence of illness.

When a study compares groups of infants, first describe the data: which group has fewer infections and by how much. Then explain the possible mechanism: the antibodies in the milk act against pathogens in the gut. If the infants' own antibody production is similar in both groups, the difference is likely to come from the antibodies supplied by the mothers.

### Why passive protection fades

Supplied antibodies are broken down over time, so their protective concentration falls unless replenished.

Passive transfer alone does not create a lasting recipient memory response to replace them rapidly.

Immediate availability and duration are different properties. Ready-made antibodies can act sooner than a first active response but give shorter protection. Repeated supply can maintain antibody availability, but it does not turn the mechanism into active immunity. If the recipient independently meets the antigen and makes its own memory cells, that additional response is active and should be identified separately.

Think of a graph of antibody concentration against time. After a dose of ready-made antibodies the concentration is high at once and then falls steadily as the antibodies are broken down. After vaccination the concentration rises more slowly but is kept up, and rises again quickly on later exposure because of memory cells.

## 3.9 Gas exchange in humans

### Key points

| Idea | Essential distinction |
|---|---|
| Breathing | Air movement into and out of lungs |
| Respiration | Energy-releasing chemical reactions in cells |
| Exchange | Diffusion across a large, thin, ventilated surface with good blood supply |
| Inspiration | External intercostals raise ribs; diaphragm flattens |
| Expiration | Quiet recoil; internal intercostals help forced expiration |
| Expired air | Less oxygen, more carbon dioxide; generally warmer and moister |
| Activity | Usually increases breathing rate and depth |
| Carbon monoxide | Reduces haemoglobin oxygen carriage |
| Nicotine | Dependence and cardiovascular stimulation |
| Tar | Airway damage and carcinogens |

### Breathing and respiration

Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs.

Respiration consists of chemical reactions in cells that release energy from nutrient molecules.

Breathing ventilates the exchange surfaces, while respiration creates the cellular demand for oxygen during aerobic activity. Gas exchange is a third related process: oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse between the alveoli and the blood. A muscle cell can respire without directly moving air. The lungs do not make energy by breathing. The aerobic and anaerobic pathways are developed in 3.10.1 and 3.10.2; ventilation is a physical movement of air and not a chemical reaction.

The three terms fit together in order: breathing brings oxygen to the alveoli, gas exchange moves it into the blood, and respiration uses it in the cells to release energy. In an exam answer, use breathing only for the movement of air.

### An effective exchange surface

Alveoli provide a large total surface area and thin exchange surfaces for diffusion between air and blood.

A good blood supply and ventilation maintain oxygen and carbon dioxide concentration gradients across the exchange surface.

Numerous alveoli distribute a large area within the lungs. Thin alveolar and capillary walls give a short diffusion path. Ventilation replaces used alveolar air with fresh air, and blood flow brings blood needing oxygen while removing blood that has gained it. Both processes help preserve gradients: a large surface cannot exchange rapidly if no useful concentration difference remains.

Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air towards the blood when its concentration is higher in the air; carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction when its concentration is higher in the arriving blood. The gases move by diffusion alone and are not pumped through the alveolar wall. A moist lining lets the gases dissolve before they diffuse across.

Adaptation questions are answered best as feature plus effect: for example, walls one cell thick give a short diffusion distance, and a rich capillary network keeps removing oxygen-rich blood so that the gradient is maintained.

### Intercostal muscles and ventilation

External intercostal contraction raises the ribs during inspiration; the diaphragm contracts and flattens, increasing thoracic volume.

Quiet expiration largely follows relaxation and elastic recoil; internal intercostals help pull ribs down during forced expiration.

When thoracic volume increases, the pressure inside falls below the outside pressure and air moves into the lungs. Relaxation allows the chest to fall back, reducing the volume and moving air out. Forced expiration adds active muscle contraction, with the internal intercostal muscles pulling the ribs down; they do not need to contract strongly during every quiet breath. The diaphragm is a separate muscular sheet below the lungs, not one of the ribs.

Inspiration in order: external intercostals contract and the ribs move up and out, the diaphragm contracts and flattens, the volume of the chest increases, the pressure falls and air flows in. Expiration reverses these changes.

### Keeping the trachea open

Cartilage supports the tracheal wall and helps prevent the airway collapsing during breathing.

Tracheal cartilage forms incomplete rings that allow flexibility while maintaining an open air passage.

During inspiration the pressure within the airways falls. A soft unsupported tube could narrow; cartilage resists this collapse. Cartilage is structural support, not the tissue that makes mucus or exchanges oxygen. The rings are C-shaped, and the gap at the back lets food pass down the oesophagus behind the trachea. Gas exchange happens mainly at the alveoli and not through the thick tracheal wall.

### Inspired and expired air

Expired air contains less oxygen and more carbon dioxide than inspired air because of gas exchange with respiring tissues via the blood.

Expired air is generally warmer and contains more water vapour after passing over moist respiratory surfaces.

Typical classroom values are about 21% oxygen in inspired air and about 16% in mixed expired air; carbon dioxide rises from roughly 0.04% to around 4%. These values are approximate and vary with activity and sampling. Nitrogen changes little because it is not used in normal cellular respiration. Expired air still contains oxygen: the body does not remove it all in one breath, which is why mouth-to-mouth resuscitation can work.

Air breathed out first from the airways has not exchanged gases at the alveoli, so a mixed breath differs from a pure alveolar sample. When interpreting two samples of air, first identify the oxygen and carbon dioxide pattern, then check that the samples were collected in the same way. Temperature alone cannot identify the air if the collection conditions differ.

Worked example: sample X has 20.9% oxygen and 0.04% carbon dioxide; sample Y has 16.0% oxygen and 4.0% carbon dioxide. Y has 4.9 percentage points less oxygen and 3.96 points more carbon dioxide, so Y is expired air.

### Testing air for carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide turns limewater cloudy; the response can compare samples when equal air volumes pass through equal amounts of indicator.

Expired air normally produces a stronger or faster carbon-dioxide response than inspired air under matched testing conditions.

For a controlled written investigation, collect a known volume of room air and the same volume of expired air separately using clean suitable equipment under teacher supervision. Pass each sample through equal fresh limewater portions at the same delivery rate and temperature. Record the time to a predefined cloudiness or compare cloudiness after the same volume. Repeat with fresh indicator. Wear eye protection and prevent mouth contact or sucking liquid back through a tube; use separate collection and delivery equipment.

Hydrogencarbonate indicator offers an alternative: more carbon dioxide shifts the usual red or orange appearance towards yellow. Controls and starting colour must be matched. Cloudiness in limewater shows the presence of carbon dioxide; it does not show oxygen consumption. Unequal air volumes cannot give a fair comparison of concentration, because a larger volume would make the response stronger even at the same concentration.

### Breathing during exercise

Physical activity raises muscle energy demand, commonly increasing oxygen use and carbon dioxide production.

Breathing usually becomes faster and deeper, increasing air movement to maintain exchange during greater demand.

Rate is breaths per minute; depth is the volume per breath. These can change independently, so rate alone is not a complete measure of ventilation. For illustration, 12 breaths/min at 0.5 dm³ per breath gives 6 dm³/min, whereas 20 breaths/min at 1.0 dm³ gives 20 dm³/min. The increase reflects both factors, not rate alone.

After activity stops, ventilation may stay above the resting level for a time as the body recovers. Deeper breathing does not mean that the alveoli become muscular pumps; the diaphragm and intercostal muscles do the work.

On a trace of breath volume against time, the number of peaks in one minute is the breathing rate, and the height of each peak shows the volume per breath. During exercise there are more peaks and they are taller, so more air is moved each minute.

### Investigating exercise and breathing

Breathing rate can be measured by counting complete breaths over a fixed time; depth requires a volume-sensitive measure or a clearly identified estimate.

An exercise comparison must standardise activity and post-exercise timing, with recovery before repeated trials.

A safe school study can measure a rested volunteer before and after comfortable standardised activity. Count complete inspiration-expiration cycles for a defined interval. Use a suitable calibrated volume recorder under trained supervision to measure depth; clean equipment and individual mouthpieces are needed where applicable. Chest movement alone is an estimate of breathing depth, not a direct volume reading.

Keep duration, pace, posture and the interval before measurement constant. A delayed count underestimates the immediate response because recovery has already begun. Obtain consent, exclude participation where school health guidance advises it and stop if discomfort occurs. Repeat after full recovery. Comparisons between different people need care because fitness, age and body size also affect the response.

Worked example: at rest 15 breaths/min at 0.40 dm³ per breath gives 6.0 dm³/min. After activity, 25 breaths/min at 0.80 dm³ gives 20 dm³/min, an increase of 14 dm³/min.

### Carbon monoxide

Carbon monoxide combines strongly with haemoglobin, reducing its availability to carry oxygen.

Reduced oxygen delivery can limit aerobic energy release in tissues and add strain to the cardiovascular system.

Carbon monoxide acts in the blood: it binds to haemoglobin much more strongly than oxygen does, so less oxygen can be carried. It does not coat the airways; that is the effect of tar. A person can move air normally while the blood carries too little oxygen, because ventilation and oxygen transport are different stages.

The result is that tissues, including the heart and the brain, receive less oxygen. In a pregnant smoker the baby also receives blood with less oxygen, which is one reason why smoking in pregnancy is harmful.

### Nicotine

Nicotine is an addictive component of tobacco smoke.

Nicotine can increase heart rate and constrict blood vessels, raising cardiovascular workload.

Nicotine causes dependence, which makes stopping tobacco use difficult. Its other effects are on the circulation: it increases heart rate and constricts blood vessels, so the heart works against greater resistance. Its main link to the gas exchange system is addiction: dependence keeps the smoker breathing in the tar and carbon monoxide that damage the airways and reduce oxygen carriage.

### Tar and airway damage

Tar in tobacco smoke contains cancer-causing substances and damages respiratory surfaces.

Tar-related airway injury impairs normal cleaning and contributes to mucus accumulation and chronic respiratory disease.

Tar contains carcinogens, substances that increase the risk of cancer in the lungs and airways. It also damages the cilia that normally sweep mucus and trapped pathogens out of the airways. Without working cilia, mucus builds up, irritants and pathogens remain, and the smoker develops a persistent cough and frequent chest infections.

In chronic disease, damaged alveolar walls reduce the exchange area and the loss of elastic recoil makes expiration harder. These effects on the structure of the airways and alveoli are different from carbon monoxide reducing oxygen carriage in the blood. Different smoke components harm the same person by separate mechanisms.

### Smoking and COPD

Tobacco smoking can cause chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, abbreviated COPD.

COPD involves persistent limitation of airflow, including changes associated with chronic bronchitis and emphysema.

Chronic bronchitis involves long-lasting airway inflammation and mucus problems. Emphysema damages the alveolar walls and elastic recoil, reducing the useful exchange area and making air harder to expel. COPD is not the same disease as lung cancer, though smoking increases the risk of both. Some people develop COPD from other exposures such as long-term dust or fumes, so smoking is a major risk factor but not the only cause.

A person with COPD becomes breathless on little effort, because less oxygen can cross the damaged exchange surface and the airways are narrowed. The damage cannot be reversed, although stopping smoking slows further decline.

### Where this connects

Use 3.3.1 for diffusion gradients. Blood transport in 3.8.1 and 3.8.2 connects alveolar exchange with body cells. Respiration in 3.10.1 and 3.10.2 explains cellular energy demand; Topic 1 supports safe investigation, rates and graph interpretation.

## 3.10.1 Aerobic respiration

### Key points

| Idea | Essential knowledge |
|---|---|
| Aerobic respiration | nutrient breakdown in cells using oxygen releases energy |
| Products | carbon dioxide and water |
| Energy uses | movement, transport, synthesis and heat |
| Investigation | correct respirometer movement with a non-living control; compare rates at matched temperatures |

### Aerobic respiration

Aerobic respiration is the chemical reactions in cells that use oxygen to break down nutrient molecules to release energy.

Respiration transfers energy from nutrients for cell activities; breathing moves air into and out of lungs.

A seed, a leaf and a muscle all contain living cells that respire. Plants respire in darkness as well as in light. Respiration releases energy already stored chemically in nutrients; it does not create energy. Oxygen reaches human tissues through gas exchange and circulation, but the release of energy happens within cells.

### The word equation

The word equation is glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.

Energy is released during aerobic respiration; it is not a material product to balance as atoms.

Read the arrow as “react to form”. Glucose is the nutrient that is respired and oxygen is the second reactant. Carbon dioxide leaves the body through the lungs; water can be used in cells or excreted. Overall this is the reverse of the photosynthesis equation, and plants carry out both processes: photosynthesis only in the light, respiration all the time.

### The balanced equation

The balanced equation is C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O.

The equation has six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and eighteen oxygen atoms on each side.

Coefficients multiply whole molecules. Check the balance atom by atom. Carbon: 6 in glucose and 6 × 1 in carbon dioxide. Hydrogen: 12 in glucose and 6 × 2 in water. Oxygen: 6 in glucose plus 6 × 2 = 12 from the oxygen molecules gives 18, and 6 × 2 in carbon dioxide plus 6 × 1 in water also gives 18. To balance an equation change only the large numbers in front of formulae; the small numbers inside a formula are fixed by what the substance is.

### Uses of released energy

Muscle contraction, active transport and synthesis of large molecules require energy released by respiration.

Respiration also contributes heat needed to maintain human body temperature.

Active transport moves particles against a concentration gradient. Building proteins supports growth and repair. Energy use continues during rest: the heart contracts, breathing muscles work and cells maintain their contents. Increased muscular activity increases energy demand, so respiration and delivery of oxygen must increase.

### Measuring seed oxygen uptake

A respirometer can measure oxygen uptake when carbon dioxide produced by seeds is absorbed separately.

A volume-matched non-living control helps distinguish respiration from changes caused by temperature or pressure.

Use a sealed respirometer with germinating seeds and a narrow graduated tube containing a marker droplet. A carbon dioxide absorbent, separated from the seeds by a barrier, removes released carbon dioxide. As oxygen is consumed, gas volume falls and the droplet moves towards the seed chamber. A teacher handles the corrosive absorbent and checks airtight connections. Use a water bath, allow equilibration before closing the apparatus, then read movement over a measured time. A live-seed chamber and a control containing an equal volume of glass beads or boiled seeds should be compared at each temperature. Record direction and distance, not just whether a bubble moves. Repeat with fresh equivalent samples; divide corrected volume change by time and seed mass if rates per gram are needed.

### Temperature and respiration rate

Respiration rate can be compared at different temperatures using equal masses of equivalent germinating seeds.

A rise in temperature can increase respiration until high temperatures impair the enzymes or damage cells.

For a temperature series, use separate comparable samples at 15, 25 and 35 °C rather than repeatedly heating one sample for different times. Read the bath thermometer rather than assuming its setting is the seed temperature. Allow each apparatus to equilibrate. An illustrative corrected oxygen uptake is 0.12 cm³ in 4 min: rate = 0.12 ÷ 4 = 0.03 cm³/min. A faster rate at 25 °C than at 15 °C supports a temperature effect over that interval. Rates do not keep rising: above the optimum temperature enzymes are damaged and the rate falls. Take readings before the oxygen in the sealed chamber becomes limiting. The same species, seed mass, hydration and germination stage reduce alternative explanations.

## 3.10.2 Anaerobic respiration

### Key points

| Process | Products and use |
|---|---|
| Muscle anaerobic respiration | lactic acid; small energy release without oxygen |
| Yeast fermentation | ethanol and carbon dioxide |
| Brewing | ethanol retained |
| Bread | carbon dioxide bubbles raise dough |
| Recovery | blood carries lactic acid for processing; oxygen supports recovery |

### Anaerobic respiration

Anaerobic respiration is the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules to release a relatively small amount of energy, in the absence of oxygen.

The products depend on the organism: human muscles form lactic acid, whereas yeast forms ethanol and carbon dioxide.

The term describes the chemistry inside cells, not breathing. A muscle may use anaerobic energy release when its oxygen supply cannot meet its demand, even while the person is breathing hard. Less usable energy is transferred per glucose molecule than during aerobic respiration because glucose is only partly broken down.

### Anaerobic energy release in muscles

The muscle word equation is glucose → lactic acid.

This process releases some energy without consuming oxygen in the reaction.

Muscle anaerobic respiration forms lactic acid only; no carbon dioxide is made in this pathway. During strenuous exercise muscles can use this pathway alongside aerobic respiration. The product differs from that of yeast, so name the organism or tissue when giving an anaerobic equation.

### Anaerobic energy release in yeast

The yeast equation is C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂.

Yeast converts glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide during alcoholic fermentation.

Count six carbon atoms on each side: four in the two ethanol molecules and two in carbon dioxide. The hydrogen and oxygen atoms balance too. Oxygen is not a reactant in this equation. The carbon dioxide can inflate dough, while ethanol is the relevant retained product in brewing.

### During strenuous activity

When muscular energy demand outpaces oxygen delivery, anaerobic breakdown of glucose increases.

Lactic acid accumulates when its production exceeds its removal.

This gives muscles an additional rapid source of energy for a limited period. Lactic acid is carried away in blood, and its concentration reflects both production and removal. Lactic acid build-up is one factor in muscle fatigue, alongside others, and soreness a day or two later is a separate effect. The build-up explains why breathing and heart rate stay high after intense activity and why recovery is needed.

### Recovery after intense exercise

Lactic acid is transported from muscles in the blood, including to the liver.

During recovery, lactic acid is processed: some can be oxidised and some converted back into glucose.

Breathing and circulation remain elevated after strenuous exercise to support the oxygen and energy demands of recovery.

The traditional term oxygen debt refers to the additional oxygen needed after exercise. Blood first removes lactic acid from muscle; the liver then helps process it. Conversion back to glucose requires energy supplied by aerobic respiration. Lactic acid is not breathed out: it is carried away in blood and processed in the liver. Recovery also restores other conditions in muscle, so extra oxygen use is a rough guide to the amount of lactic acid processed, not an exact measure.

### Brewing

Yeast uses sugars anaerobically to produce ethanol and carbon dioxide.

Brewing retains ethanol in the liquid, with temperature controlled to keep yeast active.

A sugar-containing liquid is inoculated with yeast and kept under conditions that limit oxygen after growth has been established. Gas must be able to escape safely rather than building pressure in a sealed vessel. If yeast becomes too hot its enzymes are damaged; if too cold, production slows. Fermentation stops when the sugar is used up or when the ethanol concentration becomes high enough to damage the yeast cells.

### Bread-making

Carbon dioxide released by yeast becomes trapped in dough and makes it rise.

Baking expands trapped gas, sets the dough structure and kills the yeast.

The soft elastic dough holds bubbles. Ethanol is also produced while yeast ferments sugar, but much of it evaporates during baking; it is not what inflates the loaf. Warm conditions speed activity within a suitable range, whereas very hot water damages the yeast enzymes before the dough rises.

### Relative energy yield

Aerobic respiration releases more usable energy per glucose molecule than anaerobic respiration.

Anaerobic products retain chemical energy because breakdown is incomplete.

Compare the same amount of glucose, not different quantities of food or different times. An organism that obtains much less energy per glucose must use glucose faster to sustain the same energy demand. It may still fail to meet the demand if glucose delivery or the rate of the pathway is limiting.

### Testing yeast gas production

Carbon dioxide from anaerobic yeast respiration can be collected and identified using limewater.

A boiled-yeast control helps test whether gas production requires living yeast.

For a school investigation, cool boiled glucose solution to remove much dissolved oxygen, add yeast, and cover the liquid with a thin oil layer. Fit a delivery tube to a gas collector without sealing the gas outlet. Keep a modest constant temperature in a water bath. Count gas volume over a fixed time rather than relying on irregular bubble sizes. Test a sample of gas with limewater; cloudiness supports identification as carbon dioxide. Compare active yeast with boiled yeast under matching conditions. Because a little oxygen may remain, describe the conditions as oxygen-limited; the preparation is designed to favour anaerobic respiration.

## 3.11 Excretion

### Key points

| Organ or process | Role |
|---|---|
| Liver | deamination, urea formation and breakdown of substances |
| Kidney | urea/water removal and useful-solute reabsorption |
| Ureter, bladder, urethra | transport to storage, then exit |
| Dialysis | exchange of small solutes across a membrane |
| Transplant | possible continuous function, with donor, surgical and rejection constraints |

### Deamination

Deamination is the removal of the nitrogen-containing part of amino acids to form urea.

Urea is formed in the liver and carried in the blood to the kidneys for excretion.

Protein eaten beyond the body’s needs is digested to amino acids, and the body cannot store the excess. In the liver the nitrogen-containing part of each excess amino acid is removed; the rest of the molecule can be respired for energy or converted to carbohydrate or fat. Urea is a waste product of metabolism, so its removal is excretion. Egestion, by contrast, removes undigested food that was never absorbed.

### Liver formation of urea

The liver removes nitrogen-containing groups from excess amino acids.

Their nitrogen is incorporated into urea, which is less toxic than ammonia and can be transported for excretion.

Protein digestion first supplies amino acids, which cells use to make their own proteins. Deamination of the excess releases the nitrogen-containing part as ammonia, which is toxic, and the liver converts it to urea. The kidneys then filter urea out of the blood. Urea is a waste product, not a digestive enzyme, and it is not made from bile.

### Breaking down substances in the liver

The liver chemically changes alcohol, many drugs and hormones.

Hormone breakdown helps prevent a signal continuing indefinitely after secretion changes.

Breakdown often converts a substance into forms that can be processed further or excreted. Some products are still harmful, and excessive alcohol can damage liver cells. Hormones such as adrenaline and insulin are broken down in the liver after they have acted, so their effects wear off. The effect of an individual medicine depends on its own properties.

### The urinary route

Ureters carry urine from the kidneys to the bladder.

The bladder stores urine before it passes out through the urethra.

Urine is made in the two kidneys. It flows down a ureter from each kidney to the bladder, where it is stored. When the bladder is emptied, urine leaves the body through the urethra. There are therefore two ureters but only one bladder and one urethra. On a diagram, identify the tubes by where they start and end, not by their names or shapes: a ureter links a kidney to the bladder; the urethra starts at the bladder and opens outside the body. Only urine, not blood, flows along these tubes.

### Kidney functions

Kidneys remove urea and excess water from blood into urine.

Glucose and some salts are reabsorbed into the blood rather than lost with the urine.

Excretion removes the waste products of metabolism. Glucose remains a useful nutrient; retaining it avoids wasting an energy source. Salt and water retention help keep the internal environment within a suitable range. Urine normally contains urea, water and salts, but glucose is normally recovered. Glucose in urine can mean blood glucose was very high, as in untreated diabetes (3.14).

### Dialysis

Dialysis allows small dissolved substances to diffuse across a partially permeable membrane between blood and dialysis fluid.

Blood cells and large proteins remain in the blood, while urea diffuses into fluid with little or no urea.

Suitable glucose and salt concentrations in dialysis fluid limit unwanted net loss of useful substances.

Blood passes along one side of a thin membrane while dialysis fluid flows on the other. Blood and dialysis fluid never mix; only small molecules cross. Fresh dialysis fluid keeps a urea concentration gradient, so urea keeps diffusing out. Because glucose concentrations are matched, there is little net diffusion of glucose, although individual molecules can move both ways. Excess water can also be removed under controlled conditions. A kidney machine supports patients with severe loss of kidney function but needs repeated sessions; it does not repair the kidneys.

### Transplantation and dialysis

A functioning transplanted kidney can provide continuous waste removal and freedom from repeated dialysis sessions.

Transplantation involves surgery, donor matching and possible rejection; medicines that suppress immunity can increase infection risk.

Dialysis is available without a donor organ and avoids transplant surgery, but repeated treatment takes time and can restrict daily routines and diet. A transplant may offer long-term benefit but is not guaranteed to last permanently. Which treatment suits a patient depends on their health, on donor availability and on access to treatment.

### A model membrane separation

Visking tubing can retain starch while allowing smaller glucose molecules or ions to pass.

Testing samples inside and outside the tubing distinguishes movement of a solute from movement of water.

Soak tubing, tie one end, add glucose and starch solution, then close it and rinse the outside carefully. Suspend it in water. Test outside samples at the start and after a fixed interval using iodine for starch and Benedict’s reagent in a hot-water bath for reducing sugar. A positive sugar test outside with a negative starch test supports selective passage. For sodium chloride and starch, use a fresh bag containing that mixture. After the set interval, test one outside sample with iodine and another with silver nitrate solution provided by the teacher: a white precipitate shows that chloride ions from the salt have passed out, while a negative iodine test shows the starch stayed inside. Use eye protection and never taste samples. Testing the outside water at the start as well shows that any positive result later is due to passage through the tubing, not spillage. Visking tubing behaves like the membrane in a kidney machine: small molecules and ions pass through it, large molecules such as starch and proteins do not.

## 3.12 Plant growth responses

### Key points

| Response | Direction and mechanism |
|---|---|
| Phototropism | growth relative to light |
| Gravitropism | growth relative to gravity |
| Auxin | regulates cell elongation; unequal growth bends an organ |
| Horizontal shoot | lower side grows more; bends up |
| Horizontal root | lower side grows less; bends down |
| Investigation | separate the cues and repeat with matched seedlings |

### Plant growth substances

Plant growth substances are chemicals that affect the activities of particular cells and organs.

Auxins are plant growth substances involved in coordinating growth responses.

A plant has no nervous system, but chemicals can carry information and alter how cells grow. The same chemical need not have the same effect in every organ: response depends on concentration and tissue sensitivity. This helps explain why roots and shoots bend differently after being placed horizontally. Growth substances are made in one region, such as a shoot tip, and move to other cells where they act, and they are effective in very small amounts. In this way a plant coordinates its growth with the direction of light and of gravity.

### Gravitropism

Gravitropism is a response in which parts of a plant grow towards or away from gravity.

Roots commonly show positive gravitropism, while shoots commonly show negative gravitropism.

Positive means growth towards the stimulus, not a beneficial response. A horizontally placed seedling can develop a downward-curving root and an upward-curving shoot. The growing region bends over time because opposite sides elongate at different rates. Gravitropism helps a germinating seed send its root down into the soil and its shoot up towards the light. It is a directional growth response, not a rapid movement by muscle.

### Phototropism

Phototropism is a response in which parts of a plant grow towards or away from the direction from which light is coming.

Shoots commonly show positive phototropism by bending towards one-sided light.

Growth towards light can position leaves where light is available for photosynthesis. Uniform illumination does not give a directional cue, whereas light from one side does. In darkness shoots grow long and pale without bending in any set direction.

### Auxin and growth

Auxin made near a shoot tip can alter elongation of cells behind the tip.

Unequal auxin distribution can produce unequal elongation and bend a growing organ.

If two sides of a shoot elongate equally, the organ becomes longer without curving. If one side becomes longer, it forms the outside of a bend and the tip turns towards the shorter side. Classic evidence comes from shoot tips. If the tip is removed, the shoot no longer bends towards one-sided light. If the tip is covered with an opaque cap, bending stops too, but if only the region below the tip is covered, bending still happens. This shows that the tip detects the light and sends a chemical signal, auxin, to the region behind it where cells elongate. Auxin is a chemical regulator, not food or a source of energy; cells still need nutrients, water and respiration to grow.

### Synthetic growth substances as weedkillers

Some synthetic plant growth substances disrupt growth when supplied in high concentrations.

Selective herbicides can affect broad-leaved weeds more than grasses at the intended dose.

Excess stimulation disrupts coordinated growth and can kill susceptible plants. Weedkillers of this kind are used on lawns and cereal crops: at the recommended dose they kill broad-leaved weeds such as dandelions, while grasses and cereals are largely unaffected. Selectivity is a difference in sensitivity between the plants, so it depends on the dose, the plant species and the conditions of application.

### Two directional responses

Gravitropism responds to the direction of gravity.

Phototropism responds to the direction of light.

Both are growth responses and can occur together. A root bending downward in a dark container demonstrates a gravity response without directional light. A shoot bending towards a side window may respond to light even though gravity is also present. Two examples show how to separate them. A seedling lying on its side in a dark box can respond only to gravity, so its root curves down and its shoot curves up. An upright seedling beside a window is not lying down, so a bend towards the glass is a light response. An experiment must change one cue at a time, otherwise the observed bend cannot be linked to its cause.

### Differential growth and bending

In a shoot lit from one side, more auxin collects on the shaded side and promotes greater elongation there, bending the shoot towards light.

In a horizontal shoot, greater auxin on the lower side promotes greater elongation below, bending the shoot upwards.

In a horizontal root, the higher auxin concentration on the lower side inhibits elongation there, because roots are more sensitive to auxin than shoots, so the root bends downwards.

Follow the sequence: stimulus direction changes the distribution of auxin; opposite sides elongate by different amounts; the tip bends. The longer side is on the outside of the curve. Worked example: if the shaded side of a shoot elongates 4 mm while the lit side elongates 1 mm, the shaded side is 3 mm longer and the shoot curves towards the light. A build-up of auxin that speeds up growth in a shoot therefore slows it in a root, and the same stimulus bends the two organs in opposite directions. A horizontally placed seedling in darkness still bends in response to gravity. If a seedling is rotated slowly so that no side stays lower, the effect of gravity is averaged out and little curvature develops. A rotating seedling is still under gravity; it is simply not exposed to it from one side only.

### Observing seedlings under different light conditions

Seedlings under uniform light can grow without a consistent sideways phototropic bend.

Seedlings under one-sided light commonly curve towards it, whereas dark-grown seedlings may be elongated and pale.

Grow comparable seedlings for the same time with water, temperature and starting size matched. Photograph or draw each against a reference line at the start and end; record height, curvature direction and colour separately. Darkness can affect both colour and elongation, so a longer seedling is not necessarily a healthier or better photosynthesising one. Use several seedlings in each group because individuals differ. Interpretation should separate “light direction changed curvature” from “light availability changed colour or growth”. If a window also changes temperature, the experiment has an additional variable.

### Investigating directional growth

Directional growth experiments must separate light direction from gravity direction.

Replicated matched seedlings and recorded initial orientation make curvature evidence interpretable.

For phototropism, stand matched seedlings upright in boxes admitting equal light either from one side or uniformly. Keep temperature, water and duration equal; record shoot curvature from starting positions. For gravitropism, place germinated seeds horizontally on moist support in darkness, with roots and shoots free to grow. Compare with seedlings maintained in their normal upright orientation. A slowly rotating clinostat can be a further control if available. Record both root and shoot direction; do not infer one from the other. Keep support moist rather than submerged so oxygen remains available. Repeats test whether the pattern is consistent rather than the chance shape of one seedling.

## 3.13.1 The nervous system and the eye

### Key points

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| Receptor | detects a stimulus |
| Sensory, relay, motor neurones | bring information in, connect centrally, carry response out |
| Synapse | chemical transmission at a junction |
| Effector | muscle contraction or gland secretion |
| Iris | light admission through pupil size |
| Lens | adjustable focusing on retina |
| Retina and optic nerve | detection, then signal transport |
| Near focus | ciliary contraction, slack ligaments, thicker lens |
| Distant focus | ciliary relaxation, tense ligaments, thinner lens |

### Neurones

Sensory neurones carry impulses from receptors towards the central nervous system; motor neurones carry them from the central nervous system to effectors.

Relay neurones connect neurones within the central nervous system.

A sensory neurone has its cell body on a short side branch part-way along one long fibre; the fibre brings impulses from a receptor to the spinal cord. A motor neurone has its cell body at one end, with many short branches and one long axon that carries impulses out to a muscle or gland. A relay neurone is a short neurone inside the central nervous system that links sensory and motor neurones. The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord. Textbook diagrams are simplified, so identify a neurone by where its cell body lies, how it branches and where its fibre leads, rather than by overall outline alone.

### Effectors

Effectors carry out responses: muscles contract and glands secrete substances.

An effector receives signals after information about a stimulus has been processed.

Receptors detect stimuli, such as pressure on skin; they do not pull the hand away. The muscle is the effector in that response. A gland releasing a secretion is also an effector, even though its response is not a visible movement. Detection, coordination and response are three separate functions: receptor, coordinator, effector.

### Synapses

A synapse is a junction between two neurones or between a neurone and an effector.

At a chemical synapse a signal crosses a small gap using a neurotransmitter.

The cells do not join into one continuous cell. Transmission along a neurone is an electrical impulse, whereas crossing the junction requires a chemical signal. The junction allows a signal to pass to an appropriate next cell.

### Transmission across a synapse

An impulse arriving at a neurone ending triggers release of neurotransmitter into the synaptic gap.

Neurotransmitter diffuses across and binds to receptors on the next cell, triggering its response when sufficient stimulation occurs.

Removal or breakdown of neurotransmitter helps stop continuing stimulation after release ceases.

The order is arrival, release, diffusion and receptor binding, followed by the next electrical impulse or an effector response. The impulse does not jump across the gap: a chemical carries the signal. Neurotransmitter is released from the sending side and the receptors are on the receiving side, so a chemical synapse normally passes signals in one direction only. The transmitter is then broken down or taken back up, which stops the receiving cell being stimulated continuously. A chemical that blocks release leaves the impulse arriving normally but stops the signal reaching the next cell. A chemical that blocks the receptors also stops the signal, but acts after the transmitter has been released.

### Alcohol and synaptic signalling

Alcohol is a depressant that reduces the release of neurotransmitter at synapses, which slows synaptic transmission in the nervous system.

Slower transmission delays impulses along coordinating pathways, which impairs coordination and lengthens reaction time.

A synapse is a slow step in a pathway, because a chemical has to diffuse across the gap. Alcohol reduces the amount of neurotransmitter released when an impulse arrives, so less transmitter crosses the gap and the next neurone is stimulated more slowly, or not at all. When alcohol slows transmission at many synapses, every pathway that includes them takes longer to deliver its signal to an effector. A person who has been drinking therefore reacts more slowly to a hazard, and coordination, such as walking in a straight line, becomes poorer. Judgement and self-control also decline because the decision-making parts of the brain are affected.

### Voluntary and involuntary actions

Voluntary actions involve conscious decisions, such as choosing to pick up a book.

Involuntary actions occur without a conscious decision to perform each response, such as pupil constriction in bright light.

An action can involve muscles without being voluntary. The key distinction is conscious control, not the organ used or how quick the movement is. Breathing is normally automatic but can also be controlled consciously for a short time, so some actions can be either kind.

### A simple spinal reflex arc

In a simple withdrawal reflex, a sensory neurone carries an impulse from a receptor to the spinal cord.

A relay neurone passes the signal to a motor neurone, which carries an impulse to a muscle effector.

Contraction of the effector produces the withdrawal response before conscious consideration is needed.

A skin receptor detects a potentially damaging stimulus such as a sharp object. A sensory neurone carries the impulse into the spinal cord, where it crosses a synapse to a relay neurone and then another synapse to a motor neurone. The motor neurone carries the impulse to a muscle, which contracts. The reflex arc is therefore stimulus, receptor, coordinator (the spinal cord), effector, response. Impulses also pass up to the brain, so you become aware of the pain just after the hand has already moved. The arrows run one way, from receptor to effector, with no loop back to the receptor.

### Spinal-cord section drawing

A transverse spinal-cord section has central grey matter surrounded by white matter.

A biological drawing records the visible arrangement with clear outlines, accurate proportions and precise labels.

In a transverse section the spinal cord is a roughly circular slice. The central grey matter is shaped like a butterfly or the letter H and is surrounded by paler white matter; a small central canal runs through the middle of the grey matter. To draw it, use a sharp pencil and a large space. Draw the outer boundary as one continuous line, then the grey matter inside it with the shape and relative size that you can see, not a memorised symbol. Do not shade or colour. Draw straight, uncrossed label lines that end exactly on the region named. A biological drawing earns marks for clear lines, accurate proportions and precise labels.

### Reflex action

A reflex action is a means of integrating and coordinating stimuli with responses of effectors.

Reflex responses are typically rapid and automatic, linking a detected stimulus to an appropriate effector response.

Integration means bringing the incoming information together into a coordinated response. The reflex arc is the neural route; the reflex action is the resulting coordinated response. A withdrawal reflex reduces contact with a damaging stimulus, and the pupil reflex regulates the light entering the eye.

### Sense organs

Sense organs are groups of receptor cells responding to specific stimuli: light, sound, touch, temperature and chemicals.

Receptor cells convert stimulation into signals carried towards the central nervous system.

Different receptors are suited to different kinds of stimulus. The eye detects light, the ear detects sound, and receptor cells in skin detect touch and temperature. Chemical receptors contribute to smell and taste. A sense organ includes supporting structures that help stimulation reach its receptors, but these supporting structures do not all detect the stimulus.

### Cornea

The cornea is the transparent curved front surface of the eye.

It refracts incoming light, contributing much of the eye’s focusing power.

Light first enters the eye through the cornea. It is transparent so that light can pass through and curved so that it bends the light inwards. Most of the refraction in the eye happens at the cornea, but its shape is fixed, so it cannot adjust the focus for different distances. It lies in front of the iris and pupil; the lens is behind the pupil.

### Iris

The iris is the coloured ring surrounding the pupil.

Its circular and radial muscles alter pupil diameter and regulate the light entering the eye.

The iris is a ring of muscle tissue, brown, blue or green in colour, with the pupil as the opening at its centre. Circular muscles run around the pupil and radial muscles run outwards like spokes. Their action changes the diameter of the pupil and so the amount of light reaching the retina. The iris does not change the shape of the lens; the ciliary muscles do that.

### Pupil

The pupil is the opening within the iris.

Light passes through the pupil towards the lens and retina.

The pupil is the dark opening at the centre of the iris. Light passes through it to the lens and then to the retina. Under the same illumination a wider pupil admits more light than a narrower one. The pupil has no muscle of its own: the muscles of the iris change the size of the opening.

### Lens

The lens is a transparent structure behind the pupil.

It changes shape to adjust refraction and focus images of objects at different distances on the retina.

The lens lies behind the pupil. It is transparent and elastic and is held in place by suspensory ligaments. The cornea and the lens both refract light, but only the lens can change shape, so it provides the adjustable focusing. Changing the size of the pupil changes how much light enters, not where the light is focused.

### Retina

The retina is the light-sensitive layer lining the back of the eye.

Its receptor cells respond to light and initiate nerve signals.

The retina lines the back of the eye and contains the light receptor cells, the rods and cones. A focused image on the retina stimulates these cells, which start nerve impulses. The retina is the layer on which the image forms; the optic nerve carries the signals away from the eye. When asked for the light-sensitive part of the eye, the answer is the retina, not the lens or the optic nerve.

### Optic nerve

The optic nerve leaves the back of the eye and connects it with the brain.

It carries nerve impulses generated after retinal stimulation.

The optic nerve leaves the back of the eye and runs to the brain. The retina first converts light into nerve impulses, and the optic nerve carries those impulses to the brain, where the image is interpreted. Light itself does not travel along the nerve.

### Blind spot

The blind spot is the region where the optic nerve leaves the retina.

It has no rods or cones and therefore does not detect light.

The blind spot is the small region of the retina where the optic nerve leaves the eye. There are no rods or cones there, so light falling on it cannot be detected. You do not normally notice the gap because the brain fills it in, using information from the surrounding part of the image and from the other eye.

### Pupil reflex

In bright light, circular iris muscles contract and radial muscles relax, narrowing the pupil.

In dim light, radial iris muscles contract and circular muscles relax, widening the pupil.

These muscle groups work antagonistically: when one contracts the other relaxes. In bright light the circular muscles, which run around the pupil, contract and the radial muscles, which run outwards like spokes, relax, so the pupil narrows. A smaller pupil reduces the light entering and protects the light-sensitive retina. In dim light the radial muscles contract and the circular muscles relax, so a larger pupil admits more light. The pupil is an opening, so it cannot contract as a muscle does. The response changes how much light is admitted; it does not bring a near object into focus. Walking from a bright street into a dark cinema shows it: at first you see little, then the pupils widen and you see more.

### Accommodation for near objects

For a near object, ciliary muscles contract and suspensory ligaments become less tense.

The lens becomes thicker and more curved, increasing refraction so the near object can be focused on the retina.

Light from a nearby point reaches the eye more divergent than light from a distant point. Stronger refraction is needed to focus it at the same distance behind the lens. Ciliary-muscle contraction reduces tension in the ligaments; it does not pull the lens flatter. The elastic lens rounds up when it is less stretched. Reading a book is a near-focus task, which is why the ciliary muscles tire after long periods of close work.

### Accommodation for distant objects

For a distant object, ciliary muscles relax and suspensory ligaments become more tense.

The lens is pulled thinner and flatter, reducing refraction to focus distant light on the retina.

Light rays from a distant point are nearly parallel when they arrive. They need less refraction than rays from a near point. Relaxed ciliary muscles allow the ring they form to widen, which tightens the ligaments and stretches the lens; the ligaments do not actively contract. Looking up from a book at a distant hill is a change from near focus to distant focus: the ciliary muscles relax and the lens becomes thinner.

### Rods and cones

Rods are sensitive in dim light but do not distinguish colours; cones provide colour vision and work best in brighter light.

Cones are concentrated at the fovea, while rods are more abundant away from the fovea; neither is present at the blind spot.

Use both function and position when distinguishing these receptor types. Fine detail and colour are best when an image falls on the cone-rich fovea under adequate illumination. In dim light, rod sensitivity is useful but colour discrimination is poor. The two types are receptor cells in the retina, not muscles of the iris.

### Nervous and hormonal coordination

Nervous signals usually produce rapid, relatively short-lived responses through neurones.

Hormones travel in blood and often produce slower, longer-lasting responses in their target tissues.

The delivery route explains much of the difference. A neurone directs an impulse along a specific pathway; a hormone is carried round the whole body in the blood but acts only on organs whose cells have receptors for it. The speed and duration differences are general: adrenaline acts within seconds, while an insulin response may last for hours. Nervous responses, such as a reflex, usually start almost at once and stop when the signal stops.

## 3.13.2 Drugs and their effects

### Key points

| Idea | Essential distinction |
|---|---|
| Drug | alters chemical reactions; includes medicines |
| Antibiotic | acts on susceptible bacteria, not viral targets |
| Depressant | reduces nervous-system activity |
| Dependence | withdrawal may follow reduction or stopping |
| Shared equipment | can transfer infected blood; the drug does not create HIV |

### Drugs

A drug is a substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.

Drugs can have useful therapeutic effects as well as harmful effects.

The term includes medicines; it does not mean that a substance is illegal. Effects depend on the substance, the dose, the way it is taken and the person.

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics treat bacterial infections by killing susceptible bacteria or inhibiting their growth.

Different antibiotics act on different bacterial targets, and resistant bacteria may not be controlled by a particular antibiotic.

An antibiotic works only against bacteria, and only against strains that are susceptible to it. Each time an antibiotic is used, susceptible bacteria are killed while any resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, so resistant strains become more common. This is natural selection, and it is why antibiotics should be used only when they are really needed.

### Why antibiotics do not treat viruses

Antibiotics act on bacterial structures or processes that viruses do not possess.

Viruses reproduce using host-cell machinery rather than independent bacterial metabolism.

For example, a medicine that disrupts bacterial cell-wall formation cannot act on a virus by that mechanism because a virus has no cell wall of the bacterial kind. The distinction is about mechanism: viruses are not simply too small for a medicine to reach. Viruses must be controlled in other ways, such as by vaccination.

### Depressant effects

Excessive alcohol and heroin depress central nervous system activity.

Depressant action can impair coordination and, at high doses, dangerously reduce breathing.

A depressant is defined by reduced nervous activity, not by sadness. A person may initially seem less inhibited even while judgement and coordination are impaired. Heroin and alcohol together are especially dangerous because both can slow breathing, and their effects add together.

### Reaction time and self-control

Alcohol and heroin can lengthen reaction time and impair judgement and self-control.

Impaired coordination increases the risk of injury during activities needing quick, accurate responses.

Reaction time is the interval between a stimulus and the response. A longer interval means a slower reaction, not a faster one. Reduced inhibition can make a person more likely to take risks while their ability to respond is impaired. Slow reactions after drinking come from slower nervous signalling; smoke, by contrast, damages the lungs and reduces oxygen carriage, which is a different problem.

### Alcohol and the liver

Repeated excessive alcohol consumption can damage liver cells.

Long-term damage can cause scarring that reduces normal liver function.

The liver processes alcohol, so its cells are exposed to alcohol and to the products of its breakdown. Repeated heavy drinking kills liver cells, and scar tissue replaces them. Severe scarring disrupts the organisation needed for metabolic functions, so the liver handles nutrients, drugs and waste products less well. The longer and heavier the drinking, the greater the risk.

### Dependence and withdrawal

Addiction involves continued compulsive drug use despite harmful consequences.

Withdrawal symptoms can occur after a dependent person stops or reduces a drug.

Alcohol or heroin dependence can involve physical and psychological changes. Withdrawal may involve distress, sweating, shaking, nausea or other symptoms, depending on the drug and the person. Severe alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous, so dependent people need healthcare support when they stop.

### Social consequences

Problematic alcohol or heroin use can disrupt family relationships, attendance and work or school performance.

Impaired judgement and dependence can increase financial difficulties and risks to the user and others.

Each harm has a cause that can be traced: missed work can reduce income; spending on a drug can divert limited household money; impaired coordination can expose others to injury. Treatment and support can reduce these harms.

### Injection and infection risk

Sharing injection equipment can transfer infected blood between people.

Blood-borne pathogens such as HIV can enter through contaminated needles or syringes.

Heroin itself does not make HIV. The route is transfer of a virus that is already present in infected blood. Equipment that looks clean may still carry contaminated blood, and sharing is not made safe by the absence of visible blood. HIV is not spread by touching, sharing a room or sharing food with a person who uses drugs.

## 3.13.3 Hormones and endocrine glands

### Key points

| Gland | Hormone |
|---|---|
| Adrenal | adrenaline |
| Pancreas | insulin and glucagon (see 3.14) |
| Testes | testosterone |
| Ovaries | oestrogen |

Hormones travel in blood to specific target organs. Adrenaline increases glucose availability and pulse rate to support rapid activity.

### Hormones

A hormone is a chemical substance, produced by a gland and carried by the blood, which alters the activity of one or more specific target organs.

Target organs respond because their cells have receptors suited to the hormone.

A hormone is made in an endocrine gland and released straight into the blood, which carries it round the whole body. Only the target organs respond, because only their cells have receptors that fit that hormone. Hormones can change metabolic activity, growth or reproduction. They are broken down, mainly in the liver, so a response fades some time after the gland stops secreting; adrenaline acts within seconds, while the effects of insulin last for hours.

### Adrenal glands

The adrenal glands lie above the kidneys.

They secrete adrenaline.

The two adrenal glands sit one on top of each kidney. Adrenaline is carried in the blood to the heart, liver and other organs. It raises the pulse rate and the blood glucose concentration, so the body is prepared for rapid action such as running away from danger.

### Pancreas

The pancreas lies in the abdomen near the stomach.

It secretes insulin into the blood.

The pancreas lies just below the stomach. It has two roles. It releases digestive enzymes through a duct into the small intestine, and it secretes the hormones insulin and glucagon straight into the blood. Hormones leave an endocrine gland by way of the blood, not through a duct. Insulin lowers blood glucose after a meal (3.14).

### Testes

The testes are situated in the scrotum.

They secrete testosterone.

The testes hang in the scrotum, outside the main body cavity. They make sperm and they also secrete testosterone into the blood. Testosterone causes the male secondary sexual characteristics that appear at puberty, such as a deeper voice, facial hair and increased muscle growth.

### Ovaries

The ovaries lie in the pelvis, beside the uterus.

They secrete oestrogen.

The ovaries lie in the lower abdomen, one on each side of the uterus. They release eggs and secrete oestrogen into the blood. Oestrogen causes the female secondary sexual characteristics that appear at puberty, such as breast development and wider hips, and helps to control the menstrual cycle.

### Adrenaline and metabolic activity

Adrenaline can increase pulse rate and raise blood glucose concentration.

A faster circulation and greater glucose availability help support increased muscular respiration during rapid action.

The liver can release glucose from its glycogen store. A higher pulse rate helps deliver glucose and oxygen to active tissues. Together these changes make energy release more readily available; adrenaline is not itself a nutrient and is not burned as a fuel. Worked example: a runner sees a car and steps back quickly. The step back itself is a nervous response, over in a fraction of a second. Adrenaline is secreted into the blood at the same time, and its effects follow over the next seconds to minutes: the pulse rate rises from about 70 to about 120 beats per minute and blood glucose rises. More glucose and oxygen then reach the leg muscles, which respire faster and release energy while the runner keeps running or stays alert. The hormonal effect follows the nervous one and lasts longer. After the danger passes the adrenaline is broken down and the pulse rate returns to normal.

## 3.14 Homeostasis

### Key points

| Regulated condition | Opposing responses |
|---|---|
| Temperature high/low | greater cooling versus greater heat conservation/production |
| Blood glucose high/low | insulin-supported storage versus glucagon-supported release |
| Feedback | a response opposes the deviation and reduces as normal conditions return |
| Type 1 diabetes | insufficient insulin; replacement and monitoring support control |
| Cooling model | control initial heat and surroundings; evaluate limits of the model |

### Homeostasis

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment.

In a living body, “constant” means kept within a narrow suitable range rather than exactly unchanged at every moment.

External conditions can vary widely while internal conditions remain suitable for cells. Examples include blood glucose concentration and core temperature. Enzyme-controlled reactions work effectively only within suitable conditions, so large deviations disrupt cell activity. Homeostatic responses regulate the internal variable; they do not make the outside environment constant.

### Negative feedback

Negative feedback detects a deviation from a suitable level and triggers responses that oppose it.

As the variable returns towards its normal range, the corrective response is reduced.

A receptor detects a change, a coordinating system processes the information, and effectors carry out a correction. “Negative” means opposing the original change, not damaging. If body temperature rises, increased cooling reduces it; when it falls back, the cooling response decreases. A response that amplified the original change would drive the variable further from its normal level and would not stabilise it.

### Ectothermic temperature regulation

Ectothermic animals rely largely on environmental heat rather than sustained metabolic heat production to control body temperature.

They can regulate temperature behaviourally by changing exposure to sun, shade or warm and cool surfaces.

An ectotherm need not have the same temperature as the surrounding air at each instant. A lizard can bask on warm rock in the morning, then move into shade when heat gain becomes excessive. Body orientation and timing of activity also change heat exchange. When conditions are too cold, biochemical activity and movement may slow, reducing the ability to seek a different place. The behaviour changes heat gain or loss rather than producing a fixed body temperature regardless of conditions.

### Endothermic temperature regulation

Endotherms generate substantial metabolic heat and regulate its retention or loss.

When cold they can increase heat production and reduce heat loss; when hot they can increase heat loss.

Birds and mammals generally maintain relatively stable core temperatures across a range of environmental temperatures. In a mammal the brain (a region called the hypothalamus) monitors the temperature of the blood. When it falls, skin arterioles narrow, muscles shiver and hairs are raised; raised fur or fluffed feathers trap a thicker layer of insulating air. Metabolic heat production requires a food supply, so regulation has an energy cost. Endotherms still exchange heat with their surroundings, and behaviour, such as seeking shade, supplements the physiological mechanisms. The human example below shows coordinated changes in muscles, skin blood flow and sweating.

### Human core-temperature control

Sweat evaporation removes heat; vasodilation increases skin blood flow and heat transfer to the surroundings when a suitable gradient exists.

Shivering increases muscle heat production; vasoconstriction reduces skin blood flow and helps conserve core heat.

Subcutaneous fat insulates by slowing heat transfer.

When core temperature rises, sweat glands secrete more sweat and skin arterioles widen. Heat is transferred to the skin in blood. Evaporation needs energy, taken from the skin, so it cools; sweat that drips away without evaporating provides less cooling. Humid air can limit evaporation. When cold, skin arterioles narrow, reducing warm blood near the surface, and rapid involuntary muscle contractions release additional heat through respiration. Insulation slows heat loss but does not generate heat. Vasodilation and vasoconstriction describe changes in vessel diameter, not vessels moving towards or away from the surface. These mechanisms work with negative feedback, reducing as core temperature recovers.

### Blood-glucose regulation

When blood glucose rises, pancreatic insulin promotes glucose uptake and storage, including conversion to glycogen in the liver.

When blood glucose falls, pancreatic glucagon promotes breakdown of liver glycogen and release of glucose into blood.

These opposing responses help maintain blood glucose within a suitable range by negative feedback.

After a meal, absorbed glucose enters the blood. Insulin is carried in blood to target tissues; it is not a digestive enzyme. The liver stores glycogen, a polymer made from glucose. Between meals, glucagon promotes release of glucose from liver stores. Distinguish the three names: glucose is the soluble sugar in blood, glycogen is the storage carbohydrate, and glucagon is the hormone. As the deviation is corrected, the hormonal response falls. Liver glycogen stores are finite, so release cannot continue at the same rate indefinitely without replenishment.

### Type 1 diabetes symptoms

Type 1 diabetes involves insufficient insulin, so blood glucose can remain abnormally high.

Symptoms can include frequent urination, unusual thirst, tiredness and loss of body mass.

High glucose can be lost in urine and carries extra water with it, producing thirst. Cells cannot use the glucose effectively without insulin, which contributes to tiredness and to loss of body mass as stored nutrients are broken down. Several of these symptoms also occur in other conditions, so a blood glucose test is used to confirm diabetes.

### Type 1 diabetes treatment

Type 1 diabetes is managed with insulin replacement and monitoring of glucose levels.

Food intake and activity are coordinated with insulin under individual clinical guidance.

Insulin is commonly supplied by injection or pump; swallowing ordinary insulin would expose a protein to digestion. Monitoring helps avoid both high and dangerously low blood glucose. Treatment supports regulation but does not remove the condition. Replacing the missing hormone is useful because it restores the control of blood glucose that the pancreas can no longer provide. Doses are adjusted for each patient by their healthcare team.

### Investigating cooling

Cooling of warm-water tubes can model how surface conditions affect heat loss.

A wet covering can increase cooling through evaporation, while dry insulation can slow heat transfer.

To compare wet and dry coverings, use matching tubes, the same water volume and starting temperature, and equal fabric coverage. Keep room temperature, draughts and thermometer depth constant. Record temperature at regular intervals and compare drops over the same time; rate = temperature drop ÷ time. Repeat with fresh water and exchange positions to reduce the effect of a local draught. For surface-area comparisons, change exposed area while matching the amount of water and starting temperature as closely as possible. State what differs in the geometry; unequal water volumes can otherwise create a thermal-capacity difference. The model has no blood flow, sweating control or heat production, so its result cannot demonstrate the whole mechanism of human temperature regulation. Use warm water at a teacher-approved temperature to reduce burn risk.
