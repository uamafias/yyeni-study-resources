## 1.1 Mathematical skills for biology

### Key points

| Skill | Method |
|---|---|
| Change | final minus initial |
| Mean | sum divided by number |
| Percentage | part divided by whole, times 100 |
| Ratio | compare in stated order using common units |
| Reciprocal rate | 1/time for a fixed endpoint |
| Indices | square area, cube volume |
| Rearrangement | undo addition by subtraction; multiplication by division |

### Arithmetic with biological quantities

Addition combines quantities of the same kind, while subtraction finds a difference or a change. Multiplication combines equal groups and division shares a total or finds a rate.

Keep the units beside the numbers. For a change, subtract the initial value from the final value; a negative change means a decrease.

Suppose a pot and dry soil have a mass of 260 g. After watering they have a mass of 308 g. The added mass is 308 − 260 = 48 g. If 12 identical pots each receive 40 cm³ of water, the volume needed is 12 × 40 = 480 cm³. If a shoot grows 18 mm in 6 days, its average increase is 18 ÷ 6 = 3 mm per day.

### Means and repeated measurements

The arithmetic mean is the sum of the measurements divided by the number of measurements. It describes the centre of the repeated readings, not the value of a selected convenient reading.

Repeat a measurement under the same conditions before finding a mean. Repetition helps reveal variation; it does not remove a consistent error in the apparatus.

For shoot lengths of 14, 16 and 15 mm, the total is 45 mm and the mean is 45 ÷ 3 = 15 mm. Keep the original readings in the table beside the mean so that the spread is visible. If one reading differs sharply, investigate the method and repeat it; do not delete it merely because it is inconvenient.

### Decimal place value

A decimal point separates whole units from fractions of a unit; digits to its right represent tenths, hundredths and smaller parts. Align decimal points when adding or subtracting decimal readings.

Retain extra calculator digits during a calculation and round the final answer to suitable precision. Writing more digits than an instrument can support does not make a measurement more accurate.

A mass change from 2.35 g to 2.80 g is 0.45 g, not 45 g. When comparing 0.8 and 0.65, write 0.8 as 0.80: eight tenths is greater than sixty-five hundredths.

### Fractions of a sample

A fraction expresses a number of equal parts relative to a whole: the numerator counts selected parts and the denominator states how many equal parts form the whole.

To find a fraction of a quantity, divide by the denominator and multiply by the numerator. Fractions describing parts of the same sample should use the same whole.

If 6 of 24 seedlings wilt, the wilted fraction is 6/24, simplified to 1/4 by dividing numerator and denominator by 6. Three quarters of a 40-seed sample is 40 ÷ 4 × 3 = 30 seeds. The fraction itself has no unit; the number of seedlings or seeds does. A fraction such as 1/4 is 0.25, so fractions and decimals can describe the same proportion.

### Percentages and percentage change

A percentage expresses a proportion per hundred. Percentage of a sample is the selected number divided by the whole number, multiplied by 100.

Percentage change uses the initial measurement as its denominator: change divided by initial value, multiplied by 100. A percentage increase and a final percentage of the original are different quantities.

If 18 of 30 seeds germinate, 18 ÷ 30 × 100 = 60 percent germination. If tissue mass rises from 10 g to 12 g, the gain is 2 g; 2 ÷ 10 × 100 = 20 percent gain. The final mass is 120 percent of the starting mass, but it did not increase by 120 percent. A negative percentage change means loss. State what the percentage refers to and keep the original denominator visible in working.

### Ratios

A ratio compares quantities in a stated order; simplify a ratio by dividing both sides by the same common factor. Convert quantities to the same unit before comparing like measurements.

Ratios of outcomes describe proportions, not guaranteed counts in a small sample. Keep the order of the named categories with the ratio.

A tray with 18 purple-flowered and 6 white-flowered plants has a purple:white ratio of 3:1. Reversing the categories gives white:purple = 1:3. A 3:1 ratio contains four parts altogether; it means three quarters and one quarter, not three out of one.

### Reciprocal measures

The reciprocal of a non-zero number x is 1/x. In some timed investigations, 1/time can be used as a relative rate when the same fixed endpoint is measured in each trial.

A shorter time for the same endpoint gives a larger reciprocal. Reciprocal time has units per unit time, such as s⁻¹, rather than seconds.

If identical endpoints are reached after 20 s and 40 s, their reciprocal times are 0.05 s⁻¹ and 0.025 s⁻¹. The first relative rate is twice the second. This comparison depends on using the same amount of change or the same endpoint; 1/time is not a meaningful direct comparison if different trials measure different endpoints.

### Mathematical instruments

A ruler measures length from its zero mark; a set square helps construct perpendicular axes, a protractor measures angles and compasses draw arcs or circles at a fixed radius.

To avoid parallax, keep your line of sight perpendicular to the scale at the reading: for a ruler lying flat the eye is directly above the mark, and for a vertical scale such as a measuring cylinder it is level with the mark. Keep the measuring edge aligned with the object. Starting at the worn end of a ruler instead of its zero creates an offset.

For a leaf, align the midrib from base to tip with the ruler, not the curved edge around the blade. If an object begins at 12 mm and ends at 49 mm, its length is 37 mm: use the difference, not the final scale reading. A protractor is useful when dividing a circle into sectors proportional to counts; use a set square to keep horizontal and vertical axes at right angles.

### Direct and inverse proportion

In direct proportion, multiplying one variable by a factor multiplies the other by the same factor, so y/x is constant. In inverse proportion, multiplying one by a factor divides the other by that factor, so xy is constant.

A relationship can increase without being directly proportional; check ratios or products rather than relying on a general trend.

At a constant water-delivery rate, 20 cm³ in 5 s predicts 40 cm³ in 10 s, a directly proportional change. For a fixed volume of 60 cm³, a rate of 3 cm³ per second takes 20 s, while 6 cm³ per second takes 10 s: rate and time are inversely proportional. State the fixed condition before applying either model. Biological relationships often stop following a simple proportion when another factor limits the process.

### Indices

A positive whole-number index states how many times a base is multiplied by itself. For example, 3² means 3 × 3, not 3 × 2.

Area uses squared length units and volume uses cubed length units. If a cube side doubles, its surface area becomes four times as large and its volume becomes eight times as large.

For side length L, one square face has area L² and a cube has volume L³. At L = 4 mm, the face area is 16 mm² and the volume is 64 mm³. Multiplying equal bases adds their indices: 10² × 10³ = 10⁵. Brackets matter: 2 × 3² means 18, while (2 × 3)² means 36. Calculate the power before the multiplication unless brackets say otherwise.

### Rearranging simple equations

For x = y + z, subtract z to obtain y = x − z, or subtract y to obtain z = x − y. For x = yz, divide x by the known factor to find the unknown factor.

Rearrange symbolically before substituting values and check that the resulting units match the quantity requested.

If final mass equals starting mass plus mass gain, a final 19 g and gain 4 g give a starting mass of 15 g. If distance equals speed multiplied by time, time equals distance divided by speed. A distance of 30 mm at 5 mm per second takes 6 s. Substituting the result into the original equation is a useful private check; interpreting the result means explaining its biological meaning, not merely calculating it again.

## 1.2.1 Observing, handling apparatus and planning variables

### Key points

| Investigation term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Observation | seen or measured result |
| Independent variable | deliberately changed |
| Dependent variable | measured response |
| Controlled (constant) variable | kept the same |
| Aim | relationship to investigate |
| Hypothesis | testable proposed explanation |

### Observations and inferences

An observation records what is seen or measured, while an inference is an explanation drawn from those observations. Record colour, number, dimensions or time using terms that another observer can understand.

Record readings promptly at the resolution the instrument supports, and distinguish a qualitative change from a numerical measurement.

“The leaf is yellow between the veins” is an observation; “the plant lacks a mineral” is an inference needing further evidence. A useful record gives the specimen, treatment and time as well as the result. For a colour test, compare against a stated starting colour or a control rather than writing simply “changed”. Observe comparable regions or fields of view when comparing samples; changing the region can produce an apparent difference unrelated to the treatment.

### Choosing a technique

A measuring technique should match the quantity and range being investigated, and its resolution should distinguish the differences of interest.

Practise the technique and apply the same endpoint and operating procedure across treatments so that technique changes do not explain the result.

A balance measures tissue mass more directly than estimating it from appearance. A ruler can measure a shoot several centimetres long, whereas a microscopic cell needs a calibrated image or microscope method. Collect enough measurements to compare variation. A method should also be feasible with available equipment; an extremely precise instrument does not solve poor sampling or an undefined endpoint.

### Safe and competent handling

Identify hazards before practical work and select precautions that reduce their likelihood or severity. Keep containers labelled, secure apparatus and follow the teacher’s instructions for reagents and disposal.

Use eye protection where splashes are possible, a pipette filler for liquids and suitable handling tools for hot containers; wash hands after handling biological material.

Keep cutting work on a tile and direct a blade away from fingers. Heat liquids using a stable arrangement and an appropriate water bath when directed; flammable liquids need particular care and should not be heated over an exposed flame. Report spills and damaged glass rather than carrying on. Competence includes choosing a safe way to measure accurately, not merely obtaining a reading quickly.

### Independent, dependent and controlled variables

The independent variable is the condition deliberately changed, and the dependent variable is the response measured. Controlled (constant) variables are conditions kept the same so they do not provide competing explanations.

A fair comparison changes the intended factor while making relevant other conditions comparable. A control treatment and controlled variables are different ideas.

When testing how temperature affects time to reach a colour-test endpoint, temperature is independent and time is dependent. The initial volumes and concentrations may need to be kept the same. “Amount” is too vague: specify volume in cm³, mass in g or concentration in a named unit. Explain how a condition is controlled, such as keeping tubes in the same water bath, rather than just saying it is constant.

### Aims and hypotheses

An aim states what relationship an investigation will test. A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation or relationship, and a prediction states the result expected if it is supported.

Name the independent and dependent variables so that an aim or hypothesis can be tested by the proposed measurements.

“To investigate seeds” does not identify a comparison. “To test how soaking time affects the percentage of seeds that germinate” names both variables. A hypothesis might predict a higher germination percentage after a moderate soak than after no soak, with other conditions equal. Results can support or fail to support this prediction within the range tested; a single trial does not establish a universal rule.

## 1.2.2 Finding and recording information

### Key points

Select relevant, traceable information; prepare a logical table; retain raw readings; label quantities and units; distinguish data from interpretation.

### Selecting useful information

Choose information that addresses the biological question, and organise it by the factors or outcomes being compared. Keep a record of the source and distinguish measurements from opinions or unsupported claims.

Compare sources for method, sample and date before combining their results; unlike measurements cannot be combined merely because they concern the same organism.

To compare water demand in seedlings, a table giving water volume, number of seedlings and duration is more useful than an advertisement saying a plant grows quickly. Convert compatible units and account for sample size. A source describing a different species or growing condition may still be relevant, but explain the limitation. A disagreement should lead to checking methods and conditions, not simply selecting the source whose answer you prefer.

### Logical records

Prepare a results table before taking readings, with treatment values in a logical order and separate columns for repeats and calculated means. Put the quantity and unit in the heading.

Keep raw observations distinguishable from calculated summaries and use a consistent precision supported by the apparatus.

For a soaking trial, headings might be Soaking time / h, Seeds tested / number, Seeds germinated / number and Germination / %. Recording the original counts lets another learner evaluate the calculated percentage. If results are later plotted, transfer the quantities and units consistently to the axes. Do not quietly change the order of treatments to make a smoother trend.

### Why units matter

A numerical measurement without a unit is incomplete: 12 may refer to 12 mm, 12 cm or another quantity. A unit tells the reader the scale of the measurement.

Use one stated unit within a column and convert readings before calculating a mean or comparing them.

A length of 15 mm equals 1.5 cm, but a mean of the unconverted numbers 15 and 1.5 is meaningless. Record the unit in the heading rather than repeating it inconsistently beside selected values. Counts are dimensionless but the objects counted must still be named; “seed number” is clearer than an unexplained numeral. The same care applies to rates, for which both the measured quantity and time interval matter.

## 1.2.3 Processing data and drawing graphs

### Key points

Identify the variables before plotting. Use regular scales, the independent variable on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis, quantity / unit labels on both axes, clear points and a title naming the dependent quantity against the independent one. Quote readings when interpreting.

### Processing a results table

Process raw readings with an operation that answers the investigation question, such as a mean, difference, percentage or rate. Show the operation and keep its units meaningful.

A missing table entry should be calculated from the specified rows or columns rather than guessed from the expected biological trend.

For three repeat counts of 8, 10 and 12, the mean is 10. To compare samples containing different totals, percentages are more appropriate than raw counts. Suppose tray 1 has 9 of 12 seeds germinate, tray 2 has 15 of 20 and tray 3 has 24 of 40. The raw counts make tray 3 look best (24 seeds), but the percentages are 75, 75 and 60 percent, so tray 3 did worst. A percentage germination column should be calculated from germinated divided by tested, multiplied by 100. Check copied values against the original record before using them, and leave a trace of corrections rather than silently altering inconvenient observations.

### Scales and axes

Choose axis scales that cover the data and use the available plotting space effectively. Equal distances along one axis represent equal numerical intervals on a linear scale.

A convenient scale makes interpolation and plotting reliable; an awkward changing scale can distort a relationship.

Read the smallest and largest values first. For time values from 0 to 80 s, marking 0, 20, 40, 60 and 80 s at equal distances is suitable. Scales of 1, 2, 5 or 10 units per large square are easiest to read; 3 or 7 units per square make points hard to place. For example, lengths from 0 to 47 mm on an axis ten large squares long suit 5 mm per square, because the top of the scale (50 mm) covers every value. The origin need not be forced into a narrow biological range if that hides the variation, but show a non-zero starting value clearly. If several curves are compared, use the same pair of axes and an unambiguous key.

### Plotting one or several curves

Plot a point at the intersection of its independent-variable and dependent-variable readings, checking both scales. Draw an appropriate smooth curve or best-fit line rather than forcing a line through a suspect reading.

For several data sets on the same axes, use distinguishable point styles or curve labels and identify them in a key.

Describe the relationship the observations justify. Lengths of 0, 3, 6 and 9 mm on days 0 to 3 lie on a straight line, so a ruled line of best fit is suitable. Lengths of 0, 8, 12, 13 and 13 mm rise quickly and then level off, so draw a smooth curve through the points rather than joining them with short straight lines. A bar chart is appropriate for separate categories, with a gap between the bars; a continuous relationship is not a series of unrelated categories. Do not extrapolate far beyond the measured range and present the resulting value as an observation.

### Axis labels and units

An axis label names the quantity and its unit, for example time / s. The solidus separates the name from the unit; it is not an instruction to divide the plotted values.

A figure title, axis labels and a key perform different jobs: title states the relationship, labels identify coordinates, and the key distinguishes data sets.

Use the units actually measured, not units remembered from another task. “Rate” alone is incomplete when the rate could be cm³ per minute, breaths per minute or another quantity. If a quantity is a count, name the thing counted. Position labels clearly enough that another learner can read the representation without looking back at the investigation instructions.

### Independent on x, dependent on y

Place the independent variable on the horizontal x-axis and the dependent variable on the vertical y-axis. This lets the reader see how the measured response changes with the chosen factor.

Before plotting, identify the variables from the method instead of assuming that time must be on the horizontal axis in every experiment.

In a trial changing temperature and measuring time to an endpoint, temperature belongs on x and time on y. In a growth record made at fixed intervals, time is the independent variable and length is dependent. In a trial changing light intensity and counting bubbles per minute, light intensity belongs on x and bubbles per minute on y. Read each coordinate in the order x then y. Reversing the axes changes the representation and any gradient, even though the underlying readings are the same.

### Marking points clearly

Use a small cross or an encircled dot so that the intended coordinate is clear. Large blobs or thick marks hide how accurately the point was positioned.

If a further curve is added, its points may be marked with vertical crosses (+), with a key explaining which observations each symbol represents.

Centre the symbol at the intersection of the two readings. Check a point against the nearest numbered tick marks before drawing a curve; a later neat curve cannot correct a misplaced reading. A key should identify treatments rather than merely say “first curve” and “second curve”. Use the same convention throughout a set of measurements so that scattered points are not mistaken for different samples.

### Titles

A title identifies the dependent quantity against the independent quantity. It should name the biological measurements rather than repeat a treatment code that has not been explained.

A good title does not replace axis units or the data-set key.

For measurements of seedling height on successive days, use “Seedling height against time”. For a fixed-time investigation of gas volume at several temperatures, use “Gas volume against temperature”. If treatments are compared on the same axes, name them in a key and let the title describe their common relationship.

### Reading a representation

Describe a trend by naming both variables and the direction of change, then support it with readings. Distinguish the pattern from a biological explanation for it.

Interpolation estimates within the observed range, while extrapolation extends beyond it and is less secure. A correlation alone does not establish what caused a change.

A rise from 12 mm to 20 mm over four days supports an increase of 8 mm over that interval. It does not establish that another four days will produce the same gain. Reading a value between two plotted points is interpolation: if a plant is 12 mm on day 2 and 20 mm on day 4 and the points are joined by a straight line, the day 3 length is about 16 mm. Reading beyond the last point, such as day 8, is extrapolation and is less secure. If a curve becomes horizontal, the measured quantity is staying approximately constant; that differs from a falling curve. Compare curves at the same independent-variable value and note the range to which your conclusion applies.

## 1.2.4 Units, prefixes and standard form

### Key points

| Quantity | Example unit |
|---|---|
| Length | m, mm, μm |
| Area | m² |
| Volume | m³, cm³ |
| Mass | kg, g |
| Time | s |
| Rate | quantity per unit time |

Convert before comparing: 1 cm = 10 mm; 1 mm = 1000 μm. Square/cube length factors for area/volume.

### Length, area and volume

Length measures one dimension, area measures a surface and volume measures occupied space. Squaring or cubing a conversion factor is necessary when converting areas or volumes.

For a cube of side L, surface area is 6L² and volume is L³. Surface-area-to-volume ratio decreases as similarly shaped objects become larger.

One cm is 10 mm, but one cm² is 100 mm² and one cm³ is 1000 mm³. So 3 cm² = 3 × 100 = 300 mm², and 5000 mm³ = 5 cm³. A cube of side 2 cm has surface area 24 cm² and volume 8 cm³, a ratio of 3:1 when the numerical values use compatible cm-based units. A side-4 cm cube has 96 cm² and 64 cm³, a ratio of 1.5:1. As an object becomes larger, its volume grows faster than its surface area, so there is less surface for each unit of material that needs to be supplied.

### Units for biological measurements

The SI base units commonly used here include metre for length, kilogram for mass, second for time and kelvin for temperature. Degrees Celsius are commonly used for laboratory temperature readings.

Derived units combine base units, such as square metres for area, cubic metres for volume and metres per second for speed.

Use smaller compatible units where needed: mm for a leaf, μm for a cell, g for a small tissue sample and cm³ for a liquid volume. One cm³ is one mL; one dm³ is one litre, and 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³. Name a measured quantity rather than choosing an instrument unit simply because it is familiar. Units attach to quantities, while counts such as seed number have no physical unit.

### Prefixes and conversion

Prefixes specify factors relative to the base unit: kilo is 1000, deci is one tenth, centi one hundredth, milli one thousandth and micro one millionth.

Convert to a common unit before comparing lengths or calculating magnification; 1 mm equals 1000 μm.

To convert 0.6 mm to μm, multiply by 1000 to obtain 600 μm. To convert 2500 μm to mm, divide by 1000 to obtain 2.5 mm. Converting to a smaller unit increases the numerical value because more small units fit into the same length. The physical object does not become larger when the numeral changes. To compare 0.25 mm with 180 μm, convert 0.25 mm to 250 μm; 250 μm is the longer. Check the direction of conversion by considering whether a cell is likely to be millimetres or micrometres across: most cells are tens of micrometres, so a cell recorded as 50 mm long signals a unit mistake.

### Standard form

Standard form writes a number as a value at least 1 but less than 10 multiplied by a power of ten. The power shows how many places the decimal point has moved: positive when the original number is large, negative when it is smaller than 1.

A positive power represents a large number and a negative power a small positive fraction. A negative power does not make the measured quantity negative.

The number 450000 is 4.5 × 10⁵; 0.00045 is 4.5 × 10⁻⁴. In the same way 0.000032 m is 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ m, and 6.4 × 10² is 640. When converting an answer back, check the size before typing a calculator exponent. Use brackets when entering a whole denominator such as 2 × 10⁻³. A result near a million should not acquire a negative exponent by accident. Carry the original physical unit through the conversion.

### Writing rate units

A rate unit includes the measured quantity and a time denominator. Metres per second can be written m/s or m s⁻¹; these forms express the same unit.

Do not confuse a rate with the accumulated quantity: a volume in cm³ differs from a volume delivery rate in cm³ per minute.

Write cm³/min or cm³ min⁻¹ for a liquid volume delivered each minute. Keep a space between the numerical value and the unit, and use a clear superscript for a negative power. If time is recorded in minutes rather than seconds, convert it before reporting a per-second rate. Using a slash in “time / s” as an axis heading is a labelling convention, while m/s describes a compound unit.

## 1.2.5 Errors, anomalies and conclusions

### Key points

A method evaluation connects a specific error to its effect and a feasible improvement. Keep raw results, investigate anomalies, distinguish random scatter from systematic bias, and limit conclusions to the evidence and conditions tested.

### Errors and improvements

Evaluate a method by identifying a specific source of error, explaining how it affects the measurement and choosing an improvement that addresses that source.

An improvement should change the faulty step rather than simply say “be more careful”. Repeats address random variation but do not remove a persistent calibration error.

If a ruler is viewed obliquely, parallax can shift the apparent endpoint; read with the eye directly above the mark, so the line of sight is perpendicular to the scale. If a balance reads 0.2 g with an empty pan, tare or check it before weighing. If a sample loses water while waiting, standardise the waiting interval or cover it when appropriate. Tie the conclusion about quality to the actual arrangement described. A method can be precise but inaccurate if repeated readings share the same offset.

### Keeping and processing raw data

Keep the original observations when processing data and record how derived values were calculated. An anomalous reading differs conspicuously from the other comparable readings or the overall pattern.

Check a possible anomaly against the original record and repeat the measurement under the same conditions where possible; do not change a value to fit an expected answer.

For repeat lengths 11, 12, 35 and 12 mm, the 35 mm reading deserves investigation. It might represent a recording error, a different specimen or real variation. A reasoned exclusion needs evidence and should be stated. If no justification exists, keep the reading and report the uncertainty. Comparing the raw spread with the processed mean helps a reader see why an apparently smooth summary may be misleading.

### Evaluating results biologically

Evaluate whether results are consistent with the stated method and relevant biological knowledge, while allowing for uncertainty and alternative explanations.

An unexpected outcome is a reason to inspect controls and measurements, not evidence that the value must be false.

For example, a warm treatment may differ in more than temperature if its sample also dries faster. An observed difference cannot then be attributed securely to temperature. Look for repeated trials, overlap among readings and the limits of the sample. Distinguish measurement evidence from explanations drawn from prior biological knowledge. A causal claim needs a method capable of separating the proposed factor from alternatives.

### Conclusions and hypotheses

A conclusion answers the investigation question using the measured pattern and refers to the range actually tested. It states whether results support the hypothesis rather than claiming absolute proof.

Use numerical evidence where available and separate an observed association from an inferred cause.

If seed germination is 70 percent after a 2 h soak and 85 percent after a 4 h soak, these results support an increase over that interval. They do not show that germination rises indefinitely with soaking time. Check whether treatment groups were comparable and whether repeats show consistent differences. Mention a limitation if the sample is small or a control was not maintained.

### Investigating anomalies

An anomalous value should be identified relative to comparable repeats or a well-supported trend, then investigated for a plausible source.

State what was done with the value and why; an anomaly may indicate an error, but it can also reveal a real feature of the system.

If most repeated endpoint times lie between 44 and 48 s but one is 12 s, check whether timing began late or whether a different endpoint was used. Repeating under controlled conditions can distinguish a one-off mistake from a reproducible difference. The uncertainty is greater when repeats remain widely spread; averaging alone hides rather than explains that spread.

### Trends with evidence

A trend describes how one quantity changes with another and should be supported by values. Name regions where a relationship rises, falls or levels off rather than saying simply that it changes.

Distinguish a change in a quantity from a change in its rate: a growing quantity can rise more slowly without decreasing.

A shoot length increasing by 8 mm in one interval and 3 mm in the next is still increasing, but at a lower average rate. When comparing treatments, compare values at the same time or other independent-variable setting. Data may suggest a pattern without establishing the biological mechanism behind it; explanations need relevant principles and conditions.

### Random and systematic error

Random errors produce scatter among repeated readings; systematic errors shift readings consistently in a direction, such as a balance zero offset.

Repeating and averaging can reduce the influence of random variation, while a systematic error needs its source corrected. Instrument resolution limits the detail that can be read.

With ruler divisions of 1 mm, an endpoint between divisions cannot justify a claim of hundredths of a millimetre. A simple uncertainty estimate for a single scale reading is often about half a smallest division, though the exact margin depends on the procedure and readings combined. Report that basis rather than pretending every instrument has the same uncertainty. A repeatable reading can still share a calibration error.

### Preventing avoidable error

Prevent avoidable errors by checking the apparatus, defining the measurement endpoint and standardising the procedure before collecting results.

Use matched samples and control relevant conditions, then repeat measurements to assess remaining variation.

Zero a balance with the empty container and use a measured volume rather than judging a fill level by appearance. Stand a measuring cylinder on a level bench and read the bottom of the meniscus with your eye level with it. If a method calls for blotting tissue, use the same blotting procedure for each sample so external liquid does not create different apparent masses. A useful precaution targets one named source of error, such as the zero reading of a balance.

## 1.2.6 Practical skills: planning, apparatus, tests and drawings

### Key points

A complete plan identifies what is changed, measured and controlled, provides a workable safe procedure, defines endpoints and includes repeats. Match apparatus to the quantity, use chemical tests under their stated conditions, and distinguish an observation from its deduction. Measure drawings and images with a ruler and use a scale bar or magnification to find real sizes.

### Planning and predicting

An investigation plan links a testable question to a changed variable, a measured response, relevant controls and a method that could produce comparable repeated results.

A reasoned prediction follows a biological explanation and states the conditions or range over which it is expected.

For seed germination, test a range of water volumes using equal numbers of the same seed type on the same material at the same temperature. Record germinated counts at a fixed time and calculate percentages. Predict that a dry treatment will germinate less than a moist treatment because germination needs water, while excess water may reduce oxygen availability; do not assume indefinite improvement with watering. Replicate treatment groups and define germination, such as visible root emergence, before counting.

### Apparatus in a workable plan

Choose apparatus that measures the dependent variable with suitable range and precision and allows relevant conditions to be controlled.

Describe how the apparatus is arranged, when measurements begin and end, and how repeats are made; a list of equipment alone is not a method.

To test temperature and endpoint time, use a water bath and thermometer to set and check temperature, equal sample volumes measured with suitable equipment, and a stopwatch for elapsed time. Allow the tubes to reach the intended temperature before starting the reaction. Use the same endpoint and starting event for repeats. Explain the safety precautions for heat or reagents. Record predicted trends as predictions, separate from readings actually observed.

### Timing

A stopwatch measures elapsed time between defined starting and finishing events. Start and stop it consistently across comparable trials.

Reaction time can affect short measurements; repeats or timing a longer fixed sequence can reduce its relative influence when appropriate.

If ten repeated movements are timed, divide their combined time by ten to estimate a mean interval rather than trying to react to a single very brief movement. This requires a process that repeats consistently and a defined count. State the time unit and the endpoint, not simply that the process was timed.

### Temperature measurement

A thermometer or suitable temperature probe measures temperature; read it after allowing it to respond to the sample or bath.

A water bath helps maintain a chosen temperature, but check the actual temperature rather than assuming the dial value is exact.

Keep the sensing part appropriately immersed without pressing it against a heated container wall. Record the reading with its unit and meaningful precision. If the temperature changes during the investigation, record this limitation or improve control; a single starting temperature does not demonstrate that the mixture remained there.

### Mass measurement

A balance measures mass. Zero or tare it with the empty container before adding the specimen so that the displayed value represents specimen mass.

Keep containers and the weighing procedure consistent, and allow the reading to settle before recording it.

For a wet tissue comparison, an uncontrolled film of surface water can be mistaken for tissue mass, so treat every sample the same way before weighing. Record starting and final masses in the same unit. A balance reading is a mass, not a volume; converting one to the other would require information about the substance.

### Liquid volumes

A measuring cylinder measures a range of approximate liquid volumes; a suitable pipette can transfer a specified volume, and a burette can deliver a measured variable volume.

For a burette, the volume delivered is final reading minus initial reading. Read the appropriate meniscus at eye level and choose an instrument appropriate to the volume and precision required.

Use a pipette filler rather than the mouth. Check that a burette outlet is filled and free of an air bubble before an accurate delivery; otherwise a volume change in the barrel can partly fill the outlet rather than reach the receiving vessel. For clear aqueous liquids with a concave meniscus, read its bottom at eye level. Do not confuse the capacity printed on an apparatus with the actual volume transferred.

### Tests and deductions

A test observation is a recorded result, while a deduction uses the known response of the test under appropriate conditions to infer a property of the sample.

Use positive and negative comparisons where available to check that the test worked and to distinguish a positive result from the original sample colour.

Record the reagent, conditions and actual observation. A colour change without the necessary heating, reagent or comparison may not justify the usual deduction. Quantitative comparisons need a consistent procedure, since unequal volumes, timing or temperature can produce different appearances for reasons other than sample content.

### Commenting on an arrangement

A useful comment on an experimental arrangement links its design to measurement quality, fair comparison or safety rather than merely describing the drawing.

Check that chosen and measured variables are separated, the apparatus can register the required change and relevant conditions are controlled.

For example, a container that can tip makes volume readings less dependable, and an open arrangement intended to collect a gas may lose gas before measurement. State the specific effect and a feasible repair. A criticism that depends on a feature the method does not mention should be conditional: if a joint leaks, collected volume will be underestimated.

### Simple chemical tests

For starch, iodine solution changes from brown/orange to blue-black in a positive test and stays brown/orange if starch is absent. For reducing sugar, Benedict’s reagent is heated safely in a hot-water bath; a positive response is a coloured precipitate ranging from green/yellow to orange/brick-red, depending on the amount of sugar, and a negative result stays blue.

For protein, the Biuret test gives a purple/lilac colour and stays blue if protein is absent; for lipid, an ethanol extraction followed by addition to water gives a cloudy white emulsion, while the mixture stays clear if lipid is absent. Ethanol is flammable and needs safe handling away from flames.

Litmus distinguishes acidic and alkaline solutions: blue litmus turns red in acid and red litmus turns blue in alkali. Universal Indicator gives a range of colours interpreted with its pH chart. Hydrogencarbonate indicator is commonly red/orange near atmospheric carbon dioxide, yellow at higher carbon dioxide and purple at lower carbon dioxide; compare with a reference under the same conditions. Describe colour changes precisely and follow the teacher’s reagent protocol. A reducing-sugar test is not a test for every carbohydrate: starch needs the iodine test.

### Evidence-based final conclusions

A justified conclusion combines a stated result with the biological explanation relevant to the investigation, while acknowledging an important alternative explanation or method limit.

Keep the conclusion proportional to the observations: few treatments or specimens support a limited inference rather than a claim about all organisms.

Distinguish a prediction made before data collection from a conclusion after it. Quote a comparison that answers the aim, and avoid substituting a memorised account of the process for an explanation of the actual observations. If conditions were not comparable, state that the data do not isolate the factor of interest.

### Images as observations

Describe structures visible in a specimen image separately from what is inferred from its identity. Measure between defined endpoints with a ruler on the displayed image.

A scale bar or trustworthy magnification permits a size estimate; resizing an image changes measured image length and can invalidate an old printed magnification.

Check the organism, orientation and field of view before labelling. An unfamiliar specimen may share structures already learned, but a feature that is not visible should not be labelled. A scale bar resizes with the image and therefore remains useful if it was originally calibrated correctly. For example, if a 10 mm scale bar on the image stands for 100 μm, a structure measuring 25 mm on the image is 25 ÷ 10 × 100 = 250 μm long. If a calculated size looks implausible, such as a cell 50 mm long, recheck the units and the measurement before giving an answer.

### Biological line drawings

A biological drawing records observed shape and relative proportions using clear continuous outlines rather than sketching, shading or invented detail.

Use a large enough drawing, straight label lines ending on the relevant structures, accurate labels and a title identifying the specimen and view.

Keep label lines from crossing where possible, draw them with a ruler and place labels so the drawing remains clear. Draw what the actual specimen shows rather than a remembered ideal one; absence from a field is not evidence that a structure never occurs in the organism. Put the title with the drawing and state the view, for example the whole organism from above. If magnification is requested, measure the drawing and the real specimen in the same units.

### Magnification of a drawing

Magnification is image or drawing size divided by actual specimen size after both lengths are expressed in the same unit. It is a ratio and carries no physical unit.

Actual size is drawing size divided by magnification. Drawing size is actual size multiplied by magnification.

A 30 mm drawing of a structure 0.5 mm long has magnification ×60. If a cell is 40 μm long and its image is 8 mm long, convert 8 mm to 8000 μm before dividing to obtain ×200. Write the multiplication sign before the numerical magnification, not mm or μm after it. A diagram with no trusted scale cannot provide an actual size merely from its appearance.

### Reading scales, accuracy and precision

Find the value of a smallest scale division from the labelled intervals before reading an apparatus diagram. Read from the correct reference point and include the unit.

Accuracy concerns closeness to the true value; precision concerns the detail or consistency of measurement. Repeated readings can reveal scatter but do not guarantee accuracy.

If a cylinder has ten equal intervals between 20 and 30 cm³, each interval is 1 cm³, so a meniscus three intervals above 20 reads 23 cm³. For a clear aqueous liquid, view the lower meniscus at eye level. Do not estimate extra decimal places that the apparatus cannot support. A record of several repeat readings and a mean makes variation visible; one finely written numeral can still be biased.

### Explaining an experimental technique

Explain a technique by connecting its steps to their purpose, such as maintaining a controlled variable, preventing contamination or defining a reproducible measurement.

An evaluation should use features actually stated or visible in the arrangement, and express uncertain causes conditionally.

For example, mark the initial liquid level so a later reading measures a change rather than an unexplained final volume. Keep the interval between treatment and measurement constant when timing could affect the result. A sound comment states the consequence of a problem and a feasible way to improve it, and is based on the apparatus and conditions actually described.
