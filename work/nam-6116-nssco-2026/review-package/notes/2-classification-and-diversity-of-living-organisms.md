## 2.1 Classification, naming and keys

### Key points

| Tool | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Shared features | justify the group an organism is placed in |
| Hierarchy | organise groups from broad to narrow: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species |
| Binomial | genus (capital letter) plus specific name (lower case) |
| Dichotomous key | follow pairs of contrasting visible features to a name |

### Grouping by shared features

Classification places organisms into groups using features they share. A useful diagnostic feature separates one group from the others being considered.

Use several relevant structural features rather than a single superficial resemblance such as colour or habitat.

A bird and a bat both have wings, but feathers identify the bird and hair identifies the mammal. A whale lives in water yet is a mammal, so habitat is not a dependable guide to a group; structure is.

When you classify, state the observed feature first and then use it to justify the group. An animal with six jointed legs and a body in three parts is placed with the insects because of those features, not because it was found on a leaf. Colour varies within a group and is weak evidence; the number of leg pairs is a structural feature and strong evidence.

### Two-part scientific names

In the binomial system, a species name has two parts: the genus name followed by the specific name. The genus begins with a capital letter and the specific name with a lower-case letter.

The complete two-part name identifies the species. Different species may share a genus, so the specific name alone is not a complete species name.

Humans are Homo sapiens: Homo is the genus and sapiens is the specific name. The lion, Panthera leo, and the tiger, Panthera tigris, share the genus Panthera but are different species because their second names differ. In print both words are italicised; handwritten, each is underlined separately. Common names vary between languages, whereas a scientific name is shared worldwide.

### A hierarchy of groups

A hierarchical classification contains broad groups divided into progressively smaller groups: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.

Organisms placed in the same smaller group share the broader groups above it, while organisms in one broad group need not belong to the same species.

The lion shows the whole sequence: kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Carnivora, family Felidae, genus Panthera, species Panthera leo. The tiger shares every level with the lion down to genus, and differs only at species.

Two species in one genus therefore share their family, but two organisms in one family may belong to different genera. Each step down holds fewer organisms that are more alike, so species is the narrowest level.

### Constructing and using dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key uses successive pairs of contrasting statements, with one applicable choice at each step. Each choice leads to another pair or to an identification.

Use visible, unambiguous features and make the two choices at a step mutually exclusive; follow the route from the beginning for each specimen.

Take four animals: an earthworm, a snail, a spider and an ant. The first pair of statements is 1a jointed legs absent, go to 2; 1b jointed legs present, go to 3. At step 2, 2a body divided into ring-like segments gives earthworm and 2b body not divided into segments gives snail. At step 3, 3a four pairs of legs gives spider and 3b three pairs of legs gives ant. Each animal follows exactly one route, and no route ends in a dead end.

“Large” and “small” are unreliable unless a measurement is given; “longer than 2 cm” works. Use only features you can see or that you are given. Check a key by following each organism’s route to its name.

Common faults are overlapping choices, such as “legs” and “many legs”, a statement with no matching pair, and a route that reaches two names. Questions may ask you to build a key from a table or to follow a given key.

## 2.2 Viruses and the main groups of organisms

### Key points

| Group | Diagnostic starting points |
|---|---|
| Virus | genetic material, protein coat, non-cellular |
| Monocot/dicot | cotyledons, veins, flower parts, roots |
| Mollusc/annelid | soft body with muscular foot / repeated body segments |
| Insect | three leg pairs, three body regions, one antenna pair |
| Arachnid | four leg pairs, no antennae |
| Crustacean | two antenna pairs, more than four leg pairs |
| Myriapod | many segments and many jointed legs |
| Vertebrates | fish fins, gills; amphibian moist skin; reptile dry scales; bird feathers; mammal hair and milk |

### Virus structure

A virus has genetic material enclosed by a protein coat. It is non-cellular: it is not made of cells and is not a single cell.

Inside a host cell, the virus genetic material carries the instructions used to make new virus particles. The protein coat surrounds and protects that material.

A virus has no cytoplasm and no cell membrane. Even a bacterium, which has no nucleus, has both, so a virus is not a cell of any kind. “Non-cellular” does not mean a very small single-celled organism: a virus is far smaller than a bacterium and built on a different plan. In a simple drawing, show the genetic material in the centre and the coat as an outline around it. The coat is protein, not a cellulose cell wall.

### Are viruses living?

Viruses carry genetic material and are replicated within suitable host cells, which supplies arguments for associating them with life.

They lack cellular organisation and independent metabolic activity, and cannot be replicated without host cells; these support treating them as non-living.

Name specific features: for, a virus carries genetic material and is copied inside a host cell; against, it does not respire, feed or grow by itself.

The judgement depends on the characteristics used to define a living organism. Because a virus meets few of them without a host, it is usually treated as a borderline case rather than a typical living thing.

### Monocotyledons

Monocotyledon seeds have one cotyledon; their leaves commonly have parallel veins.

Their flowers commonly have parts in threes or multiples of three, and their root system is typically fibrous.

Grasses and maize show this combination.

### Dicotyledons

Dicotyledon seeds have two cotyledons; their leaves commonly have a branching network of veins.

Flower parts commonly occur in fours or fives or their multiples, and a taproot with lateral branches is typical.

A bean plant is a familiar example.

### Molluscs

Molluscs have a soft, unsegmented body, and many have a shell that protects it.

A muscular foot is a characteristic structure; shell form and presence vary among members.

A snail shows a shell and a muscular foot. A slug has no external shell but is still a mollusc.

### Annelids

Annelids have a body divided into repeated ring-like segments.

The body is elongated and lacks jointed legs; earthworms also have small bristles used in movement.

The rings of an earthworm are true repeated segments, not surface colour bands. An earthworm cannot be mistaken for a myriapod, because it has no jointed legs.

### Insects

An insect has three body regions: head, thorax and abdomen.

It has three pairs of jointed legs and one pair of antennae; wings occur in many insects but are not a requirement for the group.

Insects, arachnids, crustaceans and myriapods are all arthropods: animals with jointed legs, a segmented body and a hard external skeleton called an exoskeleton.

An ant is an insect even when it has no wings. Count the legs attached to the thorax, and do not mistake antennae for legs.

### Arachnids

Arachnids have four pairs of jointed legs and no antennae.

Their body is commonly described as two main regions, rather than the head, thorax and abdomen of insects.

A spider shows the eight-legged arrangement. Scorpions and ticks are arachnids too.

### Crustaceans

Crustaceans have two pairs of antennae and more than four pairs of jointed legs.

A hard external covering protects the body, and the main body regions are typically a joined head and thorax region and an abdomen.

Crabs, shrimps, woodlice and water fleas are crustaceans. Count antennae and leg pairs rather than judging by shape: a woodlouse has seven pairs of legs and two pairs of antennae, so it is a crustacean although it lives on land.

### Myriapods

Myriapods have an elongated body made of many segments, with many jointed legs.

They have one pair of antennae; centipedes have one pair of legs on each segment and millipedes have two.

An earthworm has no jointed legs, whereas centipedes and millipedes have many, so they are arthropods, not annelids.

### Fish

Fish have fins and a streamlined body suited to movement through water.

They obtain oxygen through gills. Most fish have scales on the skin, but a few, such as catfish, have none.

Fins and gills are the most dependable features; in bony fish the gills lie under a gill cover (operculum). A lateral line runs along each side.

### Amphibians

Amphibians typically have moist skin without the dry scales of reptiles.

Familiar adult forms commonly have four limbs; their larvae usually live in water and differ from adults.

A frog has smooth, moist skin and begins life as a tadpole. Moist skin is more useful than living near water, because habitat alone does not separate amphibians from aquatic reptiles.

### Reptiles

Reptiles have dry skin covered with scales.

A typical reptile such as a lizard has clawed toes, and most reptiles lay eggs with leathery shells on land. Snakes have no limbs, so having four limbs is not a requirement of the group.

A lizard and a snake differ in limb presence but share the dry scaly covering. A frog, by contrast, has moist skin and lays jelly-covered eggs in water.

### Birds

Birds have feathers and wings.

Their forelimbs form the wings, and they lay eggs with hard shells. Flight ability varies, so flight itself is not a reliable defining feature.

An ostrich is a bird although it does not fly: it still has feathers and wings. A bat has wings but no feathers, so feathers identify a bird. A beak does not, because turtles and octopuses also have beaks.

### Mammals

Every mammal has hair or fur at some stage of life, and females have mammary glands that produce milk.

Visible external features include hair or fur, external ears (pinnae) and whiskers. Milk and live birth are not visible external features.

A whale is a mammal despite living in water: it has sparse hair and feeds its young on milk. Limbs and habitat vary between mammals.

### Observing and drawing local organisms

Observe a locally available organism carefully and select diagnostic features that can actually be seen, recording its shape and relative proportions.

Make a large clear line drawing with accurate labels ending on visible structures. Draw what the organism looks like, not an idealised version remembered from a book.

Handle living animals gently and return them to where they were found.

Take a sharp pencil and draw a continuous single-line outline with no shading. Check proportions by comparing lengths, for example the abdomen of an ant against its thorax. Add label lines with a ruler: they should be straight, should not cross and should end exactly on the structure. Write labels at the side in horizontal text. Give the drawing a title naming the organism. Label features that show the group, such as the three pairs of legs of an insect. You may instead be asked for adaptive features: label each with its use, such as the thick waxy cuticle that cuts water loss in a desert plant, or a beetle’s hard wing cases that protect its body.
