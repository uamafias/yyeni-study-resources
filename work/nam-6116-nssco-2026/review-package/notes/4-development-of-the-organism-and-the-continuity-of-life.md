## 4.1 Mitosis and meiosis

### Key points

| Division | Chromosome outcome | Genetic result and role |
|---|---|---|
| Mitosis | number kept the same | identical nuclei; growth, repair, replacement |
| Meiosis | diploid to haploid | different combinations of chromosomes; makes gametes |

Mitosis copies the chromosomes and shares the copies equally. Meiosis halves the number, so that fertilisation can restore it. Use the chromosome count, and not the size of the cells, to tell the two divisions apart. A root-tip squash, stained and spread thinly, shows dividing cells, and a good drawing records only what is seen.

### Mitosis

Mitosis is nuclear division, giving rise to genetically identical cells.

Before mitosis the chromosomes are copied; the copies are then separated so that the daughter nuclei carry the same genetic information as the parent nucleus.

A chromosome is a DNA-containing structure carrying genes, the instructions for making proteins. A normal diploid body nucleus has two sets of chromosomes, and mitosis keeps that number instead of halving it.

A growing root gets longer for two reasons: its cells increase in number, and the new cells then enlarge. Mitosis explains the first reason only. A cell that is simply getting bigger is not dividing.

You do not need to learn the named stages of mitosis. You do need to be able to say what goes in (one nucleus with a full set of chromosomes) and what comes out (two nuclei, each with the same full set). The terms chromosome, haploid and diploid are defined fully in 4.4.1.

### Copying and separating chromosomes

Chromosomes are duplicated exactly before mitotic nuclear division. Each daughter nucleus receives a complete set of the copies.

In ordinary growth the daughter nuclei keep the parent nucleus’s chromosome number and genetic information, apart from the rare new mutation.

If a body nucleus has 12 chromosomes, the two nuclei produced by mitosis each have 12, not six. Copying the DNA does not double the number of chromosomes in each daughter nucleus, because the copies are shared out equally between the two.

The cell usually divides too, so that each nucleus ends up in its own cell. Mitosis can be described as a short sequence: the chromosomes are copied, the copies are separated into two sets, and each set forms the nucleus of a daughter cell.

The key test of understanding is the comparison between the starting nucleus and the finishing nuclei: same number of chromosomes, same genes, same alleles.

### Where mitosis is useful

Mitosis supplies genetically identical cells for growth, repair and replacement of damaged or worn cells.

Mitosis is also the basis of asexual reproduction in many organisms with a nucleus, because the offspring keep the parent’s genetic information.

A healing cut needs replacement skin cells, a growing seedling needs more cells in its shoot and root, and the lining of the gut loses cells all the time and replaces them. In each case a cell divides to make two identical cells.

In asexual reproduction, mitosis produces the new individual from the parent’s cells, for example a new plant growing from a part of the parent or a new yeast cell budding off.

Bacteria divide by binary fission, not mitosis, because they have no nucleus. This is explained in 4.2.1.

### Reading root-tip photomicrographs

The root tip contains a region of small, closely packed cells that are actively dividing. In a photomicrograph the nuclear material of these cells can be seen in different arrangements, depending on the moment at which each cell was preserved.

A conclusion should rest on what can be seen, such as one nucleus compared with two separating groups of chromosomes in a single cell, and not on cell size alone.

Start at low power to find the region just behind the tip, where the cells are small and square. Then choose a clear field and look more closely. Cells that are not dividing show one rounded nucleus. Dividing cells show dark, thread-like chromosomes that are visible as separate structures, or two clusters of chromosomes being pulled to opposite ends of the cell.

A photomicrograph is a snapshot of many cells, each caught at a different moment. It does not show one cell changing over time. Counting the dividing cells in several fields gives a better picture of how much division is happening than looking at one field only, because dividing cells are not spread evenly.

If cells overlap, their nuclei cannot be told apart. This is why the slide must be a thin, single layer of cells.

### Preparing a root-tip squash

A root-tip squash is a thin layer of cells in which light can pass through each cell. After staining, the chromosomes can be distinguished from the rest of the cell.

The usual steps are to cut a short piece of growing root tip, soften it, stain it, lower a coverslip and press down gently, without sliding, to spread the cells.

The standard method has these steps. Cut about 2 mm from the end of a growing root. Soften the tissue by warming it for a few minutes in dilute hydrochloric acid, which loosens the cells from each other; the teacher supervises this step. Rinse the tip, place it on a slide and add a drop of stain such as acetic orcein, which colours the chromosomes dark. Lower a coverslip at an angle to avoid trapping air bubbles, cover it with filter paper and press gently straight down with a thumb. Do not slide the coverslip, which would drag and tear the cells.

Begin microscopy at low power, then increase magnification. Stain adds contrast, and spreading the cells reduces overlap. A thick lump of tissue or air bubbles make the slide difficult to read.

Wear eye protection, handle acid and stain as directed and take care with glass.

### Meiosis

Meiosis is a reduction division in which the chromosome number is halved from diploid to haploid, resulting in genetically different cells.

The resulting haploid cells each carry one chromosome set; fertilisation restores the diploid number by joining two gamete nuclei.

For an organism with a diploid number of 12, the haploid number is six. The word reduction refers to the number of chromosomes, not to the size of the cell.

The cells produced are genetically different because each gets a different mixture of the maternal and paternal chromosomes that the parent cell contained. A chromosome from the mother and the matching chromosome from the father carry the same genes, but may carry different alleles, and these are separated during meiosis. These terms are defined in 4.4.1.

You do not need the stages of meiosis, only its outcome. In humans, meiosis happens in the testes and ovaries.

### Drawing and interpreting meiosis slides

Prepared slides of reproductive tissue, such as the testis of an animal or the anthers of a flower, show cells in which the nucleus is dividing to halve the chromosome number.

A biological drawing records the cell outlines and nuclear material that can actually be seen, in their real proportions, without adding textbook details.

Good biological drawings follow set rules. Use a sharp pencil and clear, continuous lines, with no shading and no colouring. Draw large enough to show the detail, usually using at least half the page. Keep the proportions of the specimen. Label by drawing straight lines with a ruler that end on the structure being named, and keep the lines from crossing. Give the drawing a title naming the specimen and the view, and add the magnification.

Draw only what can be seen. If a low-power view does not let you count chromosomes, do not invent a number. An observation (what is visible) is kept separate from an interpretation (what it means, such as a cell having begun meiosis because its chromosomes are in two clusters).

### Meiosis and gametes

Meiosis is involved in producing haploid gametes in humans and other animals.

Fusion of haploid nuclei at fertilisation restores two sets of chromosomes; without a reduction division, the chromosome number would double in every generation.

Human body cells have 46 chromosomes. Sperm and egg cells, made by meiosis, each have 23. When a sperm nucleus fuses with an egg nucleus the zygote has 46 again, and every cell formed from it by mitosis has 46 as well.

So the sequence is: meiosis halves the number to make gametes, fertilisation joins two gametes and restores the number, and mitosis then makes the cells for growth.

Without meiosis, a zygote would have 92 chromosomes, and the number would keep doubling.

### New chromosome combinations

Meiosis produces new combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes in the haploid nuclei.

Different combinations carry different sets of alleles, which contributes to inherited variation between offspring after fertilisation.

Maternal means inherited from the mother and paternal means inherited from the father. A human body cell contains 23 chromosomes from each parent. When meiosis makes a gamete, it puts one chromosome from each pair into the gamete, but which one is chosen is a matter of chance for each pair.

So a gamete does not contain all the maternal chromosomes, or all the paternal ones. It contains a mixture. With 23 pairs, the number of possible mixtures is very large, so no two gametes from one person are likely to be the same.

Fertilisation then joins two gametes at random, which combines two different mixtures again. Meiosis has no aim of making useful characteristics; it simply makes new combinations.

### Mitosis and meiosis compared

Mitosis keeps the chromosome number and gives genetically identical daughter nuclei. Meiosis halves the chromosome number and gives genetically different haploid nuclei.

Mitosis is used for growth and repair, whereas meiosis is used to make gametes for sexual reproduction.

A comparison of the two divisions is best organised by outcome.

Mitosis: one diploid nucleus gives two diploid nuclei; the nuclei are identical; it happens in body tissue throughout life.

Meiosis: one diploid nucleus gives four haploid nuclei in the usual animal pattern; the nuclei are different from each other; it happens only in the cells that make gametes.

If a starting nucleus with eight chromosomes gives nuclei with eight, the division is mitosis. If it gives nuclei with four, the division is meiosis. Use the chromosome count, not the word growth or the size of the cells, to decide. Both processes copy the DNA before the division, so copying cannot be what distinguishes them; what differs is how many divisions follow and what each daughter nucleus receives.

### Where this connects

## 4.2.1 Asexual and sexual reproduction

### Key points

Asexual reproduction uses one parent without gametes and gives genetically identical offspring. Sexual reproduction fuses the nuclei of two gametes to form a zygote, and gives offspring that differ from each other. Fast multiplication and variation have different advantages in different conditions. Binary fission in bacteria, spores in fungi and tubers in potatoes are three examples of asexual reproduction.

### Asexual reproduction

Asexual reproduction is a process resulting in the production of genetically identical offspring from one parent.

No gametes fuse; the offspring are clones of the parent, apart from the rare mutation.

A potato tuber that grows into a new plant uses material from one parent only, and the new plant has the same genes as the parent. Genetically identical offspring can still look different. Two clones given different amounts of water or light will grow to different sizes, because the environment affects phenotype as well as genes.

Self-pollination is not asexual reproduction. In self-pollination pollen still fertilises an ovule, so two gametes fuse and the offspring can differ from the parent. The fusion of gametes, not the result, makes it sexual reproduction. Asexual reproduction needs no gametes at all.

Most asexual reproduction depends on mitosis (4.1). Bacteria use binary fission instead, described next.

### Bacterial binary fission

In binary fission the DNA of a bacterium is copied and the cell divides into two cells, each receiving a copy of the DNA.

The two offspring are genetically identical apart from mutations. This is asexual reproduction, because no gametes fuse.

Under favourable conditions of warmth and food, a bacterium can divide about once every 20 minutes. One cell becomes two, then four, then eight, so the population grows very rapidly. This is why a small number of bacteria in food can make a person ill within hours.

Bacteria do not have a nucleus, so mitosis, which is nuclear division, does not describe what they do. Growth cannot go on for ever: food runs out, waste products build up and the dividing slows or stops.

### Asexual spores in fungi

A fungus such as bread mould makes large numbers of tiny asexual spores that are released and dispersed.

A spore that lands in suitable conditions germinates and grows hyphae, forming a new fungus without any fusion of gametes.

The mycelium of a mould is made of thread-like hyphae. Some of them grow upwards and make a spore case at the tip. When the case bursts, the spores are blown away in the air. Each spore is a single cell that can grow into a new mould.

Most spores never find the warmth, moisture and food that they need. A mould therefore makes huge numbers of them, which raises the chance that some will land somewhere suitable. Spores are not seeds, since they contain no embryo and no food store.

Many fungi also reproduce sexually, but asexual spore production is the method to know here.

### Potato tubers

A potato tuber is a swollen underground stem that stores food as starch. It carries buds, called eyes.

A bud can grow into a shoot with roots, using the stored food until the new plant can make its own food by photosynthesis. This is asexual reproduction.

In a potato plant the leaves make food by photosynthesis, and the food is moved to underground stems, where it is stored as starch. These stems swell to form tubers. At the end of the growing season the above-ground parts die, but the tubers survive in the soil.

When conditions improve, a bud on a tuber starts to grow. The stored starch supplies the energy and materials for the new shoot and roots, until the shoot has leaves above the soil. The new plant is genetically identical to the parent plant.

Farmers plant pieces of tuber that each carry at least one bud. A tuber is a stem and not a seed; a seed forms after fertilisation. Because the plants are clones, a disease carried in the tuber will pass to every new plant.

### Advantages and disadvantages of asexual reproduction

Asexual reproduction can increase numbers quickly without the need to find a mate, and can keep a useful genotype.

Low genetic variation leaves all the offspring open to the same new disease or change in the environment.

Take an advantage and a disadvantage together. A farmer who clones a high-yielding plant gets a whole field of high-yielding plants quickly. But if a disease arrives to which that genotype is not resistant, every plant in the field is equally vulnerable, because they are all the same.

Low variation does not always mean failure. In a stable environment a well-suited clone can do very well for many generations. A good discussion therefore finishes with a judgement: asexual reproduction suits stable conditions where fast multiplication matters, and it is risky where the environment changes or diseases spread.

Copying a parent that is infected also copies the infection into the offspring, as with a diseased tuber.

### Sexual reproduction

Sexual reproduction is a process involving the fusion of the nuclei of two gametes (sex cells) to form a zygote and the production of offspring that are genetically different from each other.

The two gametes can come from different parents or, in self-fertilising organisms, from the same parent; the fusion of nuclei is the defining event.

Genetic differences come from two sources: meiosis, which makes gametes with new combinations of chromosomes, and fertilisation, which joins two gametes at random. A zygote is the cell formed by fusion of gamete nuclei. It divides by mitosis to form the new organism.

Sexual reproduction is not defined as needing two parents, because many plants can fertilise themselves. What matters is that two gametes fuse.

### Fertilisation

Fertilisation is the fusion of the nuclei of a male gamete and a female gamete.

In a typical diploid life cycle, the fusion of two haploid gamete nuclei forms a diploid zygote nucleus.

Pollination is the transfer of pollen to a stigma, and it comes before fertilisation. A pollen grain landing on a stigma, or a sperm reaching an egg, does not by itself show that fertilisation has happened. Fertilisation is complete only when the two nuclei have fused.

In humans, a sperm nucleus fuses with the nucleus of an egg cell. In flowering plants, a male nucleus from the pollen tube fuses with the nucleus of the egg cell in the ovule.

### Advantages and disadvantages of sexual reproduction

Sexual reproduction produces new combinations of inherited features, which can help a population to survive changes in the environment.

Making gametes, achieving fertilisation and often finding a mate need time and energy, so numbers increase more slowly than by asexual reproduction.

Variation helps a population, not each individual. If conditions change, some offspring may happen to carry features that suit the new conditions and they survive to breed. A population of clones has no such variety to draw on.

A well-suited parent does not pass on its whole genotype unchanged, because meiosis shuffles the alleles. A successful judgement says which advantage matters more in the situation: fast multiplication, or variety that provides resilience.

For example, a crop population grown from seed contains plants with slightly different tolerance of drought, so a dry year is unlikely to kill all of them.

## 4.2.2 Pollination, fertilisation and seed dispersal

### Key points

| Event | Material and site |
|---|---|
| Pollination | pollen moves from anther to stigma |
| Fertilisation | male and female nuclei fuse in the ovule |
| Dispersal | seeds or fruits move away from the parent |

Self-pollination stays within one plant, and cross-pollination reaches a different plant of the same species. Insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated flowers have different adaptations. A pollen tube carries the male nucleus to the ovule. Dispersal reduces competition and helps a species to spread.

### Self-pollination

Self-pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther of a flower to the stigma of the same flower or a different flower on the same plant.

The pollen stays within one plant. Fertilisation that follows still involves the fusion of gamete nuclei.

What decides whether pollination is self or cross is the identity of the plant, not the number of flowers. Pollen carried from one flower to another flower on the same plant is still self-pollination.

A pollen grain reaching a stigma does not mean a seed will form. The pollen must still grow a tube, and a male nucleus must then fertilise the egg in an ovule.

Self-pollination suits plants that grow alone or that live where few pollinators visit. Many crop plants, such as some beans, regularly self-pollinate.

### Cross-pollination

Cross-pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther of a flower to the stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.

It allows the genes of two different plants to be combined, if fertilisation follows.

The receiving plant must belong to the same species. Pollen blown onto a plant of another species cannot lead to fertilisation and seed formation.

The pollen is carried by an agent such as wind, insects or birds. The flowers of the plant are adapted to the agent. Wind-pollinated plants, such as maize and mahangu (pearl millet), shed pollen into the air. Many flowers of wild plants in Namibia depend on bees and other insects.

### Insect-pollinated flowers

An insect-pollinated flower has petals that attract insects, and nectar as a reward for visiting. Its anthers produce pollen that sticks to the insect’s body, and its stigma receives pollen.

The ovary contains the ovules, and the style connects the stigma to the ovary. The sepals protect the flower while it is a bud; they are not the main attraction.

Each part has a job. Large, brightly coloured petals, and sometimes scent, show the insect where to go. Nectar rewards the visit. The anthers and stigma are positioned where the insect will brush against them as it feeds, so that pollen is picked up on its body and left on the next flower. Pollen grains are often sticky or spiky, which helps them to cling to the insect. The stigma is often sticky as well, so that arriving grains stay on it.

Flowers vary in colour and shape, so the best answers pair a feature with its function, such as: petals are large and coloured, to attract insects. Use a labelled diagram of a flower to practise locating each part.

### Wind-pollinated flowers

The anthers of a wind-pollinated flower hang outside the flower and release large amounts of small, light pollen. The stigmas are large, feathery and exposed, so that they catch pollen from the air.

Wind-pollinated flowers usually have small, dull petals, and make no nectar, because they have no need to attract insects.

Wind carries pollen in all directions, so most grains never reach another flower of the same species. The plant makes up for this by producing a very large number of pollen grains. Light, smooth pollen travels further in the air than sticky pollen, which would clump together and cling to surfaces. Long, hanging filaments let the anthers swing in the wind, and a large feathery stigma gives a big surface to catch passing grains.

Grasses, including the cereals maize and mahangu, are wind-pollinated. Their flowers are small and green, with exposed anthers and stigmas. Compare this with an insect-pollinated flower, which has bright petals, nectar and sticky pollen. Several features together, not one, are the evidence for a pollination method.

### Drawing anthers and stigmas

A hand lens shows the shape and surface of the anthers and stigma of a flower.

A biological drawing keeps the proportions of what is seen, and labels the anther and the stigma with clear lines.

Look at the whole flower first, so that you know where each part sits. Then remove the petals and sepals carefully, using a mounted needle or forceps, to see the anthers and the stigma.

When you draw, use clear single lines and no shading or colour. Draw large, and keep the sizes of the anther and stigma in proportion. Label the anther at the part that holds the pollen, and the stigma at the top of the carpel, with ruled lines that end on the structure and do not cross. Add a title and a note of the magnification.

The stigma is usually sticky or feathery and the anther is a small pod that splits open to release pollen. Draw them as you see them, not as you remember them from a textbook.

### Comparing wind- and insect-pollinated flowers

Putting drawings of wind-pollinated and insect-pollinated flowers side by side lets you compare the position and shape of anthers, stigmas and petals.

A fair comparison needs drawings made from similar views, each with its scale, so that real differences can be separated from differences caused by viewing angle or magnification.

In the wind-pollinated flower the anthers hang out of the flower on long filaments and the stigmas are large and feathery and also outside the flower. In the insect-pollinated flower the anthers and stigma are inside or among large petals, and the stigma is smaller and sticky.

A drawing that is larger does not show that the real structure is larger. Record the magnification of each drawing, or the actual size, before comparing. A side view can hide a part that a view from above shows, so draw both flowers from the same direction.

When you interpret the drawings, link each difference to the pollination method: exposed structures suit wind, and structures positioned where an insect brushes against them suit insects.

### Agents of pollination

Wind and animals, especially insects, can transfer pollen from anthers to stigmas.

An agent carries the pollen. The pollen is the material that is transferred and is not an agent.

Bees, butterflies, moths and beetles are insect agents. Birds such as sunbirds can also pollinate flowers, and in some places bats do. Air currents are the agent for wind-pollinated plants.

A bee collecting nectar carries pollen on its body from the anthers of one flower to the stigma of the next. A visitor that takes nectar without touching the anthers and stigma does not pollinate the flower.

Water can carry pollen in some water plants, but wind and insects are the agents to know first.

### Self-pollination compared with cross-pollination

Self-pollination does not depend on pollen being carried between different plants, but it gives less variation than cross-pollination.

Cross-pollination can increase variation, but it depends on pollen reaching another plant of the same species, by a suitable agent.

Both routes lead to sexual reproduction, with the fusion of gametes. They differ in where the pollen comes from.

Self-pollination: the advantage is reliability, since seeds can form even if there are no pollinators or no other plants nearby. The disadvantage is that offspring have alleles from only one parent plant, so there is less variation. This is useful in a stable environment, but makes the plants less able to cope with change.

Cross-pollination: the advantage is more variation, because the alleles of two plants are combined, so a few offspring are likely to suit new conditions. The disadvantage is that the plant depends on wind or animals to carry the pollen. If the pollinators decline, fewer seeds form.

A good discussion ends with a judgement about which suits a given situation.

### Pollen tube growth and fertilisation

A pollen grain that lands on a compatible stigma grows a pollen tube, which grows down the style towards an ovule.

A male gamete nucleus travels down the tube and enters the ovule, where it fuses with the nucleus of the female gamete to form a zygote.

The sequence is: the pollen grain lands on the stigma; it germinates and a tube grows out; the tube grows down through the style and reaches the ovule; the male nucleus travels along the tube and enters the ovule; the male nucleus fuses with the egg nucleus, which is fertilisation. The zygote then divides to form the embryo in the seed.

The whole grain does not go down the style; only the tube grows, and it carries the male nucleus. Pollination and fertilisation are different events. Pollination is the transfer of the grain to the stigma, and fertilisation takes place later, inside the ovule, after the tube has grown.

The ovule becomes the seed after fertilisation, and the ovary becomes the fruit. Details of how the seed develops further are not needed.

### Investigating pollen germination

Pollen can be placed in sugar solutions of different concentrations and viewed under a microscope to see whether pollen tubes form.

To test the effect of sugar concentration, keep the pollen source, the incubation time, the temperature and the volume of solution the same, then count the fraction of grains that have grown tubes.

A suitable plan has these parts. The independent variable is the concentration of the sugar solution, for example 0, 5, 10, 15 and 20 per cent. The dependent variable is the percentage of grains with a pollen tube, found by counting a fixed number of grains in several fields of view. Use a clear rule for what counts as a tube, such as a tube longer than the width of the grain. Keep the same pollen, the same volume of solution, the same time and the same temperature. Repeat each concentration with separate preparations and calculate a mean.

Count the fields in a consistent way, without choosing the best-looking one. Sugar supplies a source for respiration, but a very concentrated solution draws water out of the grain by osmosis, so more sugar does not always mean more germination. The best concentration varies between species.

### Why seeds are dispersed

Dispersal carries seeds away from the parent plant, which reduces competition for light, water, mineral ions and space.

It can also allow seeds to reach new sites where the plant can grow, although many dispersed seeds do not survive.

Dispersal is different from pollination. Pollination moves pollen before fertilisation; dispersal moves the seeds or fruits after fertilisation.

Seeds that fall beneath the parent plant compete with it and with each other. A seedling in the shade of its parent receives little light, so it photosynthesises slowly and grows poorly. A seed carried further away has more chance of finding light and water. Dispersal also helps a species to spread into new areas, for example after a fire or flood has cleared the ground.

Plants use different agents. The fruit of the devil’s claw, a plant of the Namibian and Kalahari sands, has hooks that catch on the feet and fur of animals, which carry it away. Some seeds are carried by wind, some by water and some are eaten and then passed out by animals.

Farther is not necessarily better. A seed that lands on bare rock or a road cannot grow, so the benefit depends on reaching a suitable place.

## 4.3.1 Hormones in the menstrual cycle and pregnancy

### Key points

| Hormone | Main role in the cycle |
|---|---|
| FSH | makes a follicle develop, and the follicle makes oestrogen |
| LH | surge causes ovulation and the formation of the corpus luteum |
| Oestrogen | builds the uterus lining; high level stimulates the LH surge |
| Progesterone | maintains the lining after ovulation |

The ovaries make oestrogen and progesterone in the cycle, and the placenta makes them in pregnancy. The place where a hormone is made is not the same as the target where it acts.

### Where oestrogen and progesterone are made

Oestrogen is made by the follicles in the ovary, and progesterone is made by the corpus luteum after ovulation.

During pregnancy the placenta becomes an important source of both hormones, and they help to maintain the pregnancy.

A follicle is a fluid-filled structure in the ovary that surrounds a developing egg. After the egg is released, the remaining follicle tissue becomes the corpus luteum, which makes progesterone and some oestrogen.

It helps to separate where a hormone is made from where it acts. The ovary makes the hormone, and the target, such as the lining of the uterus, responds to it. The pituitary gland, at the base of the brain, makes FSH and LH, which act on the ovary.

### FSH

FSH from the pituitary gland stimulates the development of a follicle in the ovary, and the follicle makes oestrogen.

Oestrogen in turn helps to control the release of FSH, linking the ovary with the pituitary.

FSH is released early in the cycle. It makes one or more follicles grow and the egg inside begins to mature. As the follicle grows, it makes more oestrogen, and this has two effects: it helps to repair the lining of the uterus, and it signals back to the pituitary.

Do not mix up FSH with LH. FSH prepares an egg for release; the LH surge releases it. If FSH is suppressed, follicles do not mature and no egg is ready to be released.

### LH

A surge of LH from the pituitary gland causes ovulation and the formation of the corpus luteum.

The corpus luteum makes progesterone, which keeps the lining of the uterus in place after ovulation.

Ovulation is the release of an egg from an ovary. It is not the same as menstruation, which is the loss of the uterus lining, and it is not fertilisation. The LH surge happens in the middle of the cycle, around day 14 in a 28-day cycle. A surge means a short peak, and LH does not stay high for the rest of the cycle.

### Oestrogen

Oestrogen causes the lining of the uterus to build up again after menstruation, and it affects the release of hormones from the pituitary gland.

When its level is high before ovulation, oestrogen stimulates the LH surge. It also helps to control FSH, which keeps follicle development in step.

The effect of oestrogen depends on its level and on the point in the cycle. A rising level builds the lining, and a high level just before ovulation stimulates the pituitary to release the LH surge. During pregnancy the placenta makes oestrogen, which helps to maintain the conditions needed for the fetus.

This is a feedback system. Oestrogen made by the follicle affects the pituitary, and the pituitary hormones in turn control the ovary.

### Progesterone

Progesterone keeps the lining of the uterus in place after ovulation and during pregnancy.

It also reduces the release of FSH and LH. If there is no pregnancy, progesterone falls and the lining breaks down at menstruation.

After ovulation the corpus luteum makes progesterone. The lining stays thick and full of blood vessels, ready to receive an embryo. If an embryo does not implant, the corpus luteum breaks down, the level of progesterone falls and the lining is lost. This is menstruation.

If the woman becomes pregnant, progesterone stays high. At first the corpus luteum makes it, and later the placenta does. The high level keeps the lining in place and stops new follicles developing.

## 4.3.2 Human reproduction, pregnancy and birth

### Key points

The sperm and egg nuclei fuse in an oviduct, and the embryo implants in the uterus. The placenta exchanges oxygen, nutrients, carbon dioxide and urea between separate maternal and fetal blood supplies, and the cord carries the fetal blood. Early development increases complexity, and later development increases size. In labour the uterus contracts, the cervix widens, the baby is delivered and the afterbirth follows. Breast milk and formula milk each have advantages and disadvantages.

### Male reproductive system

The testes make sperm and the hormone testosterone. Sperm travel along the sperm ducts to the urethra, which passes through the penis.

The prostate gland and the seminal vesicles add fluids to the sperm to make semen. The penis places semen in the vagina, and the scrotum holds the testes outside the body.

Trace the route of a sperm: testis, sperm duct, urethra, out through the penis. The urethra also carries urine from the bladder, but at different times. The sperm duct is a different tube from the ureter, which carries urine from the kidney to the bladder.

The fluids from the glands give the sperm a liquid to swim in and a supply of nutrients. Semen is therefore a mixture of sperm and fluid, not sperm alone. The scrotum keeps the testes slightly cooler than the rest of the body, which suits sperm production.

In a diagram, each part is identified by its position and shape, so practise with unlabelled diagrams from different angles.

### Female reproductive system

The ovaries make egg cells and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone. The oviducts carry the egg and are where fertilisation usually happens.

The uterus is where the embryo implants and develops. The cervix is the ring of muscle at its lower end, and the vagina receives the penis and is the birth canal.

Follow the path: an ovary releases an egg into the funnel of an oviduct; cilia and muscle move it along; if it is fertilised, the embryo travels to the uterus and implants in its lining. The ovary is not joined directly to the oviduct; the egg crosses a short gap into its funnel.

The cervix holds the baby in during pregnancy and widens during labour. The vagina is a muscular tube leading to the outside.

Implantation happens after fertilisation, about a week later, and is when the embryo attaches to the lining. Practise on unlabelled diagrams, and name a part from its position and shape.

### Human fertilisation

In humans, the nucleus of a sperm fuses with the nucleus of an egg cell, normally in an oviduct, to form a zygote.

The zygote has a set of chromosomes from each gamete and begins to divide by mitosis.

Fertilisation is not the same as intercourse, ovulation or implantation. Each is a separate event in a different place.

A sperm swims to the egg and enters it. The two nuclei then fuse. Each nucleus is haploid, with 23 chromosomes, so the zygote is diploid, with 46. The zygote divides by mitosis to form a ball of cells, which moves along the oviduct and implants in the lining of the uterus.

When defining fertilisation, name the nuclei: it is the nuclei that fuse.

### Placenta and umbilical cord

The placenta is an exchange surface between the blood of the mother and the blood of the fetus. Oxygen and dissolved nutrients pass to the fetus, and carbon dioxide and urea pass to the mother.

The umbilical cord contains blood vessels that carry blood between the fetus and the placenta. The two blood supplies are normally kept separate and do not mix.

Exchange happens by diffusion across thin walls between the two blood supplies. The placenta has a large area and a short diffusion distance, which make exchange efficient. Maternal blood fills spaces in the placenta, and fetal blood runs through villi that project into them.

The fetus makes carbon dioxide and urea but cannot remove them with its own lungs and kidneys. They diffuse into the mother’s blood in the placenta, and her lungs and kidneys remove them.

The placenta is not a complete barrier. Some harmful substances, including alcohol, nicotine and some viruses, can cross it, which is why a pregnant woman is advised to avoid them.

### Growth of the fetus

In the early weeks after fertilisation the embryo becomes more complex, as cells divide and specialise to form tissues and organs. Later in pregnancy the fetus mainly increases in size.

A developing human is called an embryo in the early stages and a fetus later on. Neither word describes an unfertilised egg or a sperm.

The zygote divides by mitosis to form a ball of cells. The cells then become specialised, a process called differentiation, and form tissues such as muscle and nerve, and then organs such as the heart. The organs are formed early. This is an increase in complexity.

In the later months, the organs are present and the main change is growth: the fetus gets longer and heavier. A fetus with the same organs as a baby is simply smaller.

The two phases overlap, so there is no sudden switch between them. Mitosis provides more cells for both.

### Labour and birth

During labour the muscles of the uterus contract and the cervix widens. Stronger contractions push the baby through the cervix and the vagina.

After the baby is born, further contractions expel the placenta, which is called the afterbirth.

Labour begins with contractions of the muscular wall of the uterus, and the cervix gradually dilates (opens). The amniotic sac around the baby breaks and releases fluid, often called the waters breaking. The contractions become stronger and more frequent until the baby is pushed out, usually head first through the cervix and vagina.

The umbilical cord is clamped and cut, and the baby then breathes air. Finally the uterus contracts again to expel the placenta.

The uterus gives the expulsive force; the cervix is a ring of muscle that opens, and does not squeeze.

### Breast milk and formula milk

Breast milk contains nutrients and antibodies from the mother, which give the baby some protection against infection.

Formula milk contains no antibodies. It has to be made up with the right formula, clean water and clean, sterilised bottles.

This section compares the two milks. Here breast-feeding means giving a baby breast milk and bottle-feeding means giving formula. How the milk is given is a separate matter: a bottle can hold formula or expressed breast milk, and anyone can give a bottle, so that is not a difference between the milks.

Advantages of breast milk: it has the right balance of nutrients for the baby, it contains antibodies, it is free, and it does not have to be made up with water, so it does not carry the risk of unsafe water (expressed milk still needs clean containers).

Advantages of formula milk: it can be used if the mother cannot produce enough milk or a health worker advises her not to breast-feed. Its disadvantages are the cost, the lack of antibodies, and the risk of diarrhoea if the water or equipment is not clean.

The antibodies in the milk are made by the mother and the baby only receives them temporarily; the baby does not produce its own memory cells from them.

Where clean water is not easy to obtain, breast milk carries less risk of infection. The best choice depends on the health of mother and baby; health workers can advise.

## 4.3.3 Birth control and fertility treatment

### Key points

Birth control works by avoiding intercourse, by fertility awareness, by hormones, by barriers or by cutting reproductive tubes. A copper IUD harms sperm and releases no hormone. Fertility treatment can stimulate ovulation, but it cannot overcome every cause of infertility. Access, cost, consent and beliefs affect how people decide, and a health worker can give advice on an individual choice.

### Natural methods of birth control

Abstinence means not having sexual intercourse, so that no sperm can reach an egg. Other natural methods monitor body temperature and cervical mucus to estimate when the woman is fertile.

Using these signs depends on interpreting them correctly and avoiding intercourse in the fertile period. Cycles vary, so predictions are not always right.

A woman’s body temperature rises slightly after ovulation, so a record kept over several months helps to show when ovulation usually happens. The cervical mucus becomes clear, slippery and stretchy around ovulation, which is a sign that she is fertile.

These methods are less reliable than many others, because ovulation can be early or late in a given cycle. Abstinence is the most reliable method, and it also prevents infection by sexually transmitted infections.

### Hormonal and device methods of birth control

The contraceptive pill, an implant and an injection supply hormones that stop ovulation or change the cervical mucus. An IUD is placed in the uterus.

A copper IUD releases no hormone. The copper is toxic to sperm, so fertilisation does not take place.

The implant is a small rod placed under the skin of the arm, and it releases hormone for several years. The hormones prevent the release of eggs, and also thicken the cervical mucus so that sperm cannot easily pass through.

The copper IUD is a small device placed in the uterus by a nurse or doctor, and it can stay in place for several years. None of these methods protects against sexually transmitted infections.

### Barrier methods of birth control

The condom and the femidom (internal condom) and the diaphragm are physical barriers that stop sperm from reaching the egg.

Condoms also reduce the risk of passing on many sexually transmitted infections. A diaphragm does not give the same protection.

The condom is a thin sheath that fits over the erect penis and holds the semen. The femidom is a loose pouch that lines the vagina. The diaphragm is a dome of rubber that covers the cervix.

Because a condom prevents the exchange of body fluids, it also reduces the spread of HIV.

### Surgical methods of birth control

Vasectomy cuts and seals the sperm ducts, so that sperm cannot join the semen. In tubal ligation the oviducts are cut and tied, so that sperm and egg cannot meet.

Neither operation removes the testes or ovaries, and neither is a way to protect against sexually transmitted infections.

After a vasectomy the testes go on making sperm and testosterone, but the sperm cannot travel along the cut ducts, so the semen contains no sperm. After tubal ligation the ovaries still release eggs and make hormones, but the egg cannot travel down the blocked oviduct to meet sperm.

These operations are meant to be permanent, and neither protects against infections such as HIV.

### Hormones in contraception

The contraceptive pill contains hormones such as oestrogen and progesterone, or progesterone alone. They act on the pituitary gland to reduce FSH and LH.

With less FSH and LH, follicles do not mature and ovulation does not happen, so there is no egg to be fertilised.

Progesterone and oestrogen naturally lower FSH, and a pill supplies them from outside at a steady level. FSH is then too low to make a follicle mature, and there is no LH surge, so the woman does not ovulate.

An egg that is not released cannot be fertilised, so this method acts earlier than a condom, which stops the sperm.

### Hormones in fertility treatment

Some women do not release eggs. Under medical supervision, a hormone such as FSH can be given to stimulate the follicles to develop, and a hormone may be given to trigger ovulation.

Treatment can increase the chance of conception in some cases, but it does not guarantee a pregnancy and can cause multiple pregnancy.

FSH is given to make follicles mature, and LH or a similar hormone is given to trigger the release of the egg.

The treatment helps only if the cause of infertility is a failure to ovulate. If the oviducts are blocked, or the man makes too few sperm, stimulating the ovaries does not solve the problem. A risk is that several eggs may be released at once, which can lead to twins or triplets.

### Social aspects of contraception and fertility treatment

Access to contraception allows people to plan the number of children and when to have them. Cost, availability and the cultural or religious beliefs of a community affect whether people use it.

Fertility treatment helps some people to have children, but it can be costly, it is not available to everyone and it raises ethical questions.

A good answer names a social factor and explains its effect. For example, if couples can plan the timing of births, women may be able to continue education or work, and families can better provide for each child. If treatment is expensive and only offered in a few places, fewer people can use it, so access is unequal.

Beliefs also matter. Some communities and religions oppose some methods, and people make different choices for sincere reasons.

## 4.3.4 Sexually transmitted infections and HIV

### Key points

HIV is passed on by infected body fluids, through sex, shared needles, blood and from mother to child, and not by everyday contact. Prevention blocks each route. Untreated HIV weakens the immune system, so other infections, such as TB, become more severe. Treatment and support reduce the effects on health and on families and the economy. HIV is the virus, and AIDS is the advanced stage.

### Sexually transmitted infections

A sexually transmitted infection is an infection that is transmitted via body fluids through sexual contact.

The definition concerns transmission during sexual contact. Some of these infections can also be passed on by other routes, such as blood.

HIV is a virus that can be passed on sexually. AIDS is the later, advanced stage in which the immune system has been badly damaged. HIV is the virus, and AIDS is not a separate infection.

Other sexually transmitted infections include gonorrhoea and syphilis, which are caused by bacteria and can be treated with antibiotics. An infection is a medical condition. It is not a moral judgement on the person who has it.

The next sections explain how HIV is passed on, how its spread can be prevented and what it means for health and society in Namibia.

### How HIV is transmitted

HIV can be transmitted through body fluids during sexual contact, through the sharing of needles or other equipment that carries infected blood, through infected blood transfusion, and from an infected mother to her child during pregnancy, birth or breast-feeding.

HIV is not transmitted by everyday contact, such as sharing a classroom, shaking hands, hugging or sharing a meal.

To pass on HIV there must be a fluid that carries enough virus, such as blood, semen or vaginal fluid, and a way for it to enter the body of another person, such as through the lining of the vagina, penis or rectum, or a cut or injection. Unbroken skin is a barrier to the virus.

Blood in donated transfusions is now tested for HIV, and sterile needles remove the risk from injections. Treatment during pregnancy and birth greatly reduces the chance that a mother passes on the virus to her baby.

People living with HIV pose no risk through ordinary friendship or school contact, so they should be treated with respect.

### Preventing the spread of HIV

The spread of HIV can be reduced by using sterile needles and not sharing them, by testing donated blood, and by using condoms correctly during sex.

Testing, effective antiretroviral treatment and medical care for pregnant women with HIV reduce the chance that the virus is passed on.

Match each action to the route it blocks. Sterile needles stop transmission through blood on needles. Screening donated blood stops transmission through transfusion. Condoms stop semen and vaginal fluids from passing between partners. Abstinence, and being faithful to one uninfected partner, avoid exposure through sex. Taking antiretroviral drugs in pregnancy lowers the risk to the baby.

A general answer such as be careful or keep clean does not say how a route is blocked, so earns no credit. Effective treatment keeps the amount of virus in the blood very low, which protects the person’s health and makes passing it on much less likely.

Testing is the first step in getting treatment. Clinics in Namibia offer HIV tests and counselling, and advice from a health worker is the best guidance for any individual.

### HIV and vulnerability to other illness

Untreated HIV damages the immune system, so the body cannot defend itself as well against other infections.

With treatment and good health care, a person with HIV can stay healthy, and the number of opportunistic infections falls.

HIV infects and destroys white blood cells that coordinate the immune response. As these cells decline, infections that a healthy person would fight off successfully, such as tuberculosis (TB), pneumonia and diarrhoeal disease, can take hold and become severe. These are called opportunistic infections, because they take the opportunity given by weak defences.

HIV does not cause TB. TB is caused by bacteria, and HIV makes it easier for these bacteria to cause disease. This is why TB and HIV are often treated together in southern Africa. Where HIV is common, TB and other infections are also more common among the population. More people with active TB means more TB bacteria are passed on, so even people without HIV are more exposed.

Antiretroviral treatment allows the immune system to recover, so people on treatment are far less likely to become ill. Early testing and treatment, and good food and clean water, protect health.

### Socio-economic effects of HIV and AIDS

Serious illness can reduce the income of a household, increase the need for care and put pressure on health and education services.

Prevention, treatment and support can reduce these effects, and many people living with HIV are healthy, work and look after their families.

A good explanation follows each effect through. If a wage earner is often ill, working time and income fall. If income falls, there may be less money for food, school fees and transport. If a family member must care for the sick person, they have less time for farming, work or school.

When many adults in a community are ill, children may lose a parent and be raised by grandparents or other relatives, and the demand on clinics, hospitals and orphan support increases. Namibia, like other countries in southern Africa, has had to respond to this with testing, treatment through public clinics and support for affected families.

These are possible effects, not facts about every household. Treatment reduces them, and discrimination makes them worse by keeping people from work or from seeking care. Treat people with respect and protect their privacy.

## 4.3.5 Growth, development and germination

### Key points

Growth is an increase in dry mass and size, and development is an increase in complexity. Germination needs water, a suitable temperature and oxygen. Percentage germination = number germinated ÷ number tested × 100. Choose height, fresh mass or dry mass to suit the question, since water content and harvesting affect the measurement. A growth curve is drawn from recorded values of time and mass.

### Growth

Growth is an increase in dry mass and size.

Dry mass leaves out water, so it measures the new biological material made, not a temporary change in water content.

A seed taking up water becomes heavier at once, but this is not growth. In fact the dry mass of a seed falls at first, because the stored food is used in respiration. Growth happens when the organism makes more material and increases in size.

Fresh mass and height are easy to measure but do not mean the same as dry mass. Fresh mass changes with the amount of water, and a plant in the dark can grow tall without adding much dry mass. The best measure depends on the organism and the question.

Dry mass is found by heating the sample in an oven until its mass stops changing, which removes all the water.

### Development

Development is an increase in complexity: cells become specialised and tissues and organs form.

Cell differentiation and the formation of organised tissues and organs are what make an organism more complex. A larger cell is not necessarily more complex.

The first true leaves of a seedling are an example of development: new organs with a new function have formed. A stem getting longer is an example of growth, because it is a change in size only.

Growth and development often happen together, as when a seed becomes a seedling. When explaining development, name the new structure or function that has appeared, and do not simply say that the organism became bigger.

### Conditions needed for germination

A seed needs water, a suitable temperature and oxygen to germinate. Some species have extra requirements.

Water activates the enzymes and lets stored food be moved, a suitable temperature lets enzymes work, and oxygen is needed for aerobic respiration, which releases energy for growth.

The seed takes in water and swells. The water activates enzymes that break down the stored food in the seed, and the products are moved to the embryo. The embryo respires aerobically, using oxygen, to release energy for growth. The temperature must be warm enough for the enzymes to work, but not so hot that they are denatured.

A seed in waterlogged soil may fail because water fills the air spaces and the seed gets too little oxygen. Light is not needed by most seeds, as the seedling lives on stored food at first, although a few species germinate only in light. Photosynthesis starts later, once green leaves have formed.

A seed that does not germinate is not necessarily dead. It may be dormant, or the conditions may be unsuitable. In Namibia, mahangu is sown when the first rains arrive, because the seeds need water to germinate.

### Investigating germination

A fair germination test changes one condition and keeps seed type, viability, number of seeds and the other conditions the same.

The number of seeds that meet a clear germination rule is counted after the same time. Separate repeats show how much the results vary.

Suppose we are testing temperature. Place equal numbers of seeds of one type, taken from the same batch, on equally moist paper at several temperatures, such as 10, 20, 30 and 40 °C. Keep water and light the same. Decide in advance what counts as germination, for example a root at least 2 mm long, and count after a fixed time. Calculate the percentage germination, repeat with separate samples and compare means.

Include a favourable comparison, such as 25 °C with plenty of moisture, to show that the seeds are viable. If no seeds germinate in any condition, the seeds may be dead or the method may be faulty, and no conclusion can be drawn about temperature.

Moisture must be controlled. A warm dish dries out quickly, which would add a second changing variable.

The best temperature can only be placed between the values tested. To find it more precisely, test more temperatures close to the one with the highest result.

### Percentage germination

Percentage germination = (number germinated ÷ number tested) × 100.

Percentages let samples of different sizes be compared, but a fair comparison also needs seeds, conditions and test time to match, and repeats.

If 18 of 24 seeds germinate, 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75, and 0.75 × 100 = 75, so the percentage germination is 75%. If a second sample has 30 of 50 germinating, 30 ÷ 50 = 0.60, which is 60%, so the first sample has the higher percentage even though fewer seeds were tested and fewer germinated in total.

Seeds lose viability during storage, so older seeds often show a lower percentage. A lower percentage in one batch could also be due to a different variety or a different test time, so only compare batches that were tested in the same way.

Always write down the number germinated and the number tested, as well as the percentage.

### Measuring growth in a plant

The height of a plant, the number or area of leaves and the fresh mass can all be followed without destroying the plant. Dry mass needs a harvested sample.

Repeated measurements need a fixed starting point, the same timing and a calibrated ruler or balance. Measuring dry mass destroys the plant, so separate matched plants are used on each date.

Height should be measured from the soil surface to a fixed point, such as the tip of the shoot, using a ruler, on the same day each week. This is quick and the same plant can be followed, but it ignores the roots and the thickness of the stem.

Fresh mass changes with the amount of water, so a plant that has been watered weighs more. To measure dry mass, harvest a plant, dry it in an oven at about 80 °C until its mass stops changing, cool it and weigh it. The plant is destroyed, so a different plant of the same age must be used on each date. Many plants should be sampled and a mean found.

When the results are put on a graph, show time or age on the horizontal axis, the quantity measured on the vertical axis and give units.

### Measuring growth in a mammal

Body mass and body length or height are practical measurements that do not harm the animal and can be used to follow its growth.

The methods must be the same each time, the age must be recorded and the animal handled kindly. Short-term changes in mass can reflect food or water and not growth.

Body mass is measured on a balance that has been set to zero, and the animal should be weighed at the same time of day. Length is measured between two fixed points, such as nose to base of tail, or height from the floor to a fixed point.

A child’s growth is followed in the same way, with height and mass measured at regular visits to a clinic and recorded on a chart. A single reading is not enough, because growth is shown by the change over time.

A baby’s mass can rise or fall in a day with feeding and nappies, so measurements are compared at similar times. Measurements of living animals must not cause pain, so dry mass cannot be used.

### Growth curve of an annual plant

The growth of an annual plant, from sowing to maturity, can be followed by measuring plants repeatedly or by harvesting matched samples at intervals.

A typical curve of dry mass first dips as stored reserves are used, then rises quickly once the leaves photosynthesise well, and levels off as the plant matures.

For example, matched plants might have mean dry masses of 0.40, 0.32, 0.70, 1.80 and 2.00 g at equal time intervals. The fall from 0.40 g to 0.32 g happens because the seedling respires its stored food faster than it makes new material. Once the leaves are open and photosynthesis exceeds respiration, the dry mass rises quickly, here to 1.80 g. Later the rise slows, since the plant approaches maturity and may be limited by water, light or nutrients.

To plot a growth curve, put time on the horizontal axis and mean dry mass in grams on the vertical axis. Use a scale that fills the grid, mark each point accurately, and join the points with a smooth line. The shape tells you when growth is fastest.

This is the pattern for an annual plant such as maize or mahangu.

### Choosing a way to measure growth

Dry mass shows the amount of organic material made better than fresh mass, because it is not affected by water, but it destroys the sample.

Height is quick and lets the same plant be followed over time, but it does not necessarily match the total mass or the growth of branches and roots.

Fresh mass is easy to find, but it varies with water content. Leaf area shows how much light a plant can intercept, but it leaves out stems and roots.

A plant growing in shade may stretch upwards towards the light and become tall but thin, with a low dry mass. Height alone would suggest strong growth, and the dry mass shows the truth. So the method must suit the question being asked.

A good plan combines methods: measure height on the same plants every week, and harvest matched plants at the start and the end to find dry mass. State the advantage and the limit of each method when you compare them.

## 4.4 Inheritance

### Key points

Genetic information is passed from one generation to the next through the gametes. Features acquired during life, such as damage, are not inherited, and the environment can change the phenotype without changing an allele.

### Inheritance

Inheritance is the transmission of genetic information from generation to generation.

Genes pass from parents to offspring in the gametes. Features that an organism acquires during its life, such as a scar, are not inherited.

A plant’s alleles come from its parents, whereas a leaf torn by wind is damage during life. Offspring differ from their parents and from each other because they receive different combinations of alleles, and because their environments differ. Inheritance does not copy every parental feature unchanged.

The topics that follow explain how this information is carried (chromosomes, genes and alleles, 4.4.1), how it is passed on in crosses (4.4.2), how it varies and changes (4.4.3) and how selection acts on it (4.4.4).

## 4.4.1 Chromosomes, genes and proteins

### Key points

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Chromosome | a thread-like DNA structure carrying genes |
| Gene | a length of DNA that codes for a protein |
| Allele | a version of a gene |
| Haploid, diploid | one set, two sets of chromosomes |

Homologous chromosomes carry the same genes at the same loci, and may carry the same or different alleles. The order of bases decides the order of amino acids, and proteins control cell function.

### Chromosome

A chromosome is a thread-like structure of DNA, carrying genetic information in the form of the genes.

Genes lie along a chromosome. A chromosome contains many genes, and no single gene makes up a whole chromosome.

A chromosome is a physical structure made of DNA. A gene is a short length of that DNA. Humans have 46 chromosomes in a body cell, each carrying hundreds or thousands of genes.

Chromosomes are copied and shared out during nuclear division, which links this topic to mitosis and meiosis (4.1). Each chromosome carries many genes, so use gene for a single instruction and chromosome for the structure that carries many of them.

### Homologous chromosomes

Homologous chromosomes are chromosomes having the same genes at the same loci, but not necessarily identical, as they can have different alleles.

A homologous pair has the same genes in the same positions, with one chromosome from each parent. The alleles at a position may be the same or different.

Locus means the position of a gene on a chromosome. In a pair of homologous chromosomes, each position carries the same gene, such as the gene for flower colour. One chromosome may carry the allele for purple and the other the allele for white, or both may carry the same allele.

Chromosomes that carry different genes, or that belong to different pairs, are not homologous. In a body cell the chromosomes are in homologous pairs, except that in males the X and Y chromosomes differ from each other, and one of each pair goes into each gamete.

### Haploid nucleus

A haploid nucleus is a nucleus containing a single set of unpaired chromosomes, for example in gametes.

Human gamete nuclei are haploid. They contain one chromosome from each homologous pair.

Haploid describes the number of sets of chromosomes. It does not mean half a chromosome. If the diploid number of an organism is 10, the haploid number is 5. A haploid human gamete has 23 chromosomes, and a gamete still carries genes for many features, spread across its set.

When two haploid gametes fuse, the diploid number is restored.

### Diploid nucleus

A diploid nucleus is a nucleus containing two sets of chromosomes, for example in body cells.

Human body cells have a diploid nucleus, with a maternal set and a paternal set of chromosomes.

Diploid does not mean two chromosomes. It means two sets, so if the haploid number is 7, the diploid number is 14. Each set contains one chromosome of each kind, and the two sets together form homologous pairs.

In humans the diploid number is 46, which is 23 pairs.

### Gene

A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein.

The sequence of bases in a gene gives the order of amino acids in a protein.

A gene is not the protein itself, but the instruction for making it. Proteins make up structures in the cell and act as enzymes, antibodies and receptors, so genes influence the features of the organism through the proteins they code for.

A chromosome contains many genes. The information is carried by the sequence of bases in the DNA, and the final product is the protein.

### Allele

An allele is a version of the same gene. Alleles of one gene occupy the same position on homologous chromosomes.

Alleles occupy the corresponding position on homologous chromosomes and can differ in their DNA sequence.

A gene for flower colour may have an allele for purple and an allele for white. A pair of letters such as Rr stands for two alleles of one gene in a diploid cell, not two different genes.

Capital and small letters are used when one allele is dominant. For codominance a different notation is used (4.4.2). An allele is not a chromosome, and it is not the entire genotype of an organism.

### The genetic code

The sequence of bases in a gene determines the order of amino acids in a protein, through the genetic code.

A change in the base sequence can change the order of amino acids and so the structure and function of the protein. Some changes do not change the protein.

The DNA has four bases, A, T, C and G. The order of the bases in a gene decides the order of amino acids in the protein that is made. The order matters, and the total number of each base does not.

A protein with a different amino acid order folds into a different shape, so it may do its job less well or not at all. For example, an enzyme with a changed active site can no longer bind its substrate.

The chain to remember is: base sequence in the gene, then amino acid order, then the shape and function of the protein.

### How genes control cell function

DNA controls cell function by controlling which proteins are made, including enzymes, antibodies and receptors for neurotransmitters.

The shape of a protein decides how it interacts with a substrate, antigen or signalling molecule. Making a different amount or a changed protein can change the cell’s response.

An antibody has a shape that matches an antigen. A receptor for a neurotransmitter has a shape that recognises the transmitter at a synapse. The DNA is not itself the antibody or the signal; it carries the instructions for making the protein.

Different cells use different genes. A nerve cell makes receptors for neurotransmitters and a white blood cell makes antibodies, even though both contain the same DNA. This is how specialised cells carry out different jobs.

Link the effect to the task of the protein. A gene does not directly respire or digest: the enzymes it codes for do this.

## 4.4.2 Genotype, phenotype and genetic crosses

### Key points

The genotype is the combination of alleles and the phenotype is the observable feature. Homozygous means two identical alleles and heterozygous means two different ones. A genetic diagram shows the parents, the gametes, the offspring and the ratios. Use a clear allele key for complete dominance, codominance and X-linked inheritance. A test cross with a homozygous recessive individual can show an unknown genotype. A ratio predicts proportions and not exact numbers.

### Genotype and complete genetic diagrams

Genotype is the genetic make-up of an organism in terms of the alleles present.

A genotype is written in allele symbols: a capital letter for the dominant allele and the same letter in lower case for the recessive allele, for example Tt.

In a genetic diagram the parents are written as genotypes, pairs of allele symbols; a description such as tall or short is a phenotype and is not written in place of a genotype.

For example, a pea plant with the genotype Pp has one allele for purple flowers and one for white. Its phenotype is purple flowers, because the allele P is dominant.

### Phenotype

Phenotype is the observable features of an organism.

The phenotype depends on the genotype and on the environment. It is described as a feature, not written as a pair of alleles.

Tall is a phenotype, whereas Tt is a genotype. Two organisms with the same genotype can look different if they grow in different conditions, for example plants of the same variety given different amounts of water or fertiliser. Two organisms with the same dominant feature can also have different genotypes, such as TT and Tt.

### Homozygous

Homozygous means having two identical alleles of a particular gene.

RR, which has two dominant alleles, and rr, which has two recessive alleles, are both homozygous.

The term is used for one gene at a time. An organism can be homozygous for one gene and heterozygous for another. RR and rr have different phenotypes, but both are homozygous because the two alleles of the gene are the same.

### Heterozygous

Heterozygous means having two different alleles of a particular gene.

Rr is heterozygous. Each gamete receives one allele of the pair, either R or r.

A heterozygote is not a mixture of the two alleles. With complete dominance its phenotype is that of the dominant allele, but the recessive allele is still present and can be passed on to offspring. Under codominance, both alleles show in the phenotype, but the organism is still heterozygous because it has two different alleles.

### Dominant

A dominant allele is an allele that is expressed if it is present.

In complete dominance, a single copy of the dominant allele gives the dominant phenotype in a heterozygote.

Dominant does not mean stronger, more common or better. A dominant allele can be rare, and a recessive allele can be common. Dominance describes whether the allele shows in the phenotype when it is present.

### Recessive

A recessive allele is an allele that is only expressed when there is no dominant allele of the gene present.

In complete dominance, the recessive phenotype appears only in a homozygous recessive individual.

A recessive allele that is not expressed is still there. A heterozygote can pass it to its offspring, and it is not changed into the dominant allele. In X-linked inheritance, a male has only one X chromosome, so a recessive allele on it is expressed because there is no dominant allele to mask it.

### Monohybrid crosses

A genetic diagram for a monohybrid cross shows the parental phenotypes and genotypes, the gametes each parent can make, every possible fertilisation, and the offspring genotypes and phenotypes with their ratio.

Crossing two heterozygous parents (Tt × Tt) gives offspring genotypes TT, Tt and tt in the ratio 1 : 2 : 1, and phenotypes in the ratio 3 dominant : 1 recessive.

Crossing a heterozygous parent with a homozygous recessive parent (Tt × tt) gives offspring Tt and tt in the ratio 1 : 1.

A ratio from a genetic diagram predicts proportions, not exact numbers: a large number of offspring comes close to the ratio, but a small number can differ from it by chance.

T is dominant for tall stems and t is recessive. For Tt × Tt, the four equally likely combinations are TT, Tt, Tt and tt: genotype ratio 1:2:1 and phenotype ratio 3 tall:1 short. A second cross, Tt × tt, has T or t gametes from the first parent and t from the second, giving Tt and tt in equal proportions. This produces 1 tall:1 short. Label the parental phenotypes, parental genotypes, gametes, offspring and ratios; a grid is useful but not essential if labelled lines make each combination clear.

Each new fertilisation is a chance event. A family of eight offspring need not contain exactly two short ones; a ratio predicts probabilities and does not guarantee counts. Larger samples usually come closer to the expected ratio.

### Inheritance of sex in humans

A woman has two X chromosomes (XX), so all her eggs carry an X. A man has an X and a Y (XY), so half his sperm carry an X and half carry a Y.

Fertilisation gives an expected ratio of 1 XX (girl) to 1 XY (boy). This is a probability and does not guarantee the sex of any family’s children.

Set out the cross like any other: parents XX × XY, gametes X and X from the mother, X or Y from the father, offspring XX, XX, XY, XY. The ratio is 1 female : 1 male.

The egg always contributes an X, so it is the sperm that decides the sex of the child. Each child has a one in two chance of being a girl or a boy, whatever the sex of any earlier children.

### Test crosses

In a test cross, an organism with the dominant phenotype but an unknown genotype is crossed with a homozygous recessive individual.

If any offspring show the recessive phenotype, the unknown parent must carry a recessive allele. If all offspring show the dominant phenotype, the parent is probably homozygous, but only if there are many offspring.

Take dominant allele R. If the unknown parent is RR, then RR × rr gives all Rr, so all offspring show the dominant feature. If it is Rr, then Rr × rr gives Rr and rr in a 1:1 ratio, so about half show the recessive feature.

The recessive tester can only give r, so it makes the unknown parent’s alleles visible. A single recessive offspring shows that the parent is Rr. Four dominant offspring in a row is not conclusive, because an Rr parent could give this by chance, with a probability of one in 16.

### Codominance

In codominance, both alleles show in the phenotype of the heterozygote, and neither masks the other.

Use allele symbols that do not suggest dominance, such as a base letter with a superscript for each allele, and include a key for the phenotypes.

In one form of cattle coat colour, Cᴿ gives red hairs and Cᵂ gives white hairs. A CᴿCᵂ animal is roan, with both red and white hairs, and not pink. Crossing roan with white: the roan parent makes Cᴿ and Cᵂ gametes and the white parent makes only Cᵂ. The offspring are half CᴿCᵂ (roan) and half CᵂCᵂ (white), a ratio of 1 roan : 1 white.

The human ABO blood group is another example: the alleles for groups A and B are codominant, so a person with both is group AB.

### X-linked recessive inheritance

An X-linked allele is carried on the X chromosome. The Y chromosome carries no allele of this gene.

A male with one recessive X-linked allele shows the condition. A female needs two copies, so a female with one copy is an unaffected carrier.

Write the alleles as superscripts on the X, such as Xᴺ (unaffected) and Xⁿ (the condition), and write Y with no allele. For a carrier mother XᴺXⁿ and an unaffected father XᴺY, the offspring are XᴺXᴺ, XᴺXⁿ, XᴺY and XⁿY. One of the four, which is 25%, has the condition, and this is a boy. Among the sons alone, one in two is affected.

A son gets his X from his mother and his Y from his father, so a father cannot pass an X-linked allele to his son.

### Simulating inheritance

A simulation of a cross can use two bags of labelled counters, with one counter drawn from each bag to represent two gametes meeting. The counters are replaced after each draw.

Genotype and phenotype counts from many draws are compared with the expected proportions. The simulation tests how chance affects outcomes. It is not an experiment on real organisms.

For two heterozygous parents, each bag has equal numbers of R and r counters. Mix the counters, draw one without looking, record the pair, and put them back. After 40 draws, about a quarter should be rr, but not exactly. After 400 draws the proportions are usually closer to the expected 3 dominant : 1 recessive (one quarter rr).

Real crosses, for example in tomatoes or maize, can be used in the same way if the parents’ phenotypes are known and enough offspring are counted.

## 4.4.3 Variation and mutation

### Key points

Continuous variation has a range with intermediate values, whereas discontinuous variation has separate categories. Both genes and the environment affect the phenotype. A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA, whereas Down’s syndrome is a change in chromosome number. Where malaria occurs, heterozygotes for the sickle-cell allele survive better, which keeps the allele in the population despite the disease it causes.

### Variation

Variation is differences between individuals of the same species.

The differences may have genetic causes or environmental causes, or both. Differences between species do not count.

Two plants of the same species can differ in leaf length or flower colour. The first step is to identify the feature that is measured and the group that is compared. A difference in height does not show a genetic cause, because water, nutrition and age may also differ.

There are two sources of variation to consider: the alleles the individuals have (genetic) and the conditions they have lived in (environmental).

### Causes of phenotypic variation

Phenotypic variation is caused by genetic and environmental factors, and by their interaction.

Inherited alleles set what is possible, and conditions such as nutrition and water affect how far the feature develops.

Cloned seedlings that are genetically the same grow differently under different light, so the environment alone can cause variation. Plants of one garden can differ genetically even if they are in the same conditions.

To show that a difference is genetic, keep the environment the same for the individuals compared. To show that it is environmental, use individuals that are genetically the same, such as clones or identical twins. A visible difference alone does not tell us which cause is responsible.

### Continuous variation

Continuous variation shows a range of values with intermediate values between the extremes, as with human height or leaf length.

Many genes and the environment both contribute, so the feature is measured and not put into a few separate groups.

Measure the feature for many individuals and group the measurements into equal classes, such as 150–159 cm, 160–169 cm and so on. A histogram with touching bars suits this kind of data. Most individuals fall near the middle, with fewer at the extremes.

Human height depends on genes, but also on nutrition and health in childhood. A sample that mixes children of different ages would hide the pattern, so keep the age group the same and state the units.

### Recording and presenting variation data

An investigation of variation needs a clear population, a fair sample and a consistent way of measuring or classifying.

Measurements such as leaf length and seed mass are put into grouped frequencies. Separate categories, such as ABO blood group, are counted in each category.

Measure leaf length between the same two points, such as base to tip, and use a balance set to zero for seeds. Ten leaves from one plant are not independent samples of the species, so collect from many plants. For human height, use consent and record the data without names.

When you tabulate, include the units and check that the frequencies add up to the number of measurements. Use a histogram for continuous data, and a bar chart with gaps between the bars for categories.

For example, the leaf lengths 31, 34, 38, 42, 45, 47, 51 and 54 mm placed into classes of 30–39, 40–49 and 50–59 mm give 3, 3 and 2 leaves.

### Discontinuous variation

Discontinuous variation has distinct categories with no intermediates, such as the ABO blood groups A, B, AB and O.

These features are mostly controlled by genes alone. A bar chart with separate bars suits the data.

A person is group A, B, AB or O, and nothing in between. AB is a category of its own, and not a value between A and B. The environment has almost no effect on blood group.

Being controlled by genes does not mean that the categories are equally common. The frequencies of the groups differ between populations. The group is decided by the alleles inherited at one gene, which has three alleles in the population, though each person carries only two.

Not every human feature is a clear category like this.

### Gene mutation

A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA.

A change can alter the protein that is made, but some changes have no effect on the phenotype.

A mutation makes a new allele, which is a source of new variation. Natural selection later changes how common each allele is. A mutation is not an organism’s response to a need: it happens by chance.

In animals, a mutation is passed to offspring only if it happens in a cell that makes gametes; a mutation in an ordinary body cell is not inherited. In asexual reproduction, such as binary fission, a mutation in the parent cell is copied into the offspring.

### Radiation, chemicals and the rate of mutation

Ionising radiation, such as X-rays and gamma rays, and some chemicals can raise the rate of mutation by damaging DNA or by changing how it is copied.

A higher mutation rate means that changes are more likely. It does not mean that a useful change will be made.

Ionising radiation and certain chemicals, such as those in tobacco smoke, damage DNA, so mistakes are more likely when it is copied. A mutation can be harmful, have no effect, or occasionally be useful in a particular environment.

Mutation caused by these agents can lead to cancer if it happens in body cells, which is why people limit exposure to X-rays and avoid smoking. In school, these agents are only studied through information and models.

Not all chemicals have the same effect, and the effects depend on the dose.

### Down’s syndrome

Down’s syndrome is usually caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21, so that body cells have 47 chromosomes instead of 46.

It shows how a change in chromosome number can be a source of variation. This is different from a gene mutation, which alters bases inside one gene.

A gamete can end up with an extra chromosome 21 if the chromosomes do not separate correctly during meiosis. After fertilisation, the zygote has three copies of chromosome 21 instead of two, and every cell formed from it has the extra chromosome.

People with Down’s syndrome have a range of abilities and health needs, and should be treated with respect. The extra chromosome results from chance and nothing the parents did.

You do not need the details of how the error arises in meiosis.

### Sickle-cell anaemia

In sickle-cell anaemia the red blood cells can become sickle-shaped, break down more easily and block small blood vessels.

Possible effects include anaemia with tiredness, and episodes of pain when the blood flow to tissues is reduced. The severity differs between people.

The condition is caused by an inherited allele for an altered form of haemoglobin. It is not an infection and cannot be caught from another person.

Sickle cells carry less oxygen and break down quickly, so the person has too few red cells (anaemia), which leads to tiredness and breathlessness. Sickle cells also stick in small blood vessels and block them, which causes pain and can damage organs.

The condition is controlled by medical care, and many people with it live active lives.

### The sickle-cell allele and malaria

Where malaria is common, people who are heterozygous for the sickle-cell allele are better protected from severe malaria than people with two normal alleles.

This heterozygote advantage helps to keep the allele in the population, even though homozygous sickle-cell disease is serious.

Think through the selection. In a malaria region, people with two normal alleles can die of malaria. People with two sickle alleles can die of sickle-cell disease. Heterozygotes survive best, so they pass on both alleles, and the sickle allele remains fairly common.

Where malaria is not found, heterozygotes have no advantage, so the allele is rarer. This is why the allele is commoner in regions where malaria has long been common, such as parts of tropical Africa, than in regions without malaria.

The heterozygote still has some risk of malaria, and the protection is not complete. A person does not acquire the allele because of infection: selection works over generations.

## 4.4.4 Adaptation, selection and evolution

### Key points

Natural selection needs inherited variation and a selection pressure that causes unequal reproduction. Alleles become more or less common over generations, and individuals do not choose the changes they need. Hydrophytes and xerophytes have features that suit water or dry conditions. Antibiotic resistance is an example of selection of inherited variants. Artificial selection uses breeders’ choices over many generations to produce useful varieties.

### Adaptive features

Adaptive features are the inherited functional features of an organism that increase its fitness.

Fitness is the ability to survive and reproduce in a particular environment. It does not mean strength or large size.

A thick waxy cuticle on a leaf helps to conserve water in a dry habitat, but is not an important advantage in a plant that lives in water. A feature is adaptive in a particular environment, so say which challenge it helps with and how that leads to survival and reproduction.

Adaptive features are inherited. A scar or a broken leaf is acquired during life and is not an adaptive feature, however obvious it is.

### Adaptation

Adaptation is a process, resulting from natural selection, by which populations become more suited to their environment over generations.

Existing inherited variation and a selection pressure lead to unequal survival and reproduction, so the population changes over generations.

An individual plant closing its stomata in dry air is responding during its lifetime. This is not adaptation in the biological sense, because the population has not changed. Populations cannot choose changes because they need them.

A good explanation names the inherited variants, the pressure that acts on them and the change in the next generations. Compare this with the use of the word adaptation for a feature, as in adaptive feature above.

### Hydrophytes

Hydrophytes are plants that live in or on water. Air spaces in the tissues help them float and move gases, and floating leaves have stomata on the upper surface.

They have little supporting tissue and often flexible stems, since water holds them up. Features differ between floating and submerged plants.

In a water lily, the leaves float on the surface. The lower surface touches the water, so the stomata are on the upper surface, where they are exposed to air. Air spaces in the leaf and stem make them buoyant and carry oxygen to the roots in the mud, which have little air. The stems are flexible, so that they bend with moving water, and need little woody support.

A fully submerged plant has a different situation. It has few or no stomata, and a thin cuticle, since it takes in gases from the water. So match each feature to the plant, and do not treat all water plants as the same. Water lilies grow in the rivers and pools of northern Namibia, for example in the Kavango and Zambezi regions.

### Xerophytes

Xerophytes are plants adapted to dry conditions. Typical features are a thick waxy cuticle, small or reduced leaves, hairs on the leaves and sunken stomata.

These features reduce water loss by evaporation and by diffusion of water vapour. Deep or widespread roots help the plant to obtain water.

Water vapour leaves a leaf through the stomata by diffusion. A thick waxy cuticle stops water escaping through the leaf surface. Small leaves, or leaves rolled up, give a smaller surface area for evaporation. Hairs and sunken stomata trap a layer of humid air next to the stomata, so the concentration gradient is smaller and less water vapour diffuses out. Deep roots reach water far below the surface, and widespread roots collect water after rain.

Some xerophytes store water in swollen stems or leaves, as succulents do. Some have green stems that photosynthesise when the leaves are small.

Namibia has many examples. Welwitschia mirabilis of the Namib Desert has two long leaves that are tough and leathery and a very long taproot. The !nara plant of the dunes has no true leaves, since its green stems carry out photosynthesis, and its roots go deep to reach water. Always match a feature to the plant that has it.

### Natural selection

Natural selection is the selection of only the best adapted organisms for survival and reproduction.

In a population with inherited variation, a selection pressure makes some individuals more likely to survive and reproduce, and they pass on their alleles.

Being better adapted raises an organism’s chance of surviving and reproducing, but it does not guarantee either. Some well-adapted individuals die by chance, and some less well-adapted ones still breed. Over many generations, though, the better-adapted types leave more offspring on average, so their alleles become more common.

Being well adapted is relative to a particular environment. A feature that helps in a dry habitat may be no help in a wet one. Natural selection works on variation that already exists in the population: it does not create the variants it favours.

### The sequence of natural selection

Organisms produce more offspring than can survive. Because there is variation, resources are limited and there are other pressures, not all offspring survive and reproduce equally.

Individuals that are better suited to the conditions are more likely to survive, reproduce and pass on their alleles, so those alleles become more common over generations.

The steps are: variation within a population; production of many offspring; a struggle for survival, because of competition for resources, predators and disease; survival and reproduction of the better-adapted individuals; passing of their alleles to the next generation.

As an example, suppose a population of insects contains light and dark individuals. When the bark they live on is dark, birds see the light insects more easily and eat more of them. The dark insects survive longer and leave more offspring. Their alleles become more common.

The insects do not choose to change colour, and the predators do not aim to improve the population. The variation was already there, and the pressure only changes who reproduces.

### Evolution

Evolution is a process of change over a period of time.

In biology, the inherited characteristics of populations change over generations. One animal getting used to a hot climate is a different process.

The definition is broad, so use a population example. Evolution does not mean progress towards a fixed goal. A rising proportion of bacteria that carry an allele for antibiotic resistance is a clear example.

Evolution happens slowly in most cases, over many generations, and it depends on natural selection and on mutation that supplies new alleles.

### Changes in adaptive features

Natural selection can change how common an adaptive feature is in a population over generations.

Existing variation affects survival and reproduction under a selection pressure, and the population changes as alleles are passed on, not because each individual changes to suit its needs.

A dark form of an insect may become common on darkened bark if it already existed and was less often eaten. The darker bark is a change in the environment, and the change in the proportion of dark insects is the evolution.

A change in average size caused by better feeding alone is not evolution, because it is not inherited. Evolution is a change in the alleles of the population.

### Natural selection as the mechanism of evolution

Natural selection links the environment with the unequal reproductive success of inherited variants that already exist.

Repeated inheritance of the more successful variants changes the population over generations. Mutation supplies new variation, but it is a different process.

Natural selection explains how evolution takes place. The three links are inherited variation, a selection pressure that causes unequal success, and passing on of alleles that changes the population.

Selection is not a guarantee that all change is beneficial. If the pressure changes, a different variant may be favoured. A full answer names the feature, explains why it affects success and states that it is inherited.

Avoid saying that survivors survive because they are the survivors. Explain which feature helped.

### Antibiotic resistance

A population of bacteria includes inherited variants that differ in how well they survive an antibiotic.

When the antibiotic is used, the susceptible bacteria are killed, but the resistant ones survive and reproduce. Resistance becomes more common in the population.

The antibiotic does not make bacteria resistant. A mutation that gives resistance happens by chance, before the antibiotic is used. The antibiotic is the selection pressure. It kills the susceptible bacteria and leaves the resistant ones, with less competition, to reproduce, and their offspring are all resistant.

If the antibiotic is used again, a higher proportion of the bacteria survive. Resistance can also spread between bacteria, but you need to be able to give the explanation by selection.

Antibiotics are effective against bacteria and do not work on viruses. Patients are told to finish the course, because stopping early can leave resistant bacteria to multiply. This is one reason why TB treatment lasts for months.

### Artificial selection

Artificial selection is the modification of species by selective breeding.

People choose parents with the features they want and breed from their offspring over generations, so humans decide which individuals reproduce.

Artificial selection uses variation that is already present. A farmer who picks the largest animals to breed from will change the population only if size is inherited. Feeding one animal more may make it larger, but it does not change the alleles it passes on.

The population changes over the generations. The parent is not changed.

### Selective breeding for useful varieties

Breeders choose plants or animals with useful inherited features, breed them together and keep choosing the best of their offspring in each generation.

Aims include higher yield, drought tolerance, disease resistance or better meat or milk. Concentrating on a single feature can reduce variation or cause health problems.

A variety must be tested where it will be grown. A high-yield crop that needs a great deal of water may fail in a dry area. In Namibia, farmers need animals and crops that cope with heat and drought, so they keep hardy breeds, such as the Damara sheep and the Nguni cattle, and grow mahangu varieties that tolerate dry conditions.

Breeders may combine several useful features, and keep some genetic variation, so the population can still respond to a new disease or a change in climate. Economic importance means useful output compared with cost.
