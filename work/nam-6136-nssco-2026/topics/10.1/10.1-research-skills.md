## Choose an enquiry

### Choose an enquiry

A research topic identifies an issue that can be investigated with accessible evidence in a specified setting. Choose a safe, feasible focus rather than an entire national problem. Learners may collect in groups, but each presents an individual report; the written examination asks about the enquiry, which is not formally assessed coursework.

### Worked enquiry connection

With permission to observe a safe public site for one hour on three days, a fictional research group narrows its topic to the number of buses arriving in each equal interval. It does not claim to study private reasons for travel because it cannot interview passengers. The question fits what can be safely observed within the available time.

## A usable topic

### A usable topic

A good topic combines interest, relevance, ethical practice and a specific area and time. Test whether a manageable enquiry can answer it with available resources. Interest alone does not justify sensitive questions or unsafe fieldwork.

### Interest

Interest gives a researcher a reason to sustain attention to an issue. It should not preselect the answer; record observations that challenge expectations as well as those that agree.

Applied example: Interest can sustain attention through collection and checking. It does not justify ignoring evidence that challenges the researcher’s expectation.

### Relevance

Relevance links an enquiry to a meaningful development issue and the evidence needed. A water-journey question can inform access, while an unrelated opinion survey may not address that aim.

Applied example: It concerns a defined development issue, such as access to a service. Its variables provide evidence that can answer a practical question about that issue.

### Ethics

Ethical enquiry respects consent, privacy, safety and participants’ choice not to take part. Use non-sensitive topics, explain purpose and avoid identifiable records where unnecessary. Do not pressure participants, enter unsafe sites or ask classmates for health or violence disclosures.

Applied example: Explain the purpose and allow people to decline. Record answers without unnecessary identifying details and protect participants’ privacy.

### Specific scope

State the study area, population, variables and collection dates or period. A bounded question such as school-route travel time in one settlement during one week is more manageable than asking why education is unequal everywhere.

Applied example: Specify the place and population to be investigated. State the collection period and the variables to be measured.

### Worked enquiry connection

A fictional learner is interested in employment but cannot safely contact employers or collect private earnings records. A narrower enquiry into the opening times of accessible public training services may be feasible and relevant to development. Interest helps sustain the work; relevance, ethical access and a clear scope determine whether usable evidence can answer the question.

## Aim and objectives

### Aim and objectives

An aim states what the enquiry intends to find out; objectives are specific tasks that help achieve it. Start with a question, then choose measurable objectives and collection steps. A recommendation is a later response, not an observation already established before collection.

### Research aim

A research aim is a broad statement of what an enquiry intends to find out, linked to its research question or hypothesis. For a water-access enquiry, the aim might be to investigate return-journey times during the stated week.

### Research objective

A research objective is a specific task or target that helps the enquiry achieve its aim. For that aim, record return minutes including waiting for selected households and compare the resulting categories.

### Worked enquiry connection

An invented enquiry asks whether waiting times differ between two authorised service points. Its aim is to investigate that comparison. Specific objectives are to record waiting minutes over the same agreed period and compare the resulting distributions. The objectives guide collection; a proposal to change staffing would follow the findings rather than stand in for them.

## Question and hypothesis

### Question and hypothesis

A research question asks what the enquiry will investigate; a hypothesis predicts a testable relationship or outcome. “Do longer routes accompany lower attendance?” is a question; “Longer routes accompany lower attendance” is a hypothesis. Evidence may challenge either expectation.

### Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a tentative, testable statement about a relationship or outcome that evidence can support or challenge. Specify measurable variables and scope. A claim so vague that no observation could challenge it is unsuitable.

### Research question

A research question is a focused question that the enquiry seeks to answer using evidence. It need not predict the answer. Its wording should guide variables, participants and method.

### Worked enquiry connection

For an invented study, the question is whether sites farther from a collection depot have more uncollected containers during the same week. A testable hypothesis predicts that association. Distance and counts must be defined and collected consistently. An observed association could support the hypothesis without proving distance caused the missed collections.

## Data origins

### Data origins

Primary data is gathered first-hand for the current enquiry; secondary data was previously collected or published for another purpose. Classify origin relative to this enquiry, not whether the information is printed, digital, numerical or spoken.

Applied example: The researcher can collect current local observations first-hand. Existing records can supply a comparison if their population, units and dates are suitable.

### Primary data

Primary data is data collected first-hand for the present enquiry. A learner’s own journey observations qualify; accuracy still depends on selection, definitions and recording.

### Secondary data

Secondary data is data already collected or published for another purpose and used in the present enquiry. A published school register or service table can help if its dates, definitions and population fit; publication does not guarantee suitability.

### Worked enquiry connection

A fictional group records its own queue counts for its present enquiry: these are primary data. It also uses a previously published service timetable: that is secondary data for this enquiry. Either can be numerical, printed or digital. Origin determines the classification; suitability and accuracy still require checks on definitions, dates and coverage.

## Collection methods

### Collection methods

Questionnaires, interviews, observation and secondary-source research obtain different kinds of evidence. Specify who or what, where, when and how it is recorded. A bar chart presents collected data; it does not collect responses.

### Questionnaires

A questionnaire uses a consistent set of questions with recordable answers. Use neutral wording, one issue per question, units and non-overlapping categories. Pilot it with relevant volunteers, protect privacy and allow refusal.

Applied example: Respondents may have reasons or circumstances outside the offered choices. Results can therefore conceal relevant differences unless categories and optional explanation are carefully piloted.

### Interviews

An interview obtains information by asking participants questions and recording answers consistently. Explain purpose and consent, use a neutral schedule and ask clarifying questions without suggesting an answer. Interviews can explore reasons but require time and careful recording.

Applied example: The interviewer can ask a neutral clarifying question when an answer is unclear. That can give richer explanation than a fixed choice, although leading questions would distort it.

### Observation

Observation records directly visible or measurable features through a planned record. Define categories, units, site and time before collecting. Count from a safe public place; do not handle waste or infer private motives from visible behaviour.

Applied example: Observation records visible items or behaviour. Private motives are not visible, so an explanation of reasons needs suitable additional evidence rather than an inference from the count.

### Secondary-source research

Secondary-source research selects and records relevant existing evidence. Note publisher, date, unit, population, method and limitations. A different district or old period may not answer the current local question.

Applied example: Its period or population may differ from the question being investigated. Using it without checking those differences can produce a comparison that does not describe the current study group.

### Worked enquiry connection

A fictional travel survey asks consenting adults for minutes spent on a specified journey, including waiting, using the same neutral questions for each respondent. A separate observation sheet records visible queue length at agreed times. The questionnaire supplies reported experience, while observation supplies visible counts; the researchers should not silently substitute one measure for the other.

## Choose methods

### Choose methods

Evaluate a method by fit to the question, evidence quality, resources and ethics. Direct observation can measure visible counts but not private reasons; interviews can ask reasons but may face recall or response bias. A researcher difficulty obstructs collection; a community problem is what the enquiry investigates.

Applied example: Different methods produce different evidence. Direct counts can measure visible litter, while interviews can explore reasons; choosing first can leave the method unable to answer the actual question.

### Worked enquiry connection

An invented enquiry needs to know when a public service opens and why its hours change. Repeated safe observation records the visible opening times more directly than people’s recollections. A consenting provider interview can explain the arrangements, but its account should not be treated as an observed timetable. Different questions can justify complementary methods.

### Compare collection methods by their strengths and limits

A questionnaire can collect comparable answers from many respondents at manageable cost, and a self-completed form may reduce interviewer pressure. Closed choices can hide reasons, unclear wording can distort answers, and low literacy or non-response can exclude people. Interviews allow clarification and detailed explanations, but take more time and can be affected by leading questions or pressure to please the interviewer. Observation records visible conditions directly and can use repeatable categories, but it cannot establish private motives; unusual times or weather can distort a short visit. Secondary evidence saves collection time and may supply wider or historical comparisons, but dates, units, definitions or coverage may not fit the enquiry. Compare at least two relevant benefits and limitations when choosing; do not substitute a definition of the method for its evaluation.

## Pilot study

### Pilot study

A pilot study is a small preliminary trial of research procedures or instruments used to identify problems and make improvements before the main enquiry. Try with relevant volunteers, record difficulties, revise the instrument and check the revision. Pilot answers do not establish the whole population’s pattern.

### Worked enquiry connection

A fictional pilot finds that respondents interpret “frequent” visits differently. The researcher revises the question to ask for a count during the past seven days, checks that participants understand the period, then tries the revised wording again. The pilot identifies a measurement problem; its few answers do not establish the population’s actual pattern.

## Population and sample

### Population and sample

The research population is the whole defined group; a sample is the selected subset studied. The sampling method is the selection procedure, not the group itself. Findings need limits where the frame misses groups or responses are incomplete.

### Research population

The research population is the whole group of units or people that the enquiry is intended to investigate. Define it before sampling: all households on the relevant settlement list differs from everyone in Namibia.

### Research sample

The research sample is the selected subset of the research population from which data are collected. A list of 12 selected households describes a sample; choosing every fifth household describes the method.

### Worked enquiry connection

An invented study defines its population as households listed in two named settlements during a specified month. A sample selects some of those households for observation or voluntary questions. It cannot automatically represent households outside that area or later residents. A clear population definition makes it possible to assess what the sampling frame includes or misses.

## Sampling choices

### Sampling choices

Random, stratified and systematic sampling use different explicit selection procedures. Start with a relevant frame, justify size and selection, record non-response and avoid silently replacing a refusal with a friend. No sample guarantees perfect representativeness.

Applied example: A selection procedure can only choose members covered by its list. Missing a group from that frame can bias coverage even when selection from the list is fair.

### Random sampling

Random sampling selects by chance so each member of the defined research population has an equal chance of selection. Number a complete list and use a fair draw or random numbers without duplicates. Selecting whoever is nearest does not meet that rule.

Applied example: Distance from the researcher determines who is selected, so other households do not have an equal chance. A fair draw from a complete numbered list provides the required chance procedure.

### Stratified sampling

Stratified sampling divides a population into relevant groups and selects from each using an explicit method. For proportional selection, allocate sample places by each group’s share, then randomly select within groups. It preserves planned group coverage but cannot remove every frame or response bias.

Applied example: It deliberately allocates places to both groups in proportion to their population shares. A smaller group is therefore included by design, although non-response can still distort the final evidence.

### Systematic sampling

Systematic sampling selects at a fixed interval from an ordered list after an appropriate starting point. For 48 households and 12 places, interval is four; choose the start fairly among positions one to four, then take every fourth entry. A repeating pattern in the list may bias it.

Applied example: A repeating list pattern can coincide with the selection interval. The sample may then overrepresent one type of unit even though a fixed rule was followed.

### Worked enquiry connection

For an original stratified example, a list contains 60 households in one area and 30 in another. A proportional sample of 18 allocates 12 places and six places respectively, followed by random selection within each group. The design preserves planned group proportions. Incomplete lists and refusals still need recording; proportional allocation does not remove every source of bias.

### Sampling calculations and frames

For this separate worked example, a list of 48 households and a sample of 12 gives interval 48 ÷ 12 = 4. A fair starting position of 2 produces 2, 6, 10, 14, 18, 22, 26, 30, 34, 38, 42 and 46. For two fictional villages of 30 and 15 households, a proportional sample of 9 allocates 6 and 3 places: 30/45 × 9 and 15/45 × 9. Randomly select within each village. For a list of 24 schools, a fair draw of four distinct numbers is random selection; the sample is the selected schools, while the draw is the method. Record refusals and limitations of the lists.

## Organise, present and interpret

### Organise, present and interpret

Organise records consistently, reconcile totals, then choose a suitable graph or sketch map to answer the enquiry. A table states observations; interpretation links relevant evidence into a pattern. Percentage equals category count divided by the relevant total, multiplied by 100; identify missing data and avoid asserting causation from association alone.

### Findings

Findings report and interpret collected evidence, including comparisons and exceptions relevant to the aim. A high value differs from a large increase. Use paired observations and units rather than a list with no relationship.

### Graphs

Bar charts compare categories; pie charts or divided rectangles show parts of a stated whole when categories are compatible. For bars state axes, units, category labels and an even scale. Pie angle is category share multiplied by 360°; a divided rectangle allocates proportional lengths. Totals must reconcile. Graphs here use data and construction specifications, not an unseen verified rendering.

### Sketch maps

A sketch map locates relevant features and patterns using clear symbols and a key, title and orientation. Use observed or supplied relative positions; do not imply accurate scale unless measured. Distinguish a spatial pattern from a graph of category totals.

### Worked enquiry connection

An original observation table records 3, 6 and 5 users in three named waiting-time categories. A bar graph gives the categories on the horizontal axis and users on a labelled vertical scale from zero; separated equal-width bars show heights 3, 6 and 5. A title, time period and source identify what is represented. The middle category is largest, but the graph alone does not explain why.

### Check representation by construction

For a separate worked pie example, 20 journeys comprise 8 walking, 7 cycling and 5 using a bus. Their sector angles are 144°, 126° and 90°: divide each count by 20 and multiply by 360°. The angles total 360°. For production-cost shares of 50%, 30% and 20%, a 15 cm divided rectangle has sections 7.5, 4.5 and 3 cm, with boundaries at 7.5 and 12 cm. Label the whole, categories and period. A separate sketch-map example has a north–south road, a clinic at its north end, a bus stop midway and a reading room east of its south end; use symbols, a key, orientation and title, without implying measured scale. A research-process flow chart links question, planning/ethics, pilot, collection, presentation and conclusion; a pilot decision can loop back to revision. Arrows show procedure, not quantities. These specifications do not verify an unseen plotted figure.

## Conclusions and recommendations

### Conclusions and recommendations

A conclusion answers the question from findings within their limits; a recommendation proposes a feasible response tied to that answer and identifies an implementer. Do not introduce uncollected evidence. For community action distinguish aims, organisation, funding and implementation obstacles: assigning volunteers does not obtain money, and an obstacle needs a matched response and responsible actor.

### Conclusion

A conclusion is an evidence-based answer to the research question or judgement about the hypothesis, within the enquiry’s limits. A sample pattern can support a bounded answer, not a claim about all places. Findings are the supporting observations, not interchangeable with the conclusion.

### Recommendation

A recommendation is a proposed feasible action linked to the findings and conclusions, identifying who could implement it. Name the practical change, implementer and relevant resource condition. A wish such as “improve development” is too vague to follow.

### Worked enquiry connection

In an invented enquiry, repeated records show that a service opens late because a qualified operator arrives after the scheduled start. A feasible recommendation could revise staffing arrangements through the responsible provider. It addresses the observed obstacle more directly than an unrelated poster. State the evidence, responsible actor and resources, and distinguish a proposed response from one already carried out.

### Organisation, funding and implementation

A fictional storage activity costing N$600 with N$200 available needs N$400 additional resources. Agreeing volunteers and dates organises it; recording contributions funds it. If a required lid is late, the coordinator should arrange a provider-approved suitable alternative or delay use. For a book project costing N$100 with N$40 available, seek N$60 or suitable in-kind materials and use the offered dry room to prevent the stated storage loss. These are project plans, not evidence that either was implemented. A recommendation identifies an implementer and follows findings; a conclusion does not introduce a new result.

### Keep your own enquiry record

Keep an individual dated record with these fields: topic and study area; question or hypothesis; aim and specific objectives; dates and the defined population; sample size and actual selection procedure; consent and safety arrangements; instruments and pilot problem, revision and recheck; collection procedure; anonymised observations, units and missing values; table or graph with title and source; main findings and an exception; conclusion linked to the question; limitations; recommendation, implementer and required resources; actual researcher obstacle and response. Preserve changes and reasons. If working in a group, state what was shared and what you personally did. A missing stage should be marked not completed, rather than replaced by a fictional event. Use this record to answer Section B in your own words.

### Separate fictional demonstration from personal evidence

This demonstration is invented, not an enquiry you carried out. Suppose a safe observation found 12 visible items at one site and six at another during the same period, with one route inaccessible. The finding is a two-to-one count contrast in the observed places, not a result for the whole community. A conclusion can say the recorded sites differed, while noting missing coverage. A recommendation might ask the responsible collection provider to inspect the higher-count site. For your own answer, replace the demonstration with your actual observations and limits. Do not claim these counts, visits or recommendations as your own experience.
