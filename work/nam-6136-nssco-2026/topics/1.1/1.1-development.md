## What development means

### What development means

Development is improvement in people's quality of life across economic, social, political and environmental dimensions. Economic development concerns productive opportunities and material resources. Social development concerns conditions such as health, education, shelter and adequate food. Political development concerns people's ability to participate, exercise rights and hold decision makers accountable. Environmental development includes improving living conditions while protecting the resources on which people depend.

### A project can have several effects

Consider an invented settlement where a new workshop offers paid jobs but releases dirty water into the stream used by households. More production and earnings are possible economic gains. Unsafe water is an environmental and social cost. Calling the project development merely because output rises overlooks those costs. A useful assessment asks who gains, who loses and whether the gains can last. If a treatment system removes the waste and can be maintained, jobs and cleaner water may be achieved together. If the workshop cannot afford to maintain that system, its installation alone does not settle the issue.

### Link the dimensions

This distinction underlies the rest of the subject. Production in Topic 3.1 explains how goods and services are created. Health in Topic 7.2 and education in Topic 7.3 show why services matter to people's lives. When considering any new activity, connect its economic effect to at least one effect on people or their environment. A higher money income is evidence about one dimension; it is not a complete description of development.

## World patterns and differences within countries

### World patterns and differences within countries

Development varies across the world and within individual countries. Broad historical patterns place many higher-income industrial economies in Europe, North America and parts of East Asia, while many lower-income economies are found in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia. These are broad geographical patterns, not a current classification list or a claim about every country in a continent. High-income economies also occur outside those regions. Differences within a country can be large: an urban area may have a hospital and reliable water while a remote community has neither. A national average cannot locate those differences.

### Developed, developing, LEDC and MEDC

Developed and developing describe relative levels of development in a simplified way. More developed economies commonly have higher average incomes and wider access to services, but deprivation can persist in them. Developing covers countries with very different resources and experiences; it does not mean that nothing has developed. LEDC means less economically developed country and MEDC means more economically developed country. The economic emphasis matters. A higher income grouping does not show whether rights are protected, water is safe or income is shared equally. Use a label to introduce a comparison, then support that comparison with the indicator that actually answers the question.

### Industrialised and less industrialised economies

Industrialisation is growth in the importance and capacity of industrial production within an economy. Industrialised describes an economy with substantial industrial capacity; less industrialised describes relatively less. The existence of one factory does not settle the classification. Industrialisation can expand production, paid work and opportunities to learn technical skills. Its effect on living standards also depends on wages, safety, access to those jobs and environmental damage. An economy with a large service sector may still have substantial industrial capacity. Economic structure and quality of life are related questions, but neither is a complete substitute for the other.

### Capitalism and communism

Capitalism emphasises private ownership of productive resources and market decisions about production and exchange. Communism as an economic ideal involves common ownership of productive resources; economies described as communist have generally relied heavily on state ownership and central planning. These descriptions concern ownership and decision making. They are not names for rich and poor. In practice, an economy may combine private shops, state services and public regulation. Establish the ownership arrangement from the evidence rather than assigning a development level from its label. Political institutions and economic arrangements also need to be examined separately.

### Newly industrialised countries

A newly industrialised country, or NIC, has undergone substantial, relatively recent industrial expansion and structural change. Manufacturing and industrial employment become more important. The term is used in historical accounts of development and has no single permanent membership list. For this topic, understand the change the term describes rather than memorising a supposed up-to-date list. Expanding industry may create employment and exports while some households remain poor. To describe development, ask about the effects on income and living conditions as well as the change in production.

### Reading an original comparison

In a fictional same-year comparison, Region A has income per person of N$90,000 and 95% safe-water access; Region B has N$25,000 and 60% access. Region A combines higher average income with wider water access: income is N$65,000 higher and access is 35 percentage points higher. Those two observations support a difference in both economic resources and a living-condition indicator. They do not establish the political system or prove that income caused water access. A figure describes what was measured. A relationship links relevant figures without claiming more than they establish.

### Keep classifications separate

Topic 4.1 develops industrialisation and its consequences. The indicators in the next lesson give evidence for comparing development. When reading a source, first identify whether its labels concern income, industry or ownership. Then choose evidence relevant to the comparison. This avoids treating all three classifications as interchangeable.

## Indicators are evidence

### Indicators are evidence

An indicator is a measurable feature used as evidence about a wider condition; one indicator seldom gives a complete account of development. An environmental measure describes environmental conditions; a social measure describes people's living conditions; a political measure concerns participation or rights; an economic measure concerns productive resources, output or income. Check the definition, date, unit and population before comparing values. A percentage and a total answer different questions. Two measures can move together without one being proven to cause the other.

### Environmental evidence

Environmental indicators include the share of households using safe water, a measure of air pollution or an area of forest lost. Interpret the direction: more safe-water access is generally an improvement, while more harmful pollution is deterioration. In a fictional settlement, comparable annual records show safe-water access rising from 55% to 75% and unsafe water samples falling from 18 to 6 out of 30 tested. Access improves by 20 percentage points, and unsafe samples fall by 12. Together the observations support an improvement in water-related environmental conditions. They do not describe the settlement's air quality or identify the cause of the changes.

### Social evidence

Social indicators include education, health, housing and service access. Literacy concerns the ability to read and write according to the measure used. Life expectancy is the average number of years a person at a stated age is expected to live under the mortality conditions used in the estimate; it is not a promise for an individual. Infant mortality rate records deaths before the first birthday per thousand live births for a stated period. In fictional District C, school participation rises from 70% to 85% and infant mortality falls from 40 to 25 per thousand live births. The combination supports improvement in education access and infant survival. Keep the units distinct: 15 percentage points and 15 deaths per thousand live births are not the same measure.

### Political evidence

Political development concerns opportunities to participate and influence decisions, rights and accountability. Indicators may include participation in a decision process or evidence of access to it. In an invented community election with 1,000 eligible adults in each period, participation rises from 400 to 700 adults, while the number of neighbourhoods able to use an accessible meeting venue rises from two of five to five of five. Wider access accompanies higher participation. Counts from a meeting alone would not establish that rights are protected or that everyone had a free choice. Identify what the source actually measures and combine complementary observations rather than treating one statistic as a complete political judgement.

### Economic evidence

Economic indicators can include income per person and employment. In fictional areas D and E, annual income per person is N$30,000 and N$45,000 respectively; employment among a consistently defined working-age population is 50% and 65%. E combines N$15,000 more average income with employment 15 percentage points higher. This supports a stronger position on those two measures. It does not show how much the lowest-income households receive or the quality of available jobs. Comparable definitions matter: changing the age range or what counts as employment can change the apparent pattern.

### National totals and per-person figures

A national total is the amount for the whole country. A per-person figure divides a total by population. GNP of N$120 million shared across 6,000 people gives N$20,000 per person for that period. A learner who calls the N$20,000 figure the national total has confused the two measures. GNI per person is also an average, rather than the income received by the whole nation. The separating test is whether the national measure has been divided by population. Neither average means that each person actually receives that amount. These examples explain the total-versus-average distinction, without treating different national accounting labels as interchangeable in every dataset.

### Combine dimensions without hiding a conflict

Two fictional areas each have annual income per person of N$40,000. Area F has 90% safe-water access and a 20-minute average journey to a clinic; Area G has 50% access and an 80-minute journey. The equal income averages conceal differences in environmental and social access. Area F has water access 40 percentage points higher and a clinic journey 60 minutes shorter. Those differences justify examining service access separately from income. They do not prove that Area F is better on every aspect of development, since the data says nothing about participation or income distribution.

### Choose the measure for the question

Health in Topic 7.2 and education in Topic 7.3 explain mechanisms behind social indicators. Research in Topic 10.1 explains how data is collected and assessed. To interpret a source, name the measured dimension, compare relevant observations with their units, and state the relationship they support. Add a limitation when the question needs a wider judgement, rather than allowing a caution to replace the required description.

## GNP and the per-person measure

### GNP and the per-person measure

GNP is the value of final output attributable to a country's residents over a period, including net income from abroad. Distinguish the national total from a per-person figure. GNP per person is GNP divided by the population; an average that does not show how income is distributed. Use the same period and compatible currency units in a comparison. A larger population can produce a larger total without having more resources per person.

### Show the division and the unit

For a fictional country, annual GNP is N$240 million and population is 12,000. GNP per person = N$240,000,000 ÷ 12,000 = N$20,000 per person per year. Write out the millions before dividing, or keep a consistent power-of-ten method. This answer is an average, not the wage of a typical worker. If another fictional country has GNP of N$300 million and 30,000 residents, its average is N$10,000 per person per year despite its larger total.

### Use it for an appropriate judgement

For fictional Area H, average annual income per person is N$50,000 and safe-water access 40%; J has N$30,000 and 85%. An income-only rule prioritises J. For improving water access, H’s larger access gap provides stronger evidence of need. Income and access answer different questions: compare the measure directly relevant to the decision.

### Why an income average is incomplete

GNP per person divides a national aggregate by population, so unequal distribution can leave many households with low incomes despite a high average. Unrecorded household farming and informal sales can make measured output incomplete. Money income does not show whether people can obtain safe water, education or health care. It also leaves out losses such as damaged habitats and the value of unpaid care. Price differences change what a given income buys, so international comparisons need a comparable basis. An average may help compare economic resources, but combine it with evidence on distribution, services and environmental conditions before judging living standards.

## A pattern across living conditions

### A pattern across living conditions

Higher development is commonly associated with higher income per person, longer life expectancy and wider education and health access. Adequate housing includes shelter and access to essential services; diet concerns sufficient and varied food as well as the ability to buy it. Job opportunities matter through earnings, stability, safety and skills. A change from agriculture towards industrial and service employment can accompany development, but the sector name alone says little about wages or working conditions. Population growth also varies. It should be described from the source, rather than assumed from a country label.

### A fictional comparison

In a same-year synthetic table, Country K has annual income per person of N$22,000, life expectancy of 61 years and 58% of households with a basic sanitation service. Country L has N$68,000, 76 years and 91%. L combines higher income with longer life expectancy and wider sanitation access. The income difference is N$46,000, life expectancy is 15 years longer and sanitation access is 33 percentage points higher. The pattern spans economic and social conditions. It does not describe income inequality within either country. Nor does it justify a stereotype about an individual resident's diet, occupation or health.

### Describe people as well as production

Population in Topic 7.1, health in Topic 7.2 and education in Topic 7.3 provide explanations for these patterns. Start with what a table shows, select the relevant features, and distinguish a general association from a claim about every person. A useful comparison links the same characteristic across the two places.

## Human Development Index

### Human Development Index

HDI is a composite measure combining health, education and income dimensions of human development; it does not capture every aspect of development. Composite means that several components are brought together. This helps a comparison move beyond national income alone. A higher comparable HDI indicates a higher combined level of achievement in its included dimensions. It does not mean that each household has the same standard of living or that environmental and political conditions have been fully measured.

### Multidimensional Poverty Index

MPI is a measure of overlapping deprivations in health, education and living standards; it differs from an income-only poverty measure. Deprivation means a lack in a relevant condition, such as an essential service. The overlap matters: disadvantages can occur together in the same households. A lower comparable MPI indicates less multidimensional poverty. This is a different direction from HDI, where a higher value indicates higher achievement. Do not interpret a higher MPI as higher development simply because the number is larger.

### Read the two indices together

In an original fictional comparison made using consistent methods, Country M has HDI 0.72 and MPI 0.08; Country N has HDI 0.55 and MPI 0.24. M combines a higher HDI with a lower MPI. The HDI difference is 0.17 and MPI difference is 0.16. Together these support higher combined human development and less multidimensional poverty in M. Do not call either difference a percentage change or assume it tells how many people lack a service. The indices summarise different information and are not subtracted from each other. They help reveal why income alone is incomplete, but a national summary still needs more detailed evidence about particular groups.

### What the measures can guide

If two places have similar average incomes but different school participation and health conditions, the extra dimensions matter to a comparison of human development. If the question concerns households experiencing several disadvantages together, MPI addresses that issue more directly than an income average. Select a measure by the question it can answer. A local decision about a particular water point still needs local access and cost evidence; an index alone cannot locate the repair or establish its feasibility.

### Use dimensions, not a complicated formula

Health and education are developed in Topics 7.2 and 7.3. Here, explain what the indices represent, their directions and their limits. Calculating a full HDI or MPI from component data is outside this lesson's teaching depth.

## Factors interact

### Factors interact

Development reflects interacting historical, political, social, resource and economic factors. A mineral deposit may provide an opportunity, but machinery, transport, skills and public decisions influence whether it benefits people. A barrier can also persist after the event that created it. Explain a factor by tracing a mechanism: identify the condition, show how it changes an opportunity or constraint, then connect that change to people's lives. Avoid treating a continent, culture or resource as a fixed destiny.

### Historical influences

Past arrangements can shape present access to land, schooling and transport. In an invented region, a railway was built to carry ore directly to a port, while nearby farming settlements were left without feeder roads. Farmers now have produce to sell but cannot afford the slow journey to the railway. The inherited transport layout limits market access, so produce may remain unsold and farm income constrained. The explanation connects a historical pattern to a present mechanism. A road investment could reduce that constraint, but only if it can be maintained and farmers have buyers. History influences opportunities; it does not prevent choices that alter them.

### Political decisions and implementation

Public decisions influence where resources go and whether services function. In a fictional district, a clinic budget is approved, but money intended for repairs is diverted and the roof remains damaged. Rain then closes the treatment room. The development problem is not merely a low budget: failed use of allocated funds prevents the facility operating. Accountability and repair oversight may help convert available resources into service access. A general claim that any one political label guarantees development misses the role of specific decisions, implementation and scrutiny.

### Social opportunities

Education can develop skills, and health can affect people's ability to learn and work. Access is shaped by barriers such as distance, cost or exclusion. In an invented settlement, skilled training places are available and a workshop is seeking trained workers, but afternoon sessions clash with learners' caring duties. Changing session times could allow more of those learners to attend, gain the relevant skill and apply for the available work. The job opportunities and available training are both material facts: changing the timetable would not by itself create jobs where none exist. Avoid blaming a group for barriers that the evidence identifies in its circumstances.

### Resources and their use

Natural resources are opportunities with conditions. In a fictional farming area, productive soil is available but a damaged water pump prevents irrigation during a dry period. Repairing the pump can help the soil support crops if the water supply is sufficient and withdrawals remain sustainable. In a different case, mineral earnings might finance school buildings, yet benefits may be limited if earnings leave the region or pollution damages water. State how resources are used and who benefits; naming a mineral is not an explanation of development.

### Economic bottlenecks

Finance, equipment, infrastructure and markets affect production and earnings. An invented sewing workshop has working machines, trained workers and confirmed orders, but lacks N$2,000 for cloth. Finance for cloth would remove the stated input shortage, allowing existing resources to meet the orders and earn revenue. Buying another machine would leave the cloth shortage unresolved. The consequence is conditional on the facts: without demand, more cloth would not establish that output could be sold. This is why a developed explanation uses the actual constraint rather than a memorised claim that any investment increases development.

### Combine factors in a new case

Suppose a fictional rural processing group has a reliable water supply and trained workers, but an impassable road prevents delivery of its products. The resource and social opportunities exist, yet the economic transport barrier prevents sales. Repairing that connection could allow income to be earned if buyers remain available. This case shows interaction: strengths in one dimension do not cancel a binding constraint elsewhere. To test an explanation, ask whether removing the proposed cause would change the outcome under the supplied conditions.

### Carry mechanisms into the subject

Production in Topic 3.1, industrialisation in Topic 4.1 and rural development in Topic 5.1 revisit these connections. Public choices, social access and resources should be linked to a concrete mechanism and a development consequence. A well-supported explanation is more useful than a long list of factors with no connection between them.
