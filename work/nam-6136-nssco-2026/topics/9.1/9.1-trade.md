## Trade

### Trade

Trade is exchange of goods and services, commonly through buying and selling. International trade crosses national borders. Exports are sold abroad; imports are bought from abroad, from the perspective of the economy being described.

### Use the evidence

A fictional pottery group sells surplus goods to another district and buys packaging it cannot produce efficiently itself. Exchange can widen its market and supply useful inputs, provided transport costs and product quality permit viable trade. The benefit comes from the goods and opportunities obtained, not simply from money changing hands.

## Reasons for exchange

### Reasons for exchange

Trade can connect differing resources, skills, products and markets, allowing access beyond domestic production. Benefits depend on viable costs, access and terms; reliance on a narrow product or supplier can expose livelihoods to disruption.

### Use the evidence

Two fictional producers specialise in different products suited to their skills and resources, then exchange part of their output. Specialisation may improve productivity and widen the range available to each. Dependence on a single customer or an unreliable supplier can limit those gains. Assess the terms and reliability of exchange alongside the productive opportunity.

### Several reasons for international trade

Natural resources and climate differ, so countries can obtain products unavailable locally. Different skills and equipment make some goods cheaper or better suited to production elsewhere. Specialising can allow larger-scale production and lower average costs when demand is sufficient. Producers can reach more buyers beyond a small domestic market. Consumers and firms can obtain a wider variety of goods, services and productive inputs. These are different benefits, but transport costs, unreliable suppliers or dependence on one export can reduce them. Explain the particular benefit and its condition rather than treating every import as a loss.

## Two external measures

### Two external measures

Balance of trade is export value minus import value for goods over a period; balance of payments records wider economic transactions between residents and non-residents. State explicitly if a supplied trade measure includes services. An investment inflow is not itself a goods export and cannot automatically be counted as an improved goods trade balance.

### Balance of trade

Balance of trade is the value of goods exported minus goods imported over a stated period. Exports above imports give a surplus; below give a deficit. Use comparable currency, period and scope rather than combining counts and values.

### Balance of payments

Balance of payments is a record of economic transactions between a country’s residents and non-residents over a stated period. It includes more than goods trade, such as services, income, transfers and financial flows. Detailed sub-account accounting is outside this lesson.

### Use the evidence

A fictional country records annual goods exports of N$75 million and goods imports of N$68 million. Exports minus imports gives a goods trade surplus of N$7 million. This balance excludes services and financial transactions, so it is not a complete measure of external payments or development. A separate investment inflow must not be added as if it were a goods export.

## Prices and external pressure

### Prices and external pressure

Export revenue depends on price and quantity, while import needs create external payment requirements. A fall in export prices with unchanged quantities can reduce receipts and make essential imports harder to finance. Foreign investment provides finance through a different transaction, not an immediate export-value change.

### Trading and fluctuation

Countries trade goods and services, directly or through organised markets and firms. A producer relying on one export faces concentrated price or demand exposure; diversification and viable processing can reduce some risks but need resources and buyers.

Applied example: A price fall can reduce sales revenue even when the quantity sold is unchanged. Uncertain future prices can make investment and repayment planning harder.

### Payment difficulties

External payment pressure can arise when available foreign receipts and finance are insufficient for payment commitments. Prioritising essential needs or seeking support can help, but borrowing creates obligations and restricting imports may remove necessary productive inputs.

Applied example: Essential imported equipment or inputs can become harder to obtain. Meeting external repayment obligations can compete with other uses of scarce finance.

### A fairer economic order

Debates about a new economic order concern fairer participation, bargaining, access to technology and the distribution of gains in global exchange. Better terms can support development, but agreements need implementation and capacity; a declaration alone does not change productive opportunities.

Applied example: Improve developing producers’ opportunities to obtain a reasonable return from trade. Reduce barriers that leave weaker economies with little influence over trading or financing terms.

### Use the evidence

A fictional exporter sells 500 units at N$20 each, then the same quantity at N$16. Receipts fall from N$10,000 to N$8,000. If its foreign payment commitments do not change, fewer export receipts are available to meet them. This illustrates price exposure; a real judgement would also examine other receipts, finance and the possibility of adjustment.

## Broad trade patterns

### Broad trade patterns

Developing, newly industrialised and developed economies participate in varied commodity, manufactured and service trade. Broad patterns can include primary-product dependence or expanding manufactures, but countries differ and also trade within these groups. A category is not a current trade-data table.

### Developing economies

Some developing economies rely heavily on a few primary exports and imported equipment or goods. That concentration can expose receipts to price changes; it does not describe every developing country or prevent manufactured and service exports.

Applied example: World price falls can sharply reduce earnings from its narrow export base. It may still need to buy machinery and other manufactured goods from abroad.

### NIC patterns

Industrial expansion can increase manufactured exports and imports of equipment or components. Participation in production chains may create jobs, while imported-input dependence and low local retention can limit gains. NIC is a broad syllabus category, not a permanent current list.

Applied example: Manufactured products can form a larger share of exports. Imported equipment and intermediate inputs may support the expanding production.

### Developed economies

Many developed economies trade manufactures, services, technology and raw materials across extensive networks. They also differ in resources and specialisation. Higher income does not mean an economy exports every advanced product or avoids dependence.

Applied example: Exports can include high-value manufactured products or services. Imports can include raw materials and products made through international production networks.

### Use the evidence

In an invented export table, Country C receives 65% of export earnings from primary goods and 35% from manufactures. Country D receives 40% and 60%. D has the larger manufactured share; C may be more exposed to its primary-export markets. Neither table establishes income distribution, every product traded or the permanent development category of a country.

## Globalisation

### Globalisation

Globalisation is increasing international connectedness and interdependence through flows such as trade, investment, information and people. It differs from global warming, a rise in global average temperature. Communication can support globalisation but does not by itself establish fair access.

### Use the evidence

An invented design business shares plans with a foreign workshop and coordinates orders across several countries. These links illustrate globalisation through connected production, communication and exchange. They are conceptually different from a change in global average temperature. A cross-border link does not itself establish the scale or distribution of local development benefits.

## Opportunities and constraints

### Opportunities and constraints

Global connections can expand markets, finance and knowledge while exposing producers to competition, shocks and unequal bargaining. Judge participation, local retention, capacity and environmental effects. An increased flow is not by itself proof of a net local benefit.

### Opportunities

Access to larger markets, suitable investment and knowledge can support productive activity. Benefits require the ability to meet quality and delivery needs and opportunities for local workers or suppliers to participate.

Applied example: Access to foreign buyers can expand a producer’s market. International links can supply technology or knowledge that improves productive capability.

### Constraints

Infrastructure gaps, missing skills, finance barriers or dependence on dominant buyers can limit global participation. External competition can challenge local firms while widening consumer options; compare who gains and loses under the stated conditions.

Applied example: Stronger foreign competition can displace producers unable to match price or quality. Reliance on external buyers can expose local work to distant demand shocks.

### Use the evidence

A fictional processing agreement offers local technicians supervised training and includes purchases from nearby suppliers. Those links can retain skills and spending beyond immediate output. They require workable training, suitable supplier quality and fair work conditions. Assess actual delivery, costs and retained benefits; ownership or a foreign connection alone does not establish the result.

## Participate through capabilities

### Participate through capabilities

Infrastructure, human-resource development, market exposure and suitable investment can support international participation. Choose the action that addresses the actual constraint; promotion cannot repair a blocked road or supply a missing quality standard.

### Infrastructure

Transport, communication and reliable productive services connect firms to markets. Usable maintained networks reduce some delivery or coordination barriers, but must fit the goods and destination.

### Human resources

Relevant education and training develop capabilities to produce, manage quality and coordinate exchange. Training needs practice and work opportunities; it complements rather than replaces equipment or access.

### International exposure

Market research, exchange and appropriate promotion can help producers understand buyers and requirements. Exposure becomes useful when firms can act on the information; attention alone does not establish sales.

### Investment

Suitable foreign investment can provide productive finance, equipment or networks. Local participation, viable terms and maintained capacity affect benefits. Attracting funds is not equivalent to exporting goods.

### Use the evidence

An invented exporter has customers and transport but its containers fail the buyers’ agreed labelling requirements. Feasible quality-control training addresses the immediate access constraint more directly than extra advertising. The producer must still meet cost and delivery requirements. International exposure is useful when it leads to capabilities that fit the actual market opportunity.
