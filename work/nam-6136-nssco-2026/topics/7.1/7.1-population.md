## Density and distribution

### Density and distribution

Population density measures people per unit area; distribution describes where people live and how they are spread. A mean density can conceal concentrations. Density equals population divided by area, using comparable dates and boundaries.

### Population density

Population density is the number of people per unit area, commonly people per square kilometre. A fictional 12,000 residents over 300 km² gives 12,000 ÷ 300 = 40 people per km²; it does not mean each square kilometre contains 40 people.

### Population distribution

Population distribution is the spatial pattern showing where people live and how they are spread across an area. Describe clusters, sparse areas and relationships to locations using map evidence. A number per square kilometre does not describe the full spatial pattern.

### Apply the measure

A fictional district with 18,000 residents across 600 square kilometres has a mean density of 30 people per square kilometre. That calculation says nothing about whether the residents cluster around one town or live in scattered villages. A settlement map supplies distribution evidence, while journey information helps judge actual access to services.

## Explain settlement patterns

### Explain settlement patterns

Climate, relief, economic opportunities, services and political conditions can influence settlement. Southern African settlement is uneven; water and suitable terrain can support concentration, while jobs and infrastructure may attract people even where physical conditions are difficult. One factor seldom explains the whole pattern.

### Climate and relief

Water availability, temperature and terrain affect the feasibility of settlement and livelihoods. Gentler accessible land with usable water may support farming and building more readily than a dry steep area; infrastructure can modify some constraints.

### Economic factors

Jobs, markets and transport can draw people towards productive opportunities. A viable workplace near a usable route can encourage settlement; a mineral deposit without access or work opportunities need not attract a large population.

### Social factors

Schools, health facilities, family networks and other services can influence where people live. A service concentration may improve access, but housing and travel costs affect whether households can use it. Availability is different from equal access.

### Political factors

Safety, displacement, boundaries and public settlement decisions can affect population distribution. Conflict can force movement regardless of a place’s productive potential; safer conditions and usable rights can support return. Do not assume movements are freely chosen.

### Apply the measure

On an invented settlement map, six of nine settlements are near a railway and its service centres; three lie farther away. This is a concentration associated with transport and service access. It does not prove why each settlement formed. Historical activity, available water and land conditions may also help explain the pattern and should be investigated separately.

## Growth and rates

### Growth and rates

Population changes through births, deaths and net migration; comparable rates allow populations of different sizes to be assessed. Natural increase excludes migration. Higher population level differs from faster increase: compare differences or rates over equal intervals, not the largest final value alone.

### Growth rate

Population growth rate measures change in population over a period relative to its starting population, expressed as a percentage for that period. Use total change including migration where appropriate. A growth rate can fall while population still rises if the rate remains positive.

### Crude birth rate

Crude birth rate is live births during a year per thousand people in the population. Use births divided by the relevant population, multiplied by 1,000. It differs from births per woman and from a total number of births.

### Death rate

The crude death rate measures deaths during a year per thousand people in the population. A crude rate is influenced by age structure as well as health conditions, so a higher rate does not by itself prove worse services.

### Infant mortality

Infant mortality rate is deaths before the first birthday per thousand live births, for a stated period. Its denominator is live births, not total population; compare definitions and periods before interpreting a difference.

### Demographic transition model

The demographic transition model simplifies changes in birth and death rates and consequent natural population growth during development. The broad four-stage account moves from high birth/death rates, through falling deaths then falling births, towards low rates. It is a model, not an identical timetable for every country; migration is not its central mechanism.

### Apply the measure

Two invented populations change over the same five-year interval: C rises from 200,000 to 210,000, while D rises from 50,000 to 60,000. Both gain 10,000 people, but the percentage increases are 5% and 20%. Comparing size, absolute increase and percentage growth answers different questions; state which measure the judgement uses.

### Why birth and growth rates differ

Birth rates can remain high where children contribute to household livelihoods, families expect support in older age, early marriage lengthens the childbearing period, or high infant mortality leads parents to expect fewer surviving children. Limited access to education and voluntary family-planning information can restrict choices. Birth rates may fall with later marriage, better child survival, women’s education and wider work opportunities, accessible voluntary services, and the costs of raising children. These are interacting explanations, not stereotypes about a country or predictions about individuals. Death rates may fall as water, nutrition, sanitation and care improve. When deaths fall before births, natural increase rises; when births later fall towards deaths, growth slows. Net migration can still change total population growth, so natural increase is not the whole growth rate.

### Calculate natural increase and keep the units

Crude birth and death rates use events per 1,000 people per year. Natural increase is the birth rate minus the death rate, using the same population basis and period. In a fictional population, 30 births per 1,000 minus 10 deaths per 1,000 gives 20 per 1,000 natural increase. Divide 20 by 10 to express that as 2.0% per year. The symbol ‰ means per thousand; % means per hundred. Natural increase omits immigration and emigration. A growing population with positive natural increase can grow more slowly if that positive rate falls.

## Population policy

### Population policy

Policies may seek slower or faster growth through services, incentives or restrictions, but need evaluation against well-being and rights. A numerical target alone does not establish a beneficial outcome. Age structure, choices, implementation and long-term effects matter; historical examples must not be presented as current law.

### Reducing growth: a historical example

China’s historical one-child policy illustrates restrictions intended to reduce births; UNFPA recorded the move towards a two-child policy in 2015. Restrictions can affect births but create rights and family-welfare concerns. Evaluate voluntary information and services alongside any growth aim rather than endorsing coercion or assuming policy alone explains fertility.

### Increasing growth: a historical example

Singapore moved in 1987 from its earlier two-child approach towards encouraging larger families, as recorded by its National Library. Support with childcare, housing or parenting costs can address some barriers, but preferences, work and wider conditions mean an incentive need not produce the intended birth increase.

### Apply the measure

A fictional population programme combines confidential voluntary services with measures that keep learners in education. The service can address an immediate information or access need, while education may widen longer-term choices. Their effects occur through different pathways and need evidence over suitable periods. Respect for rights and genuine access are conditions of the programme, not optional costs to be traded away.

### Keep policy examples dated

[UNFPA’s 2015 statement](https://asiapacific.unfpa.org/en/news/statement-chinas-population-policy-changes) records China moving away from its earlier restriction. [Singapore’s National Library](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=1d106f7e-aca1-4c0e-ac7a-d35d0772707d) dates its pronatalist change to 1987. These illustrate differing policy aims; neither describes current rules. Checked 20 October 2026.

## Use population data

### Use population data

Population data can guide the location and capacity of services, employment planning and infrastructure. For a Namibian planning application, compare district age groups and access to schools or clinics rather than distributing capacity solely by national totals. Use the date and local denominators.

### Economic uses

Age and location data can inform workforce training, transport demand and markets. Counts of working-age residents do not directly measure available skills or job seekers; combine population structure with relevant labour information.

Applied example: Age and working-age numbers help plan future training and labour needs. The size and location of households help estimate demand for goods and services.

### Social uses

Children’s and older residents’ counts can inform school, health and care planning. Needs depend on location and actual access as well as age. A distant facility may offer less usable capacity than its place count suggests.

Applied example: Child population estimates help plan school places. The number and location of older residents help plan accessible health and support services.

### Apply the measure

A fictional district expects 360 children to need primary places and currently has 240 usable places, a gap of 120. A neighbouring district expects 180 children and has 200 places. The first has the larger capacity gap, but staff, classroom condition and journey distances still matter. A total population count alone would not reveal this age-specific need.

### Match data to a planning decision

Age and working-age numbers help estimate future training and labour needs. Household totals and locations help judge demand for goods and services. Child numbers support planning for school places; the location and number of older people support planning for accessible care. Use a relevant area and period and check denominators: a count of people can grow even when their percentage share falls.

## Population pyramids

### Population pyramids

A population pyramid shows age and sex composition, with age groups vertically and horizontal bars for each sex on a consistent count or percentage scale. A broad base indicates a relatively large young group; a narrower base and substantial older groups suggest a different age structure. Shape alone does not prove causes or future certainty.

### Older age structures

Many developed economies have relatively narrower young bases and larger older shares than youthful populations. These tendencies can inform care and workforce questions but do not describe every country or person. Compare the same age bands and units.

Applied example: A larger older-age share can increase demand for pensions and accessible care. A smaller working-age share can face greater support responsibilities relative to its size.

### Youthful age structures

A youthful pyramid has a relatively broad young base compared with older age groups. A large school-age share can imply future education demand, while future births also depend on survival, migration and fertility choices; do not infer inevitable rapid growth from shape alone.

Applied example: Many children increase present demand for schools and child services. As those cohorts reach working age, more employment and further training opportunities may be needed.

### Apply the measure

This fictional worked example uses percentages of each population’s total, not percentages within each sex. Population C has 36% aged 0–14, 56% aged 15–64 and 8% aged 65 or over. Population D has 24%, 58% and 18% in the same bands. C has the larger young share; D has the larger older share. These differences can inform school and older-age service planning, without implying that everyone in either age group has identical needs.

### Read and construct the shape

The complete age-sex table for this worked example is: C, 0–14 male 18% female 18%, 15–64 male 28% female 28%, 65+ male 4% female 4%; D, 0–14 male 12% female 12%, 15–64 male 29% female 29%, 65+ male 9% female 9%. Each population totals 100%. Draw age groups vertically, youngest at the bottom, with males left and females right on equal horizontal percentage scales. C’s bar lengths on each side are 18%, 28% and 4%; D’s are 12%, 29% and 9%, in the same age order. Add a title, age labels, units and sex key. These broad age groups show grouped shares rather than exact year-by-year patterns. Use finer equal-width age bands for detailed age comparisons. No unseen plotted image is claimed verified.

### Explain a pyramid before planning services

A broad base indicates a large young share, commonly associated with relatively high recent births; it does not prove one cause. A youthful population can increase demand for schools, child services and later jobs. A higher older-age share can increase demand for accessible health care, pensions and support services, while a smaller working-age share may face greater support responsibilities. Numbers matter as well as percentages: the same age share in a larger population means more people. Check cohort size, migration and changing births or survival before using a pyramid to explain a trend.

## HIV, AIDS and population

### HIV, AIDS and population

HIV is a virus affecting the immune system; AIDS is an advanced stage of untreated HIV infection. Effective treatment and accessible support change health outcomes. Illness and premature deaths can affect household care, age patterns and livelihoods, but do not assume HIV means immediate death or inability to work. Health and population judgements need evidence and non-stigmatising language.

### Apply the measure

In a fictional service review, continuity of HIV care improves while reported illness-related absence declines. This association can inform investigation of treatment access, but changes in recording or workplace conditions may also matter. Population and employment effects require consistent denominators and dates. People living with HIV should not be treated as a uniform group or excluded on assumptions about their capability.

### Health evidence

[WHO’s HIV fact sheet](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hiv-aids), checked 20 October 2026, distinguishes HIV from AIDS and explains the role of effective treatment. Ordinary social contact does not transmit HIV. This is conceptual health education, not an individual diagnosis or treatment instruction.
