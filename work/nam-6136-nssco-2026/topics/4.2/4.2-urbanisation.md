## Urbanisation is a rising share

### Urbanisation is a rising share

Urbanisation is an increase in the proportion of a population living in urban areas; distinguish it from an increase in urban population alone. Urban share is urban population divided by total population, multiplied by 100; a larger urban number does not prove a larger share. Use consistent urban definitions and a stated area and period. In a fictional country, 400,000 of 1,000,000 people are urban: 40%. Later, 600,000 of 2,000,000 are urban: 30%. The urban number rises by 200,000, yet its share falls by 10 percentage points. These figures therefore do not show urbanisation over that interval. A percentage-point change compares the shares directly; it is different from percentage growth in the urban count.

### Calculate the share before naming the trend

For a separate fictional calculation, 360,000 urban residents among 900,000 total gives 360,000 ÷ 900,000 = 0.4. Multiplying by 100 gives 40%. For a trend, compare at least two periods: if the next comparable share is 45%, the increase is five percentage points. Topic 7.1 explains population measures. Urbanisation concerns the changing urban share, urban growth the urban number; check which the prompt asks for.

### Connect causes and responses

Use the denominator and a comparable definition before describing urbanisation. Population in Topic 7.1 supplies related measures.

## Causes and characteristics

### Causes and characteristics

Rural–urban migration, different rates of natural population change and changes in settlement classification can affect the urban share. People may move in response to rural constraints and perceived urban opportunities, but migration and urbanisation are not interchangeable concepts. A push factor encourages departure, such as loss of a livelihood; a pull factor attracts people, such as access to work or education. A perceived opportunity may not become an actual job. Births and deaths in urban and rural populations also affect their relative sizes. Changing a boundary can alter measured shares without people physically moving, so compare definitions before drawing conclusions.

### Industrialised-country patterns

Many industrialised economies experienced substantial earlier rural–urban movement associated with industrial employment and service concentration. Where a large share is already urban, further growth, suburban movement or movement away from city centres can follow different patterns. These are broad historical tendencies, not current country rankings or rules for every resident. Movement from one urban area to another can change a city’s population without changing the national urban share. A crowded city alone does not show how quickly a country is urbanising. Use the supplied dates and denominators.

### Developing-country patterns

Some developing economies experience rapid urban growth where migration and natural increase combine with a growing urban share. If housing, services and work opportunities expand more slowly than needs, existing access gaps can widen. The rate and form vary between countries and settlements. Urban residents can benefit from concentrated markets, schools and services; rapid growth creates pressure when provision cannot keep pace. Avoid describing urban growth as an automatic cause of poverty or assuming that all residents have the same experience.

### Read a pattern rather than assume one

In an original fictional comparison, Area A’s urban share rises from 30% to 45%, with an annual net rural–urban inflow of 5,000; Area B’s share rises from 70% to 72%, with an inflow of 500. Both urbanise, but A has the larger percentage-point rise and supplied inflow. These observations support a contrast, not proof that migration caused the whole change. The periods and definitions must match. Topic 7.1 develops natural change and migration; do not confuse a higher starting level with faster urbanisation.

### Connect causes and responses

Industrialisation in Topic 4.1 explains some work opportunities. Rural development in Topic 5.1 explains opportunities at origins. Neither turns all migration into one simple cause.

## When needs outpace provision

### When needs outpace provision

Rapid urban growth can place pressure on housing, water, sanitation, transport and employment where provision does not expand with needs. Problems depend on service capacity, location and access as well as population numbers; residents are not themselves the cause of poor planning or exclusion. In an invented settlement, households increase from 500 to 900 while functioning water points remain at five. Households per point rise from 100 to 180. The additional demand can lengthen queues if supply and use conditions are similar. The ratio identifies pressure, not the measured waiting time or an explanation of why services were not expanded.

### Effects on rural areas

Departure can reduce available rural labour or care support, while remittances and new connections can also support rural households. The net effect depends on who leaves, when work is needed, whether earnings are available and whether money or knowledge returns. In a fictional village, several experienced workers leave before planting and remaining households cannot hire replacements. A labour shortage may delay planting and reduce production. In a different case, earnings sent home might finance tools or hired help. Do not assume departure has only a cost or only a benefit.

### Housing needs and informal settlements

When affordable housing and serviced land are insufficient, some residents build or occupy housing outside formal planning or tenure arrangements. Informal settlements vary; inadequate infrastructure or insecure access can affect living conditions, but a settlement label does not describe every household. The syllabus term shanty towns refers to a form of low-quality informal housing. Use respectful descriptions of the actual shelter, service and access conditions. In an invented town, rents exceed new workers’ earnings and no serviced low-cost plots are available. Self-built housing near work can meet an immediate shelter need while leaving water and sanitation unresolved.

### Sanitation pressure

Sanitation concerns safe management of human waste, and insufficient capacity can leave waste poorly contained or treated. When demand exceeds functioning provision, contamination of the living environment becomes a greater concern. In a fictional settlement, household numbers rise but toilets and waste-removal capacity do not. Overflowing facilities and uncollected waste show a service gap rather than proof of residents’ habits. A response needs usable facilities, maintenance and a workable disposal route, not construction alone.

### Water supply and pollution

Water provision depends on a safe and accessible supply, functioning distribution and management of demand and contamination. A damaged or overloaded system can combine access difficulties with pollution risks where waste reaches the supply. In an invented neighbourhood, households rely on a shared water point beside an overflowing drain, and several supply interruptions force collection from an unprotected alternative. Both reliability and protection matter. More pipe capacity would not by itself address waste reaching the collection area; drainage management alone would not restore missing supply.

### Transport and communication

Transport connects residents with work and services, while communication supports access to information and coordination. Growth at poorly connected edges can increase journey time and costs and limit the opportunities residents can actually reach. In a fictional settlement, homes expand beyond a bus route and workers walk far before boarding an already full bus. Frequent late arrivals can threaten access to work even when vacancies exist. A route extension, frequency improvement or better connection should be judged against distance, capacity and affordability rather than road length alone.

### Develop the mechanism

Take a new fictional case: a town adds 200 households but its water points and sanitation capacity are unchanged. Greater demand can lengthen collection queues and overload waste facilities, creating linked access and environmental problems. To explain the effect, use both the increase in demand and the unchanged capacity. Do not invent measured illness rates or queue times. Rural household effects and housing costs are separate mechanisms that also need their own evidence. Topic 7.2 develops the health context; this lesson identifies infrastructure and access pressures.

### Connect causes and responses

Link each observed service or livelihood constraint to the relevant mechanism and affected people; avoid blaming residents or assuming identical experiences.

## Responses must fit the barrier

### Responses must fit the barrier

Possible urban-growth responses include self-help housing, site-and-services schemes, rural development and planned new towns. These responses address different mechanisms and require resources, participation and continuing services; judge them by delivered access and functioning services. An urgent water-service failure needs a different first response from a shortage of job opportunities or serviced residential land. Identify affected residents, feasibility and an implementer. Compare improvements to existing areas with relocation: a distant site may solve a land constraint while making jobs less accessible. The stated criterion should guide the choice.

### Self-help schemes

Self-help housing involves residents contributing labour, decisions or resources to improve or build shelter, usually with suitable supporting arrangements. It may use local capability and staged improvements but can exclude people lacking time, physical capacity, finance or secure access. An invented community has willing builders and appropriate materials but needs technical support and a safe service connection. Supported self-help may fit those capabilities. Requiring households already working long hours to build unaided is a different proposition. Participation should be feasible and service responsibilities clear; self-help does not mean abandoning residents to provide everything.

### Site and services schemes

A site-and-services scheme provides planned plots with essential supporting services or access arrangements, with housing often developed in stages. It can address a serviced-land shortage, but affordability, location, secure usable access and continued service maintenance determine who benefits. A plot without a practical work connection can increase travel costs. A plot price above residents’ means leaves the access problem unresolved. Compare real household capabilities and the site’s services rather than treating a grid of plots as completed housing provision. This is a conceptual scheme, not a statement of current local allocation law.

### Rural development projects

Rural development can improve livelihoods, services and opportunities in rural areas. It can expand genuine choices and reduce some pressures to migrate, but does not automatically reverse urbanisation or replace urban service provision. Repairing a farm-market connection may improve the viability of rural work where products and buyers exist. People can still prefer or need to move for other reasons. Judge the programme by rural well-being and opportunity, not by treating movement itself as a failure. Topic 5.1 develops the productive strategies in more depth.

### New towns

New towns are planned settlements intended to provide an organised location for housing, work and services. They can distribute activity and provide planned infrastructure, but require substantial coordination, finance and real links between homes, jobs and services. In a fictional proposal, serviced housing is available far from employment without affordable transport. Relocation may shift rather than solve the access problem. A town with credible work opportunities and maintained services has a stronger basis. Compare timing and costs with improving existing settlements; a planned name does not establish a complete community.

### Compare an urgent and a longer response

An invented existing settlement has an urgent water-access gap. A funded extension can connect it to nearby usable supply; a new town would take several years and its job connections are unresolved. The extension better fits the immediate access criterion, while long-term housing planning remains relevant. In a different case, a well-connected serviced site may solve a land shortage more directly. A recommendation names the implementer, the practical action and why it fits the evidence. Local authorities, service providers and residents can have different responsibilities; do not imply residents alone must finance a network.

### Connect causes and responses

Match the time horizon and access problem to a feasible response. Rural Development in Topic 5.1 complements urban provision rather than replacing it.

## Two different classifications

### Two different classifications

Employment status concerns whether and how much someone works; formal or informal economic activity concerns institutional and regulatory arrangements. An informal economic activity is not an informal settlement, which describes a residential area; neither label alone determines a person’s employment status. A person can work in a registered business while living in an informal settlement. Another can carry out informal economic work elsewhere. Test whether the evidence describes work, registration or housing. The classifications can be combined rather than substituted for each other.

### Unemployment and underemployment

Unemployment is the situation of people without work who are available for and seeking work; exact statistical definitions depend on the source. Underemployment means having work that uses less than a person’s available working time or productive capacity; it differs from having no work. An invented worker seeking work but with no job is distinguished from a worker with six paid hours who is available for more. State what is measured; not everyone without a job is counted as unemployed, for example if unavailable for work under the stated definition. A low-hours example illustrates time-related underemployment without claiming that every part-time worker is underemployed.

### Formal and informal sector

Formal economic activity operates within the relevant recognised registration and regulatory arrangements; informal activity operates outside some of them. The exact statistical boundary depends on the source; informal does not by itself mean unemployed, illegal or located in an informal settlement. An invented unregistered repair activity still produces a service and can provide work and income. Its institutional classification says nothing by itself about the quality of the worker’s home. A registered shop may also offer few hours, so formal status does not establish adequate employment. Use the definition and relevant evidence together.

### An original work comparison

In a fictional survey of 100 people available for work, 30 have no work, 40 have fewer paid hours than they are available for, and 30 have their desired hours. The unemployed total is 30. The employed total is 70 (40 underemployed + 30 with their desired hours). People with unmet work needs total 70 (30 unemployed + 40 underemployed). These two totals overlap because the 40 underemployed people are included in the employed total; do not add the totals together. Calling all 70 with unmet work needs unemployed would erase the distinction. A separate registration table could classify their economic activities; their housing location would not answer it. Topic 3.1 explains labour as a productive input; here classify work status and activity correctly.

### Connect causes and responses

Keep work, institutional registration and residential location separate. An informal settlement can contain people in different work situations.
