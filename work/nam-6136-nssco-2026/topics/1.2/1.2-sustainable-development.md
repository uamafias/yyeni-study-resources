## Present needs and future opportunities

### Present needs and future opportunities

Sustainable development is development that meets present needs without undermining future generations' ability to meet their own needs. Present needs include adequate food, water, shelter and opportunities to earn a living. Future generations also need a functioning environment and usable resources. The idea connects these needs: keeping people poor is not a satisfactory way to protect a resource, and meeting needs today by destroying tomorrow's productive base is not a lasting solution.

### A water-use decision

Consider an invented settlement whose shared water source supports households and a small irrigated garden. During a dry period, unrestricted garden pumping leaves too little water for households and risks exhausting the available supply. A locally agreed allocation could reserve essential household water, limit pumping to a monitored amount and reduce avoidable losses from leaking pipes. The proposal addresses a present need without assuming that unlimited extraction can continue. Its success depends on a realistic water assessment, workable rules and maintenance. Installing a pump alone does not show that the water use is sustainable.

### Ask what lasts and who benefits

A proposal should identify the need it meets, the resource or condition it depends on and how that resource will remain usable. Benefits also need to reach the people whose needs are being discussed. A project that protects water while excluding households from essential access leaves an important development problem unresolved. Explain the trade-off with a concrete mechanism rather than labelling an activity sustainable because it sounds environmentally friendly.

### Connect to development and production

Topic 1.1 explains the economic, social, political and environmental dimensions. Topic 3.1 explains production and resource use. Sustainability asks whether improvements in those dimensions can continue while resources and living conditions are protected.

### Sustainable Development Goals

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) combine economic, social and environmental pillars—livelihoods, well-being and natural systems—with ambitions for 2030. Examples are SDG 1, No Poverty; 2, Zero Hunger; 3, Good Health and Well-being; 4, Quality Education; 5, Gender Equality; 6, Clean Water and Sanitation; 8, Decent Work and Economic Growth; 12, Responsible Consumption and Production; and 13, Climate Action. Climate-related drought and flooding threaten crops, water and infrastructure, undermining livelihoods and development gains. Suitable adaptation reduces vulnerability, while reducing emissions limits further warming and protects future needs. A particular local event cannot be attributed to climate change merely because it occurred.

## Regeneration and finite stocks

### Regeneration and finite stocks

A renewable resource can replenish on a human timescale under suitable conditions. A woodland can regrow, but cutting trees faster than they regenerate can still deplete it. A non-renewable resource, such as a mineral deposit, has a finite stock that is not replaced on that timescale. Reducing waste, recovering usable materials and avoiding unnecessary extraction conserve that stock. The separating test is whether the resource itself can regenerate within the relevant human timescale, not whether people can use it repeatedly.

### An original woodland example

In a fictional community woodland, an estimate suggests 100 trees replace losses each year under existing conditions, but 160 are cut annually. That is a net loss of 60 trees a year if those rates persist. Because wood is renewable, controlling cutting, protecting regrowth and reducing fuel waste can help maintain it. Calling it renewable does not make the excessive extraction harmless. Regeneration depends on conditions such as surviving young trees and adequate growing conditions; a single estimate should be monitored rather than treated as a permanent guarantee.

### Farming is one application

Sustainable agriculture concerns maintaining farming and its resource base, for example by protecting soil from erosion and using water carefully. Sustainable development is broader: it also concerns services, employment and other activities. To separate the concepts, ask whether the statement specifically concerns farming practices or a wider development principle. A soil-protection example illustrates sustainability; it does not make the entire concept a farming definition.

### Follow the resource through use

Rural development in Topic 5.1 examines farming in more detail. Here, describe the resource, the way it is used and the effect on future availability. Conservation measures should fit whether the problem is loss of regeneration, waste of a finite stock or damage to the wider environment.

## Responsible consumption and production

### Responsible consumption and production

Responsible consumption and production concern how resources are obtained, used and discarded. The development goal is to meet needs while reducing avoidable waste and damage. Producers can prevent material losses, design repairable products and manage waste. Consumers can avoid unnecessary purchases, maintain goods and choose suitable reusable alternatives. Recycling recovers materials after use; preventing waste avoids the loss in the first place. None of these labels proves that a particular option is best without examining how it works.

### Compare a workable alternative

An invented school serves drinking water at events. One option uses 200 single-use cups each week. A second uses durable cups, available storage and an existing safe washing arrangement. If the durable cups are repeatedly used and maintained, they can reduce the flow of discarded cups. They also require an initial purchase, washing and replacement when damaged. The stated resources make reuse feasible in this case; where safe washing is unavailable, that constraint must be solved before recommending the same arrangement. A useful judgement weighs the relevant benefits and costs against the aim rather than listing unrelated advantages.

### Prevent loss in production

A fictional tailoring group discards large usable fabric offcuts while buying new fabric for small bags. Sorting and using suitable offcuts for those bags could reduce new-material demand and waste without reducing the number of bags made. It requires suitable sizes and a workable sorting routine. The responsible choice follows the actual loss identified; a general instruction to recycle would be less precise.

### Judge by evidence

Production in Topic 3.1 connects inputs to outputs. Compare resource use for the same need or output, include the practical conditions, and make a supported judgement. A reduction in waste is useful evidence, not proof that every social or environmental effect has been resolved.

### SDG 12 in everyday production

SDG 12 concerns responsible consumption and production. Reusing suitable fabric offcuts can reduce the need for new material and reduce waste. SDG 8 concerns decent work and economic growth; a viable product can connect to this goal when it supports decent paid work. Goals can interact, but a local action does not by itself establish progress against a global goal.
