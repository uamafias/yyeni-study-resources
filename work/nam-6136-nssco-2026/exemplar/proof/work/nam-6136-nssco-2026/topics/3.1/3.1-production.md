## Capital distinctions

### Finance and productive assets

Money capital is finance available for production. Capital goods are produced assets used in production. A sewing group might hold N$600 in a cash box and use a sewing machine to make school bags. The cash can finance cloth, transport or equipment. The machine performs a productive task. Counting both as the same kind of resource hides the decision the group faces: having a machine does not mean it has money for cloth, and having money does not mean it already has a suitable machine.

### A choice with a reason

Consider an invented workshop near Otjiwarongo. Its machine works, but production has stopped because there is no cloth. A new machine would add productive equipment without removing that immediate shortage. Finance for cloth would allow the existing equipment to be used. If the machine instead breaks and cannot be repaired, the priority changes: the workshop needs access to replacement equipment as well as the money or credit to obtain it. The practical choice follows the bottleneck, rather than a rule that money or machines are more useful in every setting.

### Reading evidence about equipment

An invented sewing group records output before and after obtaining a machine. In week one it makes 30 bags using hand tools; in week two it makes 50 using the machine. In this example the equipment is associated with 20 additional bags. That comparison alone does not prove the machine caused the whole change: the hours worked, worker numbers, material supply and type of bag must also be comparable. A careful description first identifies the change, then states what the evidence can and cannot establish. More equipment is useful where it fits the productive task and can be operated and maintained.
