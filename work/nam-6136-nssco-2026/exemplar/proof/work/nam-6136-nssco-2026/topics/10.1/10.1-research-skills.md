## Applying a pilot study

### Trying a questionnaire before the main enquiry

A pilot study is a small preliminary trial. It helps a researcher discover practical problems before using the procedure throughout an enquiry. A learner researching journeys to a community water point can try a draft questionnaire with a few people similar to the intended respondents. The learner checks whether they understand the questions, whether response categories fit their experiences and whether the instrument records the information needed for the research aim. The trial should help improve the procedure; repeating a definition of a pilot study does not describe what the researcher actually did.

### A wording problem and its repair

In an invented enquiry, six volunteers try a question asking whether their water journey takes a long time. Four ask what long means. The phrase gives them no common measurement rule, so their answers may not be comparable. The learner records that difficulty and replaces the question with a request for the number of minutes for a return journey, including waiting. A second small trial checks whether the new instruction is understood and can be answered. This is a change to the instrument prompted by evidence, rather than a claim that a small trial guarantees reliable results.

### What the trial does not establish

Six volunteers can reveal confusing wording, but their answers cannot establish the travel-time pattern for a whole settlement. The pilot sample is chosen to test procedures, while the main sampling plan must address the enquiry population and aim. Record what changed and why, then describe the revised collection method clearly enough for another learner to follow it. In the final report, distinguish findings from the main collection from observations made while testing the instrument. If the trial identifies a sensitive question, revise the approach with the supervising teacher before the main collection.
