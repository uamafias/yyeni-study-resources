## Accounting for planning and control

### Accounting for planning and control

The accounting function records, organises and reports financial information. Planning uses that information to set budgets, forecast cash needs and compare possible uses of resources. Control compares actual results with plans, identifies differences and helps managers investigate their causes. A cost overrun might reflect higher material prices, more output or waste; the figure alone does not reveal which. Accurate records let managers distinguish those explanations before changing prices, suppliers or work methods. Financial statements also communicate results and position to users outside management. Historical figures do not guarantee future performance, so forecasts must state assumptions. A budget is a planned figure, not an actual receipt or permission to ignore service quality. Accounting supports decisions by supplying relevant evidence; it does not make every management choice on its own.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Oshi Pots budgets N$8,000 for monthly clay purchases but spends N$10,000 while producing more pots than planned. The accountant should compare quantities and prices, not conclude that the N$2,000 increase is entirely waste. Management can revise buying and production plans after identifying the cause. The control loop is plan, record actual results, compare, investigate and act.

## Classifying costs without mixing the questions

### Classifying costs without mixing the questions

Fixed and variable classify cost behaviour as output changes in the relevant period. Fixed total cost does not vary with output within the stated range; total variable cost changes with output. Direct and indirect classify whether a cost can be traced to a particular product or department. A direct cost is identifiable with that cost object; an indirect cost, or overhead, is shared and cannot be directly traced to it. In simple examples direct materials are variable and rent is fixed overhead, but the two pairs answer different questions and are not universal synonyms. Unit or average cost divides total cost by total output. Marginal cost is the extra cost from producing one more unit. Total cost is the full amount for the stated output, not an amount per item. Each measure has a purpose: a manager comparing total expenditure needs total cost, whereas pricing or efficiency comparisons may require unit cost. State the output and time period so that two figures are comparable.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Rani Crates has monthly rent N$2,000 and timber costing N$30 per crate. At 100 crates, assume these are its complete production costs. Rent is fixed over the stated output range; timber is variable and traceable to crates. Total cost is N$5,000 and average cost is N$50 per crate. One extra crate costs N$30 if no extra fixed cost arises, so marginal cost is N$30, distinct from average cost.

### Fixed costs

Fixed costs do not vary with the items produced or sold in the short term within the stated operating range. Rent is a common example. The total stays fixed as output changes over that range, but fixed cost per unit falls as the same amount is spread over more units. Beyond the range, extra premises or machinery can change total fixed cost. Fixed describes behaviour, not whether a cost can be traced to a product.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Oru Packs pays rent N$3,000 per month whether it makes 100 or 150 packs. The total rent remains N$3,000. Rent per pack falls from N$30 to N$20 across those outputs; a fixed total is not a fixed cost per item.

### Variable costs

Variable costs change in total as output changes. In a simple constant-rate model, each extra item requires a given amount of material, so total material cost rises with the number produced while cost per item stays constant. A changing input price or wastage rate can alter this relationship, so state the model assumptions. Variable is distinct from direct, which concerns traceability to a product or department.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Iwa Bags uses N$12 fabric per bag and makes 100 bags, then 150, with no price or usage change. Fabric cost rises from N$1,200 to N$1,800 while remaining N$12 per bag. The total changes with output; it is not a fixed N$12 bill for the business.

### Direct costs

Direct costs are costs that can be directly related to or identified with a particular product or department. Direct material is a simple example. The key is traceability: ask which product or department caused the cost and whether it can be identified without a shared allocation. Direct materials often vary with output, but direct/indirect and fixed/variable are different classifications. A fixed hire payment for equipment used solely on one identified project can be direct to that project.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Lela Doors buys timber specifically for an identified door order. The timber can be traced to that order, making it direct to the order. Its variable behaviour is a separate description of how the amount changes with output.

### Indirect costs

An indirect cost is a cost which cannot be directly related to a particular product. Such costs are often called overhead costs. Shared factory rent is indirect to individual product lines and may need allocation if managers want a full cost for each line. Indirect does not mean unnecessary. Many simple overheads are fixed, but an indirect cost can change with activity; the category concerns traceability, not an automatic fixed amount.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Tano Furnishings makes desks and chairs in one rented workshop, with no separate rented areas identified for each. The shared rent is indirect to the product lines. Charging its full amount to every desk and again to every chair would double count rather than allocate a shared cost.

### Unit costs

Unit cost is the cost of producing one item, i.e. total cost of production divided by total output. It expresses the average cost per item at the stated output, not necessarily the cost of the next extra item. Include the relevant full production costs in total cost and keep currency per unit distinct from a total money amount. Compare the same type and quality of output where possible.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Noa Trays has total production cost N$9,000 for 300 trays. Unit cost is N$30 per tray. N$9,000 is total cost, while N$30 is cost per item. That average alone does not establish the marginal cost of making tray 301.

### Average costs

Average cost is total cost divided by output. For a single type of product it is the same average-per-item concept as unit cost. It can change with the output level even when the marginal variable cost is constant, because fixed costs are spread over different quantities. Lower average cost with expansion requires relevant conditions, such as capacity use and controlled coordination costs; diseconomies can offset savings as the business grows.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Kela Cups has total cost N$4,000 at 100 cups and N$5,000 at 200 cups. Average cost falls from N$40 per cup to N$25 per cup. The increase in total cost is N$1,000 over 100 extra cups, which is different from either average figure.

### Marginal costs

Marginal costs are extra costs which a business will incur by producing one more unit of output. If output increases by several units, the change in total cost divided by the output change gives the average additional cost over that interval; it equals the per-extra-unit marginal cost only under the stated constant-increment assumption. Marginal cost is useful for examining an additional unit but does not by itself recover the business’s full fixed commitments.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Teta Tiles has total cost N$8,000 at 200 tiles and N$8,025 at 201 tiles. The extra tile adds N$25, so its marginal cost is N$25. Average cost at 200 tiles is N$40; the marginal and average figures answer different questions.

### Total costs

Total cost is the full cost of producing the stated output over a period. In the simple fixed-and-variable model it is total fixed cost plus total variable cost. Do not add a per-unit variable cost directly to a total fixed-cost bill: first multiply the per-unit amount by output. Total cost carries a money unit, whereas average and marginal cost carry money per item. Its completeness depends on which costs the situation includes.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Aro Boxes has fixed costs N$2,000 and variable cost N$10 per box for 300 boxes. Assume these are all its production costs. Total variable cost is N$3,000 and total cost N$5,000. The N$10 is an input rate per box, not the full variable bill or the total cost.

### Worked practice: Financial evidence and judgement

Fictional Ema Cabinets reports annual net profit N$30,000, capital employed N$150,000, total assets N$200,000 and total liabilities N$80,000. Its current ratio is 1.80:1 and quick ratio 0.90:1. A supplier is deciding whether to offer short credit. Debtors are currently expected to pay before the proposed supplier bill falls due. The owner is willing to share current cash-flow information. Ema has fixed workshop rent and timber costs that vary with output. For part (b) only, the largest debtor now says payment will arrive two months after the proposed supplier bill is due. All reported profit and asset figures remain unchanged.

a Evaluate whether Ema’s supplier should offer the proposed credit under the original facts. Use the ratios, relevant cost behaviour and the interests of different statement users. [12]
b Evaluate the credit decision after the debtor delay. Explain whether the earlier judgement changes. [12]

a Ema’s ROCE is 20.00% and solvency ratio 2.50:1, useful to an owner assessing return and a lender assessing total asset cover. A supplier needs short-term receipts: quick ratio 0.90:1 shows non-inventory coverage below current liabilities, but expected debtor receipts before the bill and an available current forecast can support a limited credit offer. Fixed rent and variable timber payments both compete for the receipts. Check the forecast, existing commitments and the individual due amounts, then offer a controlled limit if payment is credible; the annual profit alone cannot justify it. b The delayed largest receipt undermines the key cash-timing support while profit, ROCE and book asset cover remain unchanged. A future profitable result cannot pay the earlier bill without cash from another stated source. The supplier should now postpone or reduce credit, seek payment on delivery or require evidence of an agreed alternative cash source. This changes the original conditional willingness because receipt timing, rather than annual profitability, was decisive. Do not invent a new overdraft to preserve the original answer.

### Worked practice: Financial evidence and judgement

Fictional Ema Cabinets reports annual net profit N$30,000, capital employed N$150,000, total assets N$200,000 and total liabilities N$80,000. Its current ratio is 1.80:1 and quick ratio 0.90:1. A supplier is deciding whether to offer short credit. Debtors are currently expected to pay before the proposed supplier bill falls due. The owner is willing to share current cash-flow information. Ema has fixed workshop rent and timber costs that vary with output.

Evaluate whether Ema’s supplier should use profitability or liquidity evidence as its primary basis for the credit decision. Give a concise plan, including the owner’s different interest and one cost distinction. [12]

Knowledge: profit measures performance; liquidity concerns short-term payment; solvency compares total assets and total liabilities. ROCE is 30,000 / 150,000 × 100, giving 20.00%, relevant to an owner’s return but not sufficient for a supplier’s due date. Evidence: current ratio 1.80:1 falls to quick ratio 0.90:1, so part of coverage depends on inventory. Debtors are expected before the bill and the owner offers cash-flow evidence, which may support credit after verification. Costs: workshop rent is fixed in the stated range while timber varies with output; future payments for both still matter to cash. Judgement: prioritise current liquidity and cash timing for short credit, use profitability and asset cover as supporting evidence and verify the forecast. An owner may give return more weight, but cannot ignore liquidity that threatens continuity.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why average cost N$40 per pot is not the marginal cost of one extra pot. Fictional Yara Pots has total cost N$4,000 at 100 pots and N$4,025 at 101 pots.

Average cost at 100 pots divides total cost by output, giving N$40 per pot. The extra pot adds N$25 to total cost, so its marginal cost is N$25. Average includes the allocation of all costs across output; marginal focuses on the extra unit.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why fictional Rula Works’ rent is an expense but its net profit is not a type of fixed cost. Its rent is unchanged over the stated output range.

Rent is a cost charged against income and is fixed in the stated range because its total does not vary with output. Net profit is the residual after relevant costs and expenses are deducted from income. Income/expense describes financial performance categories; fixed/variable describes cost behaviour. A profit result cannot be reclassified as fixed merely because a rent bill is fixed.

## Linking the accounting concepts

### Linking the accounting concepts

Income and expenses describe financial performance over a period, while assets, liabilities and owner’s equity describe financial position at a date. Gross profit is sales revenue less cost of sales; net profit is gross profit plus other income less other expenses in a simple trading statement. Owner’s equity is the residual interest after liabilities are deducted from assets. Drawings withdraw value for a sole owner’s personal use and reduce equity; they are not an operating expense. Capital employed is the long-term finance in use, measured here as owner’s capital plus non-current liabilities. Employment of capital concerns where finance is invested in assets, rather than the number of employees. Working capital is current assets less current liabilities. A loan creates cash and a liability, not trading income. A machine purchase exchanges one asset for another rather than making its full price an immediate ordinary expense in this simple distinction. Keep the type of transaction clear before deciding where it belongs.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri Traders: annual turnover N$240,000, cost of sales N$144,000, other expenses N$72,000, credit sales N$219,000 and credit purchases N$146,000. At year end: non-current assets N$90,000; inventory N$30,000; debtors N$18,000; cash N$12,000; current liabilities N$30,000 (including creditors N$20,000); non-current liabilities N$40,000; owner’s equity N$80,000. Opening inventory was N$18,000. Use the opening and closing inventory average for inventory turnover. No other income or expense is assumed in this simple example. Neri has gross profit N$96,000 and net profit N$24,000. Its total assets are N$150,000 and total liabilities N$70,000, leaving equity N$80,000. Capital employed is N$120,000 and working capital N$30,000. The same business therefore has several different financial measures, each answering a different question.

### Income

Income is value earned by the business during a period, including sales revenue and any other specified income. In a trading example turnover is sales revenue. Income need not be received immediately in cash: a credit sale can create a debtor. A new owner contribution or loan receipt supplies finance but is not income earned from selling goods or services.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Eta Goods makes N$8,000 credit sales and borrows N$5,000. The sales are trading income; the loan is finance creating a liability. Treating both as sales would overstate performance.

### Expenses

Expenses are costs charged against income for a period when measuring profit. Examples include rent, wages and interest for that period. Paying cash is not enough to classify a transaction as an expense: buying a machine creates an asset and repaying loan principal reduces a liability. A cost can be charged as an expense before or after the related cash payment.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Lara Prints pays monthly rent N$2,000 and buys equipment costing N$10,000. Rent is an expense for the month. The equipment purchase is an asset acquisition; it is not automatically a N$10,000 ordinary monthly expense.

### Gross profit

Gross profit is sales revenue less cost of sales for the period. Cost of sales concerns the goods sold, not all operating expenses. In a simple trader it is opening inventory plus purchases less closing inventory, using the figures specified. Gross profit is a money amount; gross profit margin expresses it relative to sales as a percentage.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri has annual turnover N$240,000 and cost of sales N$144,000. Gross profit is N$96,000. Rent and other expenses still need to be considered before finding net profit.

### Net profit

Net profit is the surplus after the relevant expenses have been deducted from income. In the simple trading model, start with gross profit, add other income if any and deduct other expenses. A loss occurs when relevant expenses exceed income. Net profit is not the business’s cash balance and is distinct from net profit margin, which relates it to turnover.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri has gross profit N$96,000 and other expenses N$72,000, with no other income. Net profit is N$24,000. This does not prove that N$24,000 is immediately available to spend because income and payments can have different timing.

### Capital employed

Capital employed is the long-term finance in use in the business. In Annexe A it is owner’s capital plus long-term liabilities; with a consistent Statement of Financial Position it also equals total assets less current liabilities. It is the denominator for ROCE in this syllabus. Use year-end figures unless instructed otherwise, rather than silently substituting a different average.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri has owner’s equity N$80,000 and non-current liabilities N$40,000. Capital employed is N$120,000. Its N$30,000 current liabilities are not added to this long-term-finance definition.

### Employment of capital

Employment of capital concerns how the business uses finance in assets and operations. Sources explain where finance comes from; employment explains where it is invested, for example machinery, inventory and receivables. It does not mean the number of jobs created. A productive asset base can still leave insufficient immediately available cash if funds are tied up in slow-moving stock.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri holds non-current assets N$90,000 and current assets N$60,000. These identify uses of resources, while owner’s equity and liabilities explain financing claims. A machine is employment of finance, not a new provider of finance.

### Owner’s equity

Owner’s equity is the owner’s residual interest in the business’s assets after deducting liabilities. The accounting relationship is assets less liabilities equals equity. Contributions and retained profit can increase it, while drawings reduce it in a sole-owner example. Equity is not necessarily cash: the corresponding assets may include equipment and amounts customers owe.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri has total assets N$150,000 and total liabilities N$70,000. Equity is N$80,000. The owner cannot infer that the business has N$80,000 cash from this residual figure.

### Drawings

Drawings are money or other value taken from the business by its sole owner for personal use. They reduce owner’s equity and, for cash drawings, reduce business cash. They are not an ordinary business expense incurred to earn sales, so they do not reduce trading profit in the same way as rent or wages. Distinguish owner withdrawals from a payment to an employee for work.

### Putting the idea to work

A fictional sole trader takes N$2,000 from business cash to buy personal groceries. Record drawings and reduced cash, not wages or cost of sales. Calling it an expense would misstate the business’s operating performance.

### Non-current assets

Non-current assets are resources held for continuing use in business operations rather than expected short-term conversion in ordinary trading. Examples include premises, machinery and delivery equipment. Their accounting value is not necessarily what a buyer would pay immediately. An asset’s purpose matters: a vehicle held for repeated deliveries differs from a vehicle held as stock by a dealer.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Oka Supply uses a van repeatedly for deliveries and holds packaged goods for sale. The van is a non-current asset; goods for sale are inventory within current assets. A high van value does not make it immediately spendable money.

### Current assets

Current assets are resources expected to be realised, sold or used within the normal operating cycle or short term, such as inventory, debtors and cash. They differ in liquidity: cash is immediately available, debtors must pay and inventory must be sold. The total is therefore not an immediately available bank balance.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri has inventory N$30,000, debtors N$18,000 and cash N$12,000. Current assets total N$60,000. Only the stated N$12,000 is already cash; the rest depends on conversion and collection.

### Liabilities

Liabilities are obligations the business owes to others. Current liabilities fall due in the short term, while non-current liabilities are longer-term obligations. Supplier creditors and an agreed short-term overdraft are common current examples; a long-term loan can be non-current. Classify any portion falling due soon appropriately when the question supplies it. A liability is not an asset simply because borrowing brought cash into the business.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri has current liabilities N$30,000 and non-current liabilities N$40,000. Total liabilities are N$70,000. The N$30,000 is relevant to current and quick ratios, whereas solvency compares assets with the full N$70,000.

### Working capital

Working capital is current assets less current liabilities. It indicates net short-term resources supporting day-to-day operations. Positive working capital is not proof of available cash because inventory or debtors may be slow to turn into money. Insufficient working capital can make paying near-term commitments difficult even if long-term assets are valuable. Working capital is a money amount, distinct from the current ratio.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri has current assets N$60,000 and current liabilities N$30,000. Working capital is N$30,000. The current ratio is 2.00:1, a different format relating the same two totals; neither makes all current assets cash.

## Profit and cash are different

### Profit and cash are different

Profit measures income earned less the relevant costs and expenses over a period; cash is money available and movements of money received or paid. A credit sale can add to profit before the customer pays. Paying for a long-lived asset reduces cash without making its full purchase price an immediate expense in the simple trading-profit calculation. Borrowing increases cash and liabilities rather than profit. Repaying loan principal reduces cash and liabilities rather than being an ordinary trading expense, whereas interest is an expense. Depreciation reduces recorded profit while involving no current cash payment for that depreciation charge. These timing and classification differences explain why a profitable enterprise may struggle to pay bills. Examine receipts, payments and due dates rather than assuming a profit figure is money in the bank.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Teya Cabinets sells a cabinet on credit for N$5,000. The materials and other relevant cost of that sale total N$3,000, already paid. The customer will pay next month. The sale contributes N$2,000 profit before other relevant expenses, but it provides no customer cash yet. The business has already paid N$3,000, so cash timing can be difficult despite a profitable sale. When the debtor pays, cash rises and the amount owed falls; it is not a second sale.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why a credit sale can increase profit before cash rises. Fictional Hila Repairs earns N$3,000 for completed work with relevant costs N$2,000 already paid, and the customer pays next month.

The work contributes N$1,000 profit under the stated figures, but no customer receipt has yet arrived. The amount owed is a debtor. When payment arrives next month, cash rises and the debtor falls; it does not create the same income again. Profit timing and cash timing are different.

## Using statements and ratios together

### Using statements and ratios together

An Income Statement reports income, expenses and resulting profit or loss for a period. A Statement of Financial Position reports assets, liabilities and equity at a date. Ratios relate selected figures to answer a specific question. Profitability includes gross and net profit margins and return on capital employed. Liquidity concerns ability to meet short-term debts and is indicated by current and quick ratios. Inventory turnover concerns how quickly stock is used or sold and is an efficiency measure in Annexe A, even though it also influences liquidity. The solvency ratio here is total assets divided by total liabilities, expressed x:1. Do not replace it with a different textbook’s debt percentage. Compare ratios with an earlier period, similar business or relevant conditions. A high current ratio can reflect slow inventory, and strong reported profit does not establish prompt cash receipts. Use year-end balances where appropriate unless average figures are specified. Give ratio answers to two decimal places and distinguish percentages, x:1, days and times per annum.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri Traders: annual turnover N$240,000, cost of sales N$144,000, other expenses N$72,000, credit sales N$219,000 and credit purchases N$146,000. At year end: non-current assets N$90,000; inventory N$30,000; debtors N$18,000; cash N$12,000; current liabilities N$30,000 (including creditors N$20,000); non-current liabilities N$40,000; owner’s equity N$80,000. Opening inventory was N$18,000. Use the opening and closing inventory average for inventory turnover. No other income or expense is assumed in this simple example. Neri’s gross margin is 40.00%, net margin 10.00%, ROCE 20.00%, current ratio 2.00:1 and quick ratio 1.00:1. Its solvency ratio is 2.14:1 rounded. Inventory turnover is 6.00 times per annum using the specified average. These describe different aspects: the gap between the current and quick ratios highlights inventory’s importance, while profit margins do not tell us when debtors pay.

### Income Statement

An Income Statement reports income and expenses and the resulting profit or loss over a period. Read the heading to identify the period. Sales less cost of sales gives gross profit; deduct the other relevant expenses and add any other specified income to find net profit. It measures performance, not the total assets owned at one date. A profitable statement can contain credit sales not yet collected.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri reports turnover N$240,000, cost of sales N$144,000 and other expenses N$72,000. The statement shows gross profit N$96,000 and net profit N$24,000 for the year. It does not state that all sales have been collected or that those profits remain in cash.

### Statement of Financial Position

A Statement of Financial Position reports assets, liabilities and equity at a particular date. It separates current and non-current categories so users can examine resources and obligations. Assets equal equity plus liabilities when the statement is complete and consistently prepared. It describes recorded position rather than revenue earned over a period. Book values do not guarantee sale proceeds and year-end balances may not describe every day of the year.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Neri has non-current assets N$90,000, current assets N$60,000, current liabilities N$30,000, non-current liabilities N$40,000 and equity N$80,000. Assets total N$150,000. Liabilities plus equity also total N$150,000. The equality shows how the resources are financed; it does not establish their cash realisation or profitability.

### Return on capital employed

ROCE = net profit / capital employed × 100. Capital employed is owner’s capital plus long-term liabilities using year-end figures here. ROCE relates annual profit to the long-term finance invested, so it measures return rather than short-term bill-paying ability. Compare like periods and accounting bases; a higher percentage can reflect improved profit or a reduced denominator and needs explanation.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has net profit N$24,000, owner’s equity N$80,000 and long-term liabilities N$40,000. Capital employed is N$120,000. ROCE = 24,000 / 120,000 × 100, giving 20.00%. The business earns N$20 annual net profit per N$100 capital employed under these figures.

### Gross profit margin

Gross profit margin = gross profit / turnover × 100. It shows the percentage of sales revenue left after cost of sales, before other expenses. A higher margin might arise from higher selling prices, a different product mix or lower purchase costs. It does not by itself show a higher cash balance or net profit; other expenses and sales volume matter.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has gross profit N$96,000 and turnover N$240,000. Gross margin = 96,000 / 240,000 × 100, giving 40.00%. N$40 of each N$100 sales remains to meet other expenses and provide net profit.

### Net profit margin

Net profit margin = net profit / turnover × 100. It shows the percentage of sales left as net profit after the included costs and expenses. Compare it with gross margin to examine how other expenses affect performance. A high percentage on low sales can still produce a smaller absolute profit than a lower percentage on very high sales. It is not a cash-flow measure.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has net profit N$24,000 and turnover N$240,000. Net margin = 24,000 / 240,000 × 100, giving 10.00%. N$10 of each N$100 sales remains as net profit.

### Current ratio

Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities, expressed x:1. It compares short-term resources with short-term obligations, not profit with sales. A high figure may provide a cushion, but inventory can be slow-moving and debtors may pay late. A very high figure can also indicate funds tied up unproductively. A suitable level depends on the business and cash cycle rather than one universal ideal.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has current assets N$60,000 and current liabilities N$30,000. Current ratio = 60,000 / 30,000 = 2.00:1. N$2 current assets back each N$1 current liabilities, but some resources must be converted into cash.

### Quick or liquid ratio

Quick ratio = (current assets − inventory) / current liabilities, expressed x:1. It removes inventory to examine coverage without depending on its sale. Debtors included in the remaining assets still need to pay, so a quick ratio is not a cash ratio and cannot guarantee immediate settlement. Compare with the current ratio to see how strongly coverage depends on stock.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has current assets N$60,000, inventory N$30,000 and current liabilities N$30,000. Quick ratio = (60,000 − 30,000) / 30,000 = 1.00:1. N$1 non-inventory current assets backs each N$1 current liabilities.

### Rate of inventory turnover

Rate of inventory turnover = cost of sales / average inventory, expressed times per annum for annual figures. Use the average inventory specified; if opening and closing balances are given for that purpose, add them and divide by two. It indicates how often the average inventory is sold or used over the year. Faster turnover can release cash and reduce storage, but may mean inadequate stock if it causes shortages. Compare similar products: fresh food and durable furniture have different expected cycles.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has cost of sales N$144,000 and opening and closing inventory N$18,000 and N$30,000. Use their average. Average inventory = (18,000 + 30,000) / 2 = N$24,000. Inventory turnover = 144,000 / 24,000 = 6.00 times per annum.

### Solvency ratio

Solvency ratio in this syllabus = total assets / total liabilities, expressed x:1. Include current and non-current liabilities in the denominator. It indicates recorded asset coverage of total obligations. A value above 1 indicates recorded assets exceed liabilities, but realisable values can differ and long-lived assets may not pay debts due today. It therefore differs from liquidity and from profit margin. Do not substitute a debt-to-equity or debt-percentage formula.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has total assets N$150,000 and total liabilities N$70,000. Solvency ratio = 150,000 / 70,000, rounded to 2.14:1. Recorded assets are about N$2.14 per N$1 total liabilities, not N$2.14 cash per debt dollar.

### Worked practice: Financial evidence and judgement

Fictional Neri Traders: annual turnover N$240,000, cost of sales N$144,000, other expenses N$72,000, credit sales N$219,000 and credit purchases N$146,000. At year end: non-current assets N$90,000; inventory N$30,000; debtors N$18,000; cash N$12,000; current liabilities N$30,000 (including creditors N$20,000); non-current liabilities N$40,000; owner’s equity N$80,000. Opening inventory was N$18,000. Use the opening and closing inventory average for inventory turnover. No other income or expense is assumed in this simple example. A supplier is considering another short credit sale to Neri and wants evidence of prompt payment. A shareholder is assessing returns. Supplier payment terms for existing creditors are forty-five days.

a Analyse Neri’s profitability and liquidity using gross margin, net margin and quick ratio, and distinguish these measures from solvency. [8]
b Evaluate whether the supplied financial evidence is enough for a supplier to extend further forty-five-day credit. [12]

a Gross profit = 240,000 − 144,000 = N$96,000, giving gross margin 40.00%. Net profit is N$24,000, giving net margin 10.00%. The thirty-percentage-point difference is the burden of other included expenses relative to turnover. ### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why fictional Ava Traders can be profitable and show positive solvency cover while struggling to pay next week’s supplier. It reports a profit, most assets are long-lived equipment, inventory is slow-moving and customers have not paid.

Profitability concerns the surplus or return earned over a period. Solvency here compares total recorded assets with total liabilities. Liquidity concerns meeting short-term debts as they fall due. Equipment and slow-moving stock may provide recorded asset cover without becoming cash before next week, while unpaid sales can contribute to profit before collection. The three measures therefore cannot be substituted for one another.

## Retained profit and spendable cash

### Retained profit and spendable cash

Retained profit is profit kept within the business instead of distributed to owners. It forms part of accumulated equity, not a promise that an equal cash balance exists. The retained value may be employed in machinery, inventory or receivables. Spendable cash depends on actual cash and bank balances, payment commitments and any reserve needed for operations. A business can retain a profit and still need to collect debtors before buying equipment. Selling a surplus asset may release cash without creating the same amount of profit. Internal financing decisions should therefore test whether funds are genuinely available rather than quoting retained profit alone. A large accumulated balance of retained earnings can coexist with limited liquidity.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Eno Print retains N$30,000 profit for the year but spends N$25,000 cash on a machine. For this illustration assume all retained profit was initially received in cash and there are no other movements or opening cash. After the purchase, cash remaining from those funds is N$5,000. The retained profit is still N$30,000 under the simplified assumptions, but much of the value is now held in equipment. It cannot pay N$30,000 to a supplier merely by pointing to retained profit.

## Users need different financial evidence

### Users need different financial evidence

Managers use accounts for planning, control and decisions about resources. Owners and potential investors assess returns, risk and how their investment is used. Lenders assess repayment prospects and financial strength; suppliers offering credit assess whether short-term debts are likely to be paid. Employees may assess job security and the ability to sustain pay, while government may use appropriate records for public revenue and policy purposes. Customers with a long-term supply arrangement may assess continuity of supply. Users may emphasise different figures: a shareholder may focus on return, whereas a short-term supplier needs cash timing and liquidity as well. Financial statements are historical, summarised and affected by valuation; they do not capture every service-quality or management factor. Ratios are evidence for a decision, not a substitute for checking the user’s actual concern.

## Mark up

### Mark up

Mark up = gross profit / cost of sales × 100. It expresses gross profit relative to the cost of goods sold, whereas gross margin uses sales revenue as its denominator. The percentages differ even for the same transaction. Mark up is not the increase in total net profit and does not account for all other operating expenses. Retain full working and round the final percentage to two decimal places.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has gross profit N$96,000 and cost of sales N$144,000. Mark up = 96,000 / 144,000 × 100, rounded to 66.67%. The 40.00% gross margin uses sales N$240,000 instead, so it is not interchangeable with mark up.

### Debtors collection period

Debtors collection period = debtors / credit sales × 365 days for annual credit sales. It estimates how many days of credit sales are represented by the debtors balance. A longer period can mean cash is tied up longer and collection needs attention, but sales patterns and agreed terms also matter. Use credit sales, not total sales when the two differ. Use year-end debtors unless average figures are specified.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has year-end debtors N$18,000 and annual credit sales N$219,000. Collection period = 18,000 / 219,000 × 365 = 30.00 days. The balance represents about thirty days of annual credit sales, not proof that every customer pays on day thirty.

### Creditors payment period

Creditors payment period = creditors / credit purchases × 365 days for annual credit purchases. It estimates how long payment to suppliers is taking relative to the purchases base. Longer payment can conserve cash temporarily but may damage supplier confidence, interrupt supplies or lose discounts if it breaches agreed terms. A shorter period can indicate prompt payment but may use cash before necessary. Judge against terms, not a universal preference for the longest period.

### Putting the idea to work

Neri has year-end creditors N$20,000 and annual credit purchases N$146,000. Payment period = 20,000 / 146,000 × 365 = 50.00 days. Compare the fifty-day estimate with supplier terms before calling it good or bad.
