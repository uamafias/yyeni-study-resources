## Reasons people work

### Reasons people work

People work for income to meet living needs, security, social contact, recognition, achievement and opportunities to develop their abilities. Different people place different weight on these rewards, and priorities can change. Work can support needs without satisfying all of them: a well-paid job may be insecure or isolating. Job security means confidence that employment will continue. Social security concerns protection or benefits against specified risks, rather than simply keeping the same job. Do not substitute one meaning for the other. An employer choosing a motivation approach needs evidence of staff needs, not an assumption that everyone wants the same thing.

### Putting the idea to work

At Omi Foods one worker values reliable earnings while another values learning a more responsible role. A pay rise may appeal to the first worker, while greater responsibility may better address the second worker’s stated need. The employer should still check whether the work and basic conditions are acceptable.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain the difference between job security and social security. A worker expects her employment to continue; a separate benefit arrangement protects eligible workers against a specified income risk.

The expectation of continued employment is job security. The benefit arrangement is social security or protection. Confidence about keeping a job does not itself describe a benefit or insurance arrangement.

## Using theories as explanations, not guarantees

### Using theories as explanations, not guarantees

Taylor emphasises financial incentives and systematic organisation of work. Maslow describes a hierarchy of physiological, safety, social, esteem and self-actualisation needs. Herzberg distinguishes hygiene or maintenance factors that prevent dissatisfaction from motivators within the work itself. These theories suggest different reasons for behaviour and different responses. Pay and safe conditions matter, but they may not provide achievement, recognition or responsibility. Use the theory that explains the stated problem, identify its limits and assess the workforce rather than mixing categories from different theorists. Needs need not follow a rigid identical order for every person. A manager should investigate and review the response instead of treating a theory as proof that one measure will motivate everyone.

### Putting the idea to work

Nela Repairs pays adequately and provides secure conditions, but experienced technicians complain that repetitive work gives them no responsibility or recognition. Herzberg suggests addressing the work itself through meaningful responsibility and recognition. More pay could be welcomed but does not directly resolve the stated complaint. Choose job improvement with staff consultation; basic pay still matters if later evidence shows it is inadequate.

### Taylor’s approach

Taylor’s scientific-management approach examines tasks systematically, seeks an efficient method, selects suitable workers and links financial reward to output. Piece-rate pay can reward additional acceptable units, making a clear effort–earnings link where output is measurable and workers can influence it. The approach gives money a central role rather than explaining motivation through a hierarchy of needs. Its limits include neglecting social needs and job satisfaction, and encouraging speed at the expense of quality or safety if targets are poorly designed. It suits some repetitive measurable work better than complex team services where individual output is difficult to attribute.

### Putting the idea to work

Tula Packs can count correctly completed packs and workers control the pace within safe limits. An output-linked reward may encourage more acceptable packs, but quality checks and attainable targets are needed. Paying for rejected packs would reward the wrong result.

### Maslow’s hierarchy

Maslow’s hierarchy identifies physiological needs such as basic living requirements, safety needs such as secure and safe work, social needs such as belonging, esteem needs such as recognition and self-actualisation through developing potential. Pay can help basic needs, secure conditions support safety, team relationships support social needs, praise can support esteem and challenging opportunities can support development. The model suggests unmet needs influence motivation, with lower needs commonly receiving attention before higher ones. Do not assume every worker follows an identical fixed sequence or stops valuing money after one need is met. Managers need to identify which needs matter in the particular case.

### Putting the idea to work

Hema Services’ new worker is worried about unreliable earnings, while an experienced colleague wants opportunities to develop skills. Reliable pay can help the first worker’s basic needs, while suitable developmental work can address the colleague’s higher-level aspirations. One uniform reward may not fit both.

### Herzberg’s two-factor approach

Herzberg distinguishes hygiene or maintenance factors, including pay, conditions, supervision and policies, from motivators such as achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement and the work itself. Poor hygiene factors can create dissatisfaction; putting them right can remove that dissatisfaction without necessarily producing lasting motivation. Motivators concern positive satisfaction from meaningful work. Job enrichment can add genuine responsibility and challenge rather than merely more repetitive tasks. The model does not justify ignoring wages or claiming that every employee responds identically. Diagnose the actual complaint and avoid labelling Maslow’s hierarchy or Taylor’s output incentives as Herzberg’s categories.

### Putting the idea to work

Oha Design staff have safe conditions and fair supervision, but are not allowed to make creative choices on their own projects and receive no recognition when projects are completed. Giving capable staff responsibility for design choices and recognising achievement targets motivators. Fixing a broken chair would address a hygiene problem, which is worthwhile but different.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain the distinction in Herzberg’s theory: Ula Print repairs unsafe conditions and separately gives experienced staff responsibility for checking completed work.

Safer working conditions address a hygiene or maintenance factor and can reduce dissatisfaction. Meaningful responsibility addresses a motivator. Hygiene and maintenance name the same category here, not two separate theories. Neither classification is Maslow’s hierarchy or Taylor’s output-pay model.

a Explain how Maslow and Herzberg could account for Ena’s staff concerns without mixing their categories. [8]

Here the complaint is repetitive work and recognition, not an inadequate effort–earnings link. A financial incentive could leave the complaint unresolved and put pressure on speed. Prefer meaningful responsibility with support and recognition, monitor the outcome and retain fair pay. A measurable production problem with clear financial dissatisfaction would strengthen Taylor’s relevance; those conditions are not stated.
