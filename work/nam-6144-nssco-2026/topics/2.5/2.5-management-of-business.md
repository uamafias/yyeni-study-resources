## Effective management

### Effective management

Effective management is to plan, organise, lead and control people in a business to achieve business objectives and solve business problems. Effectiveness concerns achieving suitable objectives; efficiency concerns how well resources are used in doing so. An enterprise can make many units cheaply but still miss its delivery or quality objective. Good management connects goals with resources, work and review. Planning alone is insufficient if nobody allocates tasks, provides guidance or checks results. Conversely, constant checking without a clear goal gives staff no agreed standard. A manager must recognise constraints and coordinate departments instead of pursuing one target while undermining another. An output target should therefore be considered alongside quality, available staff and customer requirements.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Ewa Desks promises complete desks by Friday, but the workshop makes many tabletops while no one schedules the legs. A large tabletop count is not the agreed objective. Management must coordinate the parts and assembly so customers receive complete desks on time, while using resources without unnecessary waste.

### Worked practice: Coordinated management and decision levels

Fictional Hela Packs misses its weekly dispatch target. Existing capacity is sufficient, but materials and staff are scheduled at different times. A supervisor can introduce a coordinated rota and daily checks within existing authority. The owner is considering buying a second production line as an alternative.

Evaluate Hela’s coordinated rota and daily checks against purchasing the second line under the original facts. Give a concise plan. [12]

Concepts: management coordinates planning, organising, leading and controlling towards objectives. A rota is an operational arrangement; a new line is a wider investment decision. Case for the rota: adequate stated capacity and mismatched timing mean coordination addresses the cause; checks reveal whether output reaches dispatch on time. Limitation: the manager must act on the checks and communicate changes, not merely collect figures. Case for a new line: it could increase capacity but no current capacity shortage is stated, and extra equipment may sit idle while timing problems continue. Judgement: correct the rota first and review results; escalate if evidence reveals a genuine capacity constraint.

## Why businesses need effective management

### Why businesses need effective management

Resources and staff must work towards common objectives. Effective management clarifies priorities, allocates tasks and resources, resolves conflicts and responds to changing conditions. Without coordination, one department may make promises that another cannot meet, causing delay, wasted materials and disappointed customers. Clear objectives help staff understand the intended result; suitable organisation assigns responsibility; leadership builds understanding and effort; control detects problems early enough to correct them. A good manager investigates the cause before blaming workers or buying extra capacity. An order delay might reflect missing material, unclear instructions or an unrealistic promise rather than laziness. Effective management can improve survival and performance, but it does not guarantee success against every external shock.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Oshi Meals accepts event orders without checking its kitchen schedule. Staff duplicate preparation for one event and miss another. A shared schedule, clear responsibility and a check of kitchen capacity can prevent incompatible promises. This reduces avoidable waste and late deliveries, protecting customer confidence. Extra staff would not necessarily solve a coordination failure if nobody changes the booking process.

## The four connected functions

### The four connected functions

Planning sets objectives and chooses how to achieve them. Organising allocates people, tasks and other resources. Leading guides and motivates people so they understand and work towards the objectives. Controlling compares results with standards and initiates corrective action. These functions form a connected cycle rather than four isolated jobs. A manager plans a delivery deadline, organises the required staff and materials, explains priorities and supports the team, then checks progress and corrects a shortage. Feedback from control can change the next plan. Managers often perform several functions during one conversation, so classify the action described rather than the manager’s job title. Checking a result is control; choosing the future target is planning. A manager’s personal honesty is a quality supporting these functions, not a fifth function.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Luma Bags must fill a school order. Its manager sets a completion date, assigns cutting and sewing work, explains quality requirements and compares completed bags with the schedule. Setting the date is planning; assigning tasks is organising; guiding the team is leading; comparing progress is controlling. If sewing falls behind, the manager should investigate and reallocate resources if appropriate, linking control back to organisation and planning.

### Planning

Planning sets objectives and decides how resources and actions will achieve them. A useful plan states the intended result, timing, responsibilities and assumptions. It can reduce avoidable uncertainty, although forecasts cannot remove every risk. Plans should be revised when evidence changes rather than followed mechanically.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Awa Tiles estimates demand and schedules materials for next month’s output. Choosing the future target and how to meet it is planning. Counting what was actually completed belongs to control.

### Organising

Organising arranges people, tasks, authority and resources to carry out plans. It clarifies responsibilities and coordinates contributions so that required work is neither duplicated nor omitted. A plan for increased output is ineffective if materials and staff are unavailable at the right time. Organising is an activity; an organisation chart is one tool for showing the resulting responsibilities.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Karo Furniture allocates cutting, sanding and assembly tasks and ensures tools are available. Matching the people and tools to these tasks is organising. Merely announcing the target does not make those resources available.

### Leading

Leading guides, communicates with and motivates people towards business objectives. It includes explaining priorities, listening to difficulties and supporting coordinated effort. It is more than issuing orders: people need to understand the task and have the means to perform it. The most suitable approach depends on staff, urgency and risk.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nima Meals’ supervisor explains why allergen checks matter and listens to a worker’s concern about unclear labels. Guidance and listening support correct action and shared understanding. The supervisor should resolve the label problem rather than assuming motivation alone removes it.

### Controlling

Controlling sets or uses performance standards, measures actual results, compares them with the standard and takes corrective action where needed. It is not simply punishing staff or checking attendance. Investigating causes matters because the same shortfall can arise from different problems. Feedback can lead to revised plans, different resource allocation or clearer guidance.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Ova Prints compares actual spoiled sheets with its quality target and finds an equipment fault. Repairing the fault addresses the cause. Blaming the operator without investigating would not be effective control.

## Qualities of a good manager

### Qualities of a good manager

Useful managerial qualities include clear communication, sound judgement, integrity, fairness, confidence, willingness to listen and the ability to organise and adapt. Communication makes responsibilities and instructions understandable. Judgement weighs relevant evidence before acting. Integrity supports trust because staff and suppliers can rely on truthful information. Fairness can help cooperation when allocating work or handling disputes. Confidence should be paired with listening rather than treating every challenge as disloyalty. Adaptability matters when a plan no longer fits the facts. A quality describes how capable a manager is; a function describes the management activity being performed. Naming planning and controlling does not by itself identify personal qualities. Support a quality with its business effect rather than claiming that a pleasant personality automatically achieves objectives.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Omu Repairs’ manager listens when technicians warn that a promised deadline is unsafe, checks the evidence and communicates a realistic revised plan to the customer. Listening, judgement and integrity are qualities shown by the response. Revising the schedule is planning and explaining the priorities supports leading. Distinguishing qualities from functions makes the description precise.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain the difference between the two descriptions of fictional manager Nela: “she is honest and listens carefully” and “she plans output and checks results”.

Honesty and listening are managerial qualities: attributes affecting how she manages. Planning and controlling are functions: activities she performs. The qualities can improve those functions, for example honest reporting supports useful control, but naming a function does not identify a personal quality.

## Strategic, tactical and operational decisions

### Strategic, tactical and operational decisions

Strategic decisions set the long-term direction of the whole business and usually involve senior management and substantial commitment. Tactical decisions translate the strategy into departmental or medium-term action, often through middle management. Operational decisions organise routine day-to-day work, commonly through supervisors and lower-level managers. The difference concerns scope, importance and time horizon, not simply how difficult a decision feels. Opening a new region can be strategic, selecting a regional promotion plan tactical and assigning today’s delivery route operational. The decisions need alignment: efficient daily work cannot achieve a strategy if departments pursue incompatible targets. In a small enterprise one owner may make all three types, so do not assume different people are essential to the distinction. Escalate decisions beyond a role’s authority while allowing competent staff to handle appropriate routine matters.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Tano Foods chooses to enter a new town next year. Its marketing head chooses a launch campaign, while a shift supervisor allocates today’s packing tasks. The market-entry choice is strategic; the campaign implementing it is tactical; the shift allocation is operational. A late packing task usually needs an operational response first, unless it reveals a persistent problem requiring a wider change.

### Strategic decisions

Strategic decisions concern the long-term direction and major objectives of the business. They are normally taken at senior level and can commit substantial resources, such as entering a new market or changing the main product range. Their effects spread across functions and cannot be assessed from today’s work schedule alone.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Yeta Tools decides whether to establish operations in another region. This changes direction and long-term commitment, so it is strategic. The subsequent daily dispatch choices implement the strategy but are not themselves the same decision.

### Tactical decisions

Tactical decisions turn wider strategy into more specific plans, often for a department or a medium-term period. They concern how to implement the strategic direction, for example a promotion programme or departmental capacity plan. Middle managers commonly make them within agreed authority. A small owner may also make tactical decisions; classification follows the decision’s scope rather than the title alone.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Elo Foods has chosen to enter a new town and its sales head chooses a three-month launch campaign. The campaign is tactical because it implements the market-entry strategy. It differs from both choosing the new market and allocating a worker’s task today.

### Operational decisions

Operational decisions concern routine day-to-day activity, such as allocating shifts, scheduling a delivery or reordering ordinary supplies within an agreed plan. Supervisors and lower managers often make them. They still affect customers and costs, but their scope is narrower than the business’s strategic direction. Escalate an unusual commitment beyond the role’s authority instead of treating every daily problem as permission to change strategy.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Onda Works’ supervisor assigns today’s available staff to existing orders. This is operational because it implements current commitments. Choosing a completely new product market would require a wider strategic decision.

a Coordinating the existing rota is operational and fits the stated mismatch; planning, allocation, guidance and review make it a complete management response. Senior management should now evaluate extra capacity, including a second line, against cost, resources and contract durability. The judgement changes towards investigating investment rather than relying on the operational fix alone. Do not buy automatically; the capacity case is now stronger, but affordability and implementation remain to be assessed.

### Worked practice: Coordinated management and decision levels

Fictional Nawa Desks misses a delivery target because cutting and assembly schedules do not match. Existing equipment and staff are adequate when work is coordinated. The supervisor proposes a shared daily schedule and progress checks. The owner instead considers a new factory without first investigating the mismatch.

a Explain how effective management could improve Nawa’s ability to meet its delivery objective. [8]
b Evaluate whether the missed target should trigger an operational correction or an immediate strategic expansion. [12]

a Planning should translate the delivery promise into a workable sequence; organising should allocate cutting and assembly staff and materials consistently. Leading should clarify priorities and enable staff to report difficulties. Controlling should compare progress with the agreed schedule and investigate differences. The functions reinforce one another: checks provide information for revised allocations, while clear guidance makes the revised plan understandable. Since capacity is stated to be adequate, coordination can reduce unfinished work and protect customer confidence without assuming extra machines are necessary. b Operational correction is narrower and can target the known scheduling failure. It preserves resources while testing whether the existing setup can fulfil commitments. Strategic expansion might support future growth, but that need is not established and it adds a long-term cost before the actual cause is resolved. Choose the coordinated schedule and review delivery completion. Escalate to tactical capacity planning or strategic investment only if reliable evidence shows a wider constraint. Effective management means selecting a response suited to the cause and level, not making the most expensive decision.
