## A business plan

### A business plan

A business plan is a document containing the business objectives and important details about the operations and finance of the firm. It explains what the enterprise intends to do, for whom, how it will operate and how it expects to finance the activity. It supports coordinated decisions, communication with potential funders and later review. Forecasts are assumptions to test, not promises that sales or funding will occur. A useful plan connects its sections: planned demand must fit capacity, staffing, spending and cash timing. A polished document cannot compensate for unrealistic evidence. Review the plan when conditions change rather than treating completion of the document as completion of the business.

### Putting the idea to work

Nela Meals proposes a small weekday lunch service for nearby workers. Its plan should connect customer evidence with meals, collection arrangements, staff, equipment and finance. A menu alone does not explain whether the proposed service can operate and pay its bills.

## The elements must fit together

### The elements must fit together

A business plan includes a description of the business, market research, a marketing plan, production plan, organisational plan, financial plan and action plan. The description establishes the proposal and objectives. Research supports demand assumptions; marketing explains the target and offering; production explains how output will be supplied; organisation allocates responsibilities; finance sets out needs, sources and forecasts; the action plan turns decisions into timed tasks. These are connected components, not interchangeable names. Compare assumptions across them: a large sales target is inconsistent with too little capacity unless the plan explains how capacity will change. A production schedule alone leaves ownership, demand and finance unanswered.

### Putting the idea to work

Omi Bags predicts many orders but its production plan provides one worker and equipment capable of much less output. The sections need reconciliation before commitment. Either establish feasible extra resources or revise the sales expectation; copying the high forecast into every section does not make it realistic.

### Description of the business

This component identifies the proposed activity, ownership arrangement, location, objectives and main offering. It gives readers a clear basis for understanding the remaining sections. State a realistic purpose and scope rather than claiming the enterprise will be successful because it exists. The description is not a substitute for evidence of demand or a cash forecast.

### Putting the idea to work

Nela Meals proposes a sole-owned weekday lunch collection service near a workplace cluster in Tsumeb. Model description: provide one affordable portable lunch range to nearby workers, begin with a limited trial and review repeat paid orders before expanding. The scope makes later capacity and marketing decisions more specific.

### Market research in the plan

The research component records the information supporting demand and customer assumptions, including sources, methods, findings and limitations. Show why the information fits the target market and what remains untested. Old regional data or favourable comments alone may not establish local paid demand. The plan should identify the next evidence needed, not hide uncertainty behind a precise-looking sales figure.

### Putting the idea to work

Hela Lunches has neutral interviews with nearby workers but no paid-sales trial. Hela’s plan can report preferences and the sample limit, then schedule a small trial at the proposed price. It should not label every interested interviewee a confirmed daily customer.

### Marketing plan

The marketing plan states target customers, marketing objectives and coordinated choices of product, price, promotion and distribution. It explains how the enterprise will reach and serve its chosen market within a budget. It differs from research, which provides evidence, and from a single advertisement, which is one action. Targets should be reviewable and consistent with operational capacity.

### Putting the idea to work

Nela Meals can prepare one weekday range and use local collection. Model marketing plan: target nearby workers seeking quick lunch; offer a portable meal at a tested feasible price; use a small accessible notice and direct information to nearby workplaces; provide prearranged collection; limit initial orders to confirmed capacity; review paid orders and repeat purchases weekly. This connects customer need with the whole offer.

### Production plan

The production plan explains how goods or services will be supplied: methods, equipment, inputs, suppliers, premises, capacity, timing and quality arrangements. It translates the sales proposal into a feasible operation. Job, batch and flow production are different methods developed in topic 4.5; the selected method must fit volume and variety. The plan is one part of the whole document, not the entire business plan.

### Putting the idea to work

Nela Meals will prepare a limited set of lunches before collection. Its production component should specify ingredient supply, safe preparation resources, staff tasks, maximum feasible daily meals, collection timing and quality checks. Unconfirmed sales above that capacity need a revised operation, not an unexplained promise.

### Organisational plan

The organisational plan identifies roles, responsibilities, staffing and reporting. It shows who will carry out the proposed work and which skills are needed. A small business still needs responsibility for buying, production, customer contact and records even when one person performs several roles. Include relevant development needs; training may be on-the-job or off-the-job as explained in topic 3.5. A list of impressive titles does not provide the people or capabilities.

### Putting the idea to work

Eha Bags has one owner and one assistant. The plan can allocate purchasing and finance oversight to the owner and specified cutting and sewing work to the assistant, while stating who checks quality and handles customers. Tasks should fit available time rather than assume one person can do everything simultaneously.

### Financial plan

The financial plan identifies start-up and operating needs, finance sources, expected income and costs and cash timing. Explain assumptions and whether funding is agreed or only proposed. Profit forecasts and cash forecasts answer different questions: a credit sale can add profit before receipt. Test whether payments fall due before funds arrive. A lender assesses viability and repayment, so a document full of unsupported optimistic figures is weak evidence.

### Putting the idea to work

Ota Crafts expects to buy materials before collecting customer payments. The plan should show the material-payment dates and likely receipts, identify the temporary funding need and state how it would be covered. Calling the future sale profitable does not resolve that timing gap.

### Action plan

An action plan turns the proposal into specific tasks, responsible people, deadlines and dependencies. It lets managers coordinate implementation and check progress. A deadline should allow prerequisite work: equipment cannot be tested before it is obtained and staff cannot use a process safely before relevant preparation. Review missed tasks and revise the schedule where necessary rather than record dates without follow-up.

### Putting the idea to work

Nela Meals plans a limited launch after checking demand and resources. Model action extract: owner completes customer-price trial in week 1; owner confirms input supplier and capacity in week 2; team tests preparation and collection in week 3; owner reviews finance before accepting week-4 orders. Each later task depends on the preceding evidence and resources.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why Oru Bags’ equipment list and production schedule are not its entire business plan.

They belong to the production component, explaining how output can be made. The wider plan also needs the business description, research, marketing, organisation, finance and action arrangements. A technically feasible process alone does not establish customers or cash availability.

## Using and reviewing the plan

### Using and reviewing the plan

A business plan helps set goals, define the product, identify the consumer, organise research, coordinate marketing and seek finance. It can reveal gaps before money is committed and provide a reference against which actual progress is reviewed. A potential lender can assess the proposal and repayment assumptions, but a plan does not guarantee approval. Managers can assign actions and investigate differences between forecast and actual results. The plan should be updated when evidence changes: a lost customer can affect production, promotion and cash together. Uses are the decisions the document supports; elements are its contents. Explain the link rather than merely repeating a section heading.

### Putting the idea to work

Tula Repairs plans a new service but a paid trial produces fewer orders than forecast. Use the results to revise demand and resource commitments, then update finance and the action timetable. Ignoring the evidence to preserve the original document would defeat the planning purpose.

### Goals as a planning use

A business plan makes objectives explicit and provides a basis for reviewing progress. A defined result and period help distinguish a successful action from merely being busy. Goals must fit resources and should be revised through evidence rather than adjusted just to conceal failure.

### Putting the idea to work

Hena Print targets a reliable small-order service and monitors on-time completion over its first month. The plan provides a standard for review. A large number of leaflets distributed would not by itself prove that the service goal was met.

### Product decisions as a planning use

A plan helps define the product or service and test whether its features meet customer needs within capacity and cost limits. It can reveal that an attractive feature is unaffordable or impractical. The product should fit the wider offer: promised variety affects equipment, inputs and staffing as well as promotion.

### Putting the idea to work

Tano Bags considers many styles but can reliably produce one durable range. Planning exposes the mismatch and supports a focused feasible launch rather than an unsupported promise of variety.

### Consumer focus as a planning use

A plan identifies who is expected to buy, the need served and how those consumers can be reached. This helps prevent production for an undefined everyone. Consumer interest must include the ability and willingness to pay the feasible price, not merely liking the idea. A narrow target also creates dependence that should be recognised.

### Putting the idea to work

Nalu Meals targets nearby workers with short breaks. Its plan can align portability and collection speed with that need. Tourist evening menus would be a different consumer offer requiring separate evidence and capability.

### Research priorities as a planning use

Planning helps identify missing information, choose relevant research and decide what evidence should precede investment. It can prioritise an unknown price response over collecting facts already available. Research has a cost, so link it to a consequential uncertainty rather than gather unrelated statistics. Findings should update the plan when they contradict initial assumptions.

### Putting the idea to work

Omi Furniture knows workshop capacity but not whether buyers will pay its proposed price. The next study should test price and demand among suitable buyers. Another description of machinery would not close that market gap.

### Coordinating marketing as a planning use

The plan links marketing actions to objectives, budgets and operational promises. It can prevent a promotion generating orders the business cannot deliver. Product, price, promotion and access should support the same target. Review actual response and cost rather than assume that carrying out an advertisement achieves the objective.

### Putting the idea to work

Eya Repairs plans a promotion but has limited appointment capacity. It should coordinate the message and booking limit with available staff, then review completed paid jobs. Advertising unlimited immediate service would conflict with the operation.

### Seeking finance as a planning use

A plan communicates the proposal, funding need, expected return and cash assumptions to potential finance providers. It can help lenders or investors assess risk, while helping owners see whether finance is affordable. It does not create an entitlement to a loan or guarantee that forecasts will occur. State the requested amount and use, supporting evidence and contingencies clearly.

### Putting the idea to work

Tula Tools seeks a loan for equipment and presents cost, customer evidence and repayment forecasts. The lender can assess whether expected cash supports instalments. A high sales goal without cost or receipt evidence would be less persuasive and does not establish repayment capacity.
