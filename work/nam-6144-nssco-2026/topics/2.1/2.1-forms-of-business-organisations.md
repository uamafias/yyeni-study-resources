## Objectives depend on purpose and ownership

### Objectives depend on purpose and ownership

A business objective is a result the organisation seeks to achieve. Private enterprise is owned by private individuals or organisations and commonly seeks profit, survival or growth. Non-profit making activity gives priority to a social or service purpose rather than distributing profit to owners. A surplus can still be necessary to replace equipment and maintain the service. Public enterprise is owned and controlled by the state and may aim to make essential services accessible as well as cover its costs. The categories describe different aspects and can overlap: a privately organised charity can pursue a non-profit purpose. Do not classify an organisation’s purpose from its size or from one year’s financial result. A profit-seeking business that makes a loss remains profit-seeking; a non-profit organisation can record a surplus. Objectives influence decisions about prices, locations and which users are served. However, resources constrain each organisation. A service objective does not remove the need to pay workers and suppliers, and a profit objective does not prevent an owner from considering employees and the community.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume fictional Tula Transport is state owned and aims to connect remote settlements. Privately owned Lika Coaches prioritises a return on its owners’ investment. Tula may retain a lightly used route to support access, while Lika may favour a more profitable route. Either operator still needs resources to maintain vehicles; different objectives explain the different choices without proving either is efficient.

### Non-profit making activity

Non-profit making activity is organised primarily to provide a social or other service benefit rather than to distribute profit to owners. It still needs income to meet costs. A surplus can be retained to sustain or improve the activity, so non-profit activity can charge fees and seek a surplus without changing its primary purpose. Success is assessed against the service objective alongside financial sustainability.

### Putting the idea to work

A fictional community study centre charges modest fees to maintain its learning room. Charging fees does not turn its stated service purpose into a profit-distribution objective. If income exceeds costs, it can use the surplus to maintain the centre.

### Private enterprise

Private enterprise is business activity owned and controlled by private individuals or organisations rather than the state. Common objectives include profit, survival, growth and a return to owners, although private owners can also pursue social aims. A loss in one year does not change ownership or prove that the business has a non-profit purpose.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Dali Joinery is owned by two individuals who want a financial return. Their decision to buy a saw is likely to depend partly on expected sales and costs, because the stated objective is a return on their investment.

### Public enterprise

Public enterprise is owned and controlled by the state. Objectives may include providing essential services, maintaining access in less profitable areas or supporting employment, alongside meeting financial targets. State ownership does not mean that the enterprise has unlimited resources or that its customers pay nothing. Decisions must recognise both its service purpose and the cost of achieving it.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume fictional Riverlink Passenger Service is state owned and tasked with serving remote settlements. A route with low ticket revenue may still serve the access objective. The enterprise must finance the route, so the benefit should be weighed against the resources it consumes.

## The ownership choices

### The ownership choices

Forms of organisation allocate ownership, control, finance, risk and the right to receive a surplus. The syllabus covers sole proprietors, partnerships, private limited companies, public limited companies, co-operatives, close corporations, public corporations, joint ventures and franchises. They are not all alternatives on the same axis. A franchise is a contractual business arrangement, for example, and its outlet can be owned through a sole trader or company. A joint venture is a collaboration that can be organised in different ways. Start with the stated arrangement instead of assuming its legal features. Limited liability means the owners’ liability for business debts is normally restricted to their investment or agreed unpaid contribution; it does not cancel the enterprise’s debts or guarantee lenders repayment. A separate legal identity means an incorporated enterprise has an existence distinct from its owners. Continuity is consequently less dependent on one owner’s continued participation. Ordinary sole trading and partnership in the syllabus model involve unlimited liability. More owners or limited liability does not itself guarantee abundant finance; funders still assess the business. The following comparisons explain the ownership models without requiring detailed registration rules.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume two friends own a small repair service as an ordinary partnership. They compare it with a private limited company before adding expensive equipment. Partners share ownership and decisions but carry unlimited liability in this model. A company introduces separate legal identity and normally limited shareholder liability, while adding administration. Comparing risk and control is more useful than treating company status as an automatic guarantee of success.

### Sole proprietors

A sole proprietor, or sole trader, is the single owner of an unincorporated business. The owner controls decisions and receives the profit, but bears unlimited liability: business debts can expose personal assets rather than stopping at money invested. Sole ownership does not mean working without employees. Direct control and quick decisions can suit a small activity where the owner understands customers. The owner also keeps the reward for success and can preserve privacy over many internal choices. Against this, available capital depends heavily on the owner’s resources and borrowing, one person must supply or obtain a broad range of skills, and workload or illness can interrupt operation. Continuity is closely tied to that owner. These are tendencies, not evidence that a sole trader cannot grow or employ staff.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Etunda Repairs is owned by one experienced technician who employs an assistant. The owner has enough money for basic tools but wants to approve every job. Sole ownership preserves quick control over repairs and the owner keeps the profit. It also leaves the owner carrying the business risk and may make a larger equipment purchase difficult to fund. Employing an assistant does not turn it into a partnership.

### Partnerships

An ordinary partnership is a business owned by two or more people who agree to operate together. In the syllabus model, partners share decisions and profits and have unlimited liability; it is not a limited company with shareholders. A partnership agreement can clarify contributions, responsibilities, profit sharing and how disagreements or departure will be handled. Pooling funds may make investment easier than for one of the partners alone, while complementary skills can improve decisions and share the workload. The disadvantages include conflict, divided control and the need to share profit. A partner may face consequences from commitments made on behalf of the partnership, so trust and clear authority matter. Simply adding another owner does not prove the business is incorporated or has limited liability. Different specialised legal arrangements should not be assumed when the case states an ordinary partnership.

### Putting the idea to work

Two fictional mechanics form Leni Repairs. One contributes technical expertise and the other contributes savings and manages orders. Their complementary resources may support more work than either could manage alone. They must agree how profits and decisions are shared, and in this ordinary-partnership model both face unlimited liability rather than shareholder protection.

### Private limited companies

A private limited company is an incorporated enterprise owned by shareholders, with a separate legal identity and normally limited shareholder liability. Its shares are not offered freely to the general public. It can suit a restricted group of owners who want continuity and the ability to raise equity within that group. A public limited company can offer shares to the public; a private limited company retains restrictions on access and transfer. Limited liability normally limits shareholders’ exposure to their investment or agreed unpaid share contribution; it does not promise that lenders will be repaid. The enterprise can continue despite a shareholder leaving, since it has a separate identity. Disadvantages include administration, sharing control with other shareholders and a narrower potential share market than a public limited company. Share funding is not an automatic entitlement: someone must be willing to invest.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nima Furnishings is a private limited company whose founders invite one relative to subscribe for new shares. The arrangement can bring equity while retaining a restricted ownership circle. The founders must share ownership rights and dividends with the new shareholder. They cannot assume the same access to public share funding as a public limited company.

### Public limited companies

A public limited company is an incorporated enterprise with normally limited shareholder liability that can offer shares to the general public. A private limited company restricts that access; both forms can be privately owned and therefore belong to the private sector. Public share access can widen the potential pool of equity finance and help large investment, although investors must still find the offer attractive. Existing owners may lose some voting influence as more shares are issued. Numerous shareholders and professional management can separate ownership from daily control. Wider ownership also brings formal disclosure, administration and accountability demands. A public limited company is not the same as a public corporation owned by the state. Classify ownership by who holds and controls the business, not by the single word public.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume fictional Kato Equipment seeks funds from a wide range of private investors through a public limited company. A wider share offer may support its large investment proposal, but the founders may have to accept reduced voting influence and more formal reporting. It remains private sector if private investors own and control it.

### Co-operatives

A co-operative is owned by members who join to meet shared needs and provide a mutual benefit. Democratic member influence, commonly on a one-member-one-vote basis, distinguishes its purpose from simply rewarding the largest investor. Producer members might combine purchasing, processing or marketing, while consumer members might obtain supplies through a shared store. Members can gain bargaining strength, share facilities and spread costs they could not bear alone. Surpluses can be retained or distributed according to the co-operative’s rules and member participation. Collective decisions can be slower, members may disagree about priorities, and raising capital can be difficult if outside investors have limited influence. A co-operative can earn a surplus; its shared-benefit purpose does not mean that expenses disappear.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Nawa Growers Co-operative combines its members’ vegetable deliveries into one collection service. Members may reach buyers more reliably and share transport expenses. They must agree collection times and contributions; a dispute between large and small growers can slow changes even when the combined service is useful.

### Close corporations

A close corporation is an incorporated ownership form with a separate legal identity, normally limited member liability and ownership described through members’ interests rather than company shares. It can suit a small group whose members participate directly in running the enterprise. Do not call the owners shareholders or assume it raises funds by a public share issue. Its advantages include continuity distinct from an individual owner and the normal protection of limited liability. A member-managed structure can retain close control, but members must coordinate decisions and provide or obtain sufficient finance. A narrow ownership group may restrict access to capital and disagreements can obstruct management. The limited-liability model does not remove debts owed by the corporation or any extra commitment specifically undertaken by a member. Precise membership limits and legal procedures are outside this explanation.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Ehalo Metals CC has members who jointly manage a workshop. They seek funds for a larger machine. Its owners hold members’ interests, not publicly traded company shares. They can discuss contributions or borrowing, but cannot treat a public share sale as an automatic feature of a close corporation. Separate identity supports continuity while their limited resources can constrain investment.

### Public corporations

A public corporation is a state-owned enterprise established to carry out a public business or service function. It is managed separately from ordinary departmental activity while remaining publicly owned and accountable. Its objectives can combine service access and financial performance. State support and a broad service mandate may enable provision that a private supplier would find commercially unattractive. However, the resources have an opportunity cost, and political influence or weak incentives may impede decisions. Neither efficiency nor inefficiency follows automatically from ownership. Distinguish a public corporation from a public limited company: the former is state owned, whereas public availability of company shares does not itself imply state ownership.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume fictional Regional Water Works is a public corporation required to serve low-density communities. Its service mandate can support continued water access where revenue is low, but someone must finance the infrastructure. Assess access and financial use together instead of assuming that charging a fee makes it private sector.

### Joint ventures

A joint venture is an arrangement in which independent parties combine resources for an agreed business activity or project and share its risks and rewards under their agreement. It may be temporary or continue while the project operates; it is not necessarily a complete merger of the participants. One partner can supply local market knowledge while another supplies equipment or finance. Sharing expertise and risk can make a project possible that neither could undertake alone. The disadvantages include divided rewards, conflicting objectives and disputes about control or contributions. A successful arrangement needs clear responsibilities and an agreed basis for decisions. Do not assume a particular legal identity or liability merely from the phrase joint venture; use any stated organisational arrangements.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Hila Builders and Omu Solar collaborate to supply solar-powered school shelters. Hila provides construction expertise and Omu provides electrical equipment. Their complementary skills support the project without requiring each firm to buy all the other’s capabilities. They must agree responsibility for delays and how income is shared. Both can retain their separate operations outside the venture.

### Franchises

A franchise is an agreement in which the franchisor permits a franchisee to use its brand and business format under stated conditions. The franchisor grants the right; the franchisee pays for and operates under that right. A franchisee can gain an established identity, operating guidance and promotional support rather than starting an unknown format from nothing. These advantages can reduce some start-up uncertainty but do not guarantee sales or profit. Fees and continuing payments add costs, and rules may restrict products, suppliers, prices or appearance according to the agreement. For a franchisor, franchisees’ investment can support expansion and fee income, but poor service at an outlet can damage the shared brand and standards require monitoring. The outlet’s ownership form and the franchise contract are distinct: the franchisee could operate through a sole trader or incorporated business.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Tara Bowl grants Selma the right to run an outlet under its name and menu. Selma pays an initial fee and continuing payments and must use the agreed menu. Tara Bowl is the franchisor and Selma is the franchisee. Selma gains a recognised format but gives up menu freedom and pays fees. Tara can expand through Selma’s investment but risks brand damage if the outlet disappoints customers.

### Worked practice: Appropriate ownership and change of form

Fictional Nima Bakery in Okahandja is an ordinary partnership owned by two bakers. They need finance for an oven. A relative offers equity investment only if the business becomes a private limited company. The bakers want a small ownership group and normally limited liability. The proposed investor would obtain a minority interest; assume no personal guarantees and no desire to offer shares to the general public.

a Explain how converting Nima from an ordinary partnership to a private limited company would affect finance and control. [8]
b Evaluate private limited company status against a public limited company for Nima’s stated circumstances. [12]

a Ordinary partners share decisions and profits and have unlimited liability in this model. A company can issue the proposed shares to the relative, securing the offered equity rather than a repayment obligation for that equity. Ownership rights and future distributions would now be shared with a third investor. The founders’ percentage influence would decline, although the stated minority interest does not itself give that investor control. Incorporation brings normal limited shareholder liability and continuity but more formal administration. These effects depend on an actual subscription and acceptable rights, not merely a change in name. b Both private and public limited companies are incorporated with normally limited shareholder liability. A private company can accommodate the one relative and the desired restricted ownership circle. A public company can seek a wider pool of shareholders, which may help a much larger financing requirement, but wider ownership and disclosure could dilute the bakers’ desired control and add demands they do not need for this proposal. Choose private limited status: the stated investor offers the needed finance without a public offer. A public company would be more plausible if finance requirements exceeded what restricted investors could supply and the founders accepted wider participation. Neither form guarantees that the oven will earn a return; investment suitability remains a separate decision.

### Worked practice: Appropriate ownership and change of form

Fictional Ekuva Cycle Care in Rundu is owned by a sole trader who has sufficient funds for its current two-person service. The owner values sole control and is considering an ordinary partnership with a mechanic, who would contribute skills but no needed additional finance. There is currently no shortage of technical skill in the business. For part (b) only, the owner’s only experienced mechanic leaves and cannot be replaced by hiring at an affordable wage. The proposed partner can now supply the missing technical expertise; all other facts remain unchanged.

a Evaluate forming the ordinary partnership under the original facts, compared with remaining sole owned. [12]
b Evaluate that choice after the stated loss of the experienced mechanic. Explain whether the decision should change. [12]

a A sole trader owns the enterprise alone; an ordinary partnership shares ownership, decisions and profit and retains unlimited liability in this model. Ekuva already has enough money and skill for its two-person service. A partner could share the workload and bring another view, but the facts do not identify a shortage that requires giving up control. Remaining sole owned better matches the owner’s stated preference while current resources suffice. This is a conditional choice about Ekuva, not a claim that partnership is generally inferior. b The unavailable mechanic creates a decisive expertise shortage that the proposed partner can fill. Without it, the owner may be unable to maintain the service; sharing profit is less damaging than losing the capacity to earn it. Partnership would still mean joint decisions, potential disagreement and unlimited liability, so the owners should agree responsibilities and profit sharing. Form the partnership if those arrangements are acceptable, because the partner now supplies an essential resource that hiring cannot affordably replace. The recommendation changes because the value of the contribution changes, while the control cost remains.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain which party is the franchisor and which is the franchisee. Fictional Temba Snacks licenses its name and operating format to Rauna, who pays fees and runs the new outlet.

Temba Snacks is the franchisor because it grants the brand and format rights. Rauna is the franchisee because she buys and uses those rights under the agreement. Fees flow from the franchisee to the franchisor in this case; running the local outlet does not make Rauna the owner of the whole brand.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain why a proposal for fictional Holo CC to sell ordinary company shares uses the wrong ownership vocabulary. Holo remains a close corporation and its current owners are members.

Close-corporation owners hold members’ interests, not ordinary company shares. They may change contributions or membership under the relevant arrangements, but a public share issue is not a feature to assume for the stated CC. Company shareholders and close-corporation members describe different forms; changing the vocabulary does not change the form.

### Keeping the ideas apart

Explain the difference in owners’ normal liability between an ordinary partnership and a private limited company. Assume no separate personal guarantees or wrongdoing in either fictional business.

In the ordinary-partnership syllabus model, partners have unlimited liability and personal assets can be exposed to business debts. A private limited company has separate legal identity and shareholders normally have limited liability tied to their investment or agreed contribution. Calling partners shareholders wrongly transfers company protection to an ordinary partnership. Limited liability protects owners in the stated way; it does not promise repayment to creditors.

## Choosing an appropriate form

### Choosing an appropriate form

Choose ownership by matching the arrangement to the circumstances. A sole trader may suit a modest owner-managed activity requiring quick decisions and little finance. A partnership can pool skills and money when partners can agree on decisions and profit sharing. A private limited company may suit a group seeking continuity and limited liability while retaining a restricted ownership circle; a public limited company may suit much wider equity funding when owners accept wider participation and scrutiny. A co-operative fits members seeking a shared benefit and democratic influence. A close corporation can suit a small group wanting incorporated ownership with member involvement. A public corporation may fit a state service objective. A joint venture can combine complementary resources for a specific project, whereas a franchise can provide an established brand and business format in exchange for fees and restrictions. Compare actual advantages and costs rather than ranking forms universally. Availability of finance, willingness to share control, liability, purpose, continuity and the owners’ skills can point in different directions. A supported decision identifies which constraint is decisive and states when an alternative would be preferable.

### Putting the idea to work

Fictional Sera Foods needs another owner’s investment for new ovens. Its founder wants to retain a small ownership group, and both investors are concerned about their personal exposure to business debt. Assume no personal guarantees are required. A private limited company meets the stated preference for a restricted ownership group and normally limited shareholder liability better than an ordinary partnership. It brings administration and shared decisions, so it is worthwhile only if the investment and risk benefits justify those costs. A public limited company would invite wider ownership than the founder currently wants. On these facts choose the private limited form, while recognising that a very small, low-risk start could have remained sole owned.

## When ownership and control separate

### When ownership and control separate

Ownership is the right to hold the enterprise or an interest in it. Control concerns directing its decisions. In a small owner-managed enterprise the same person often provides finance and directs daily work. As a company grows, numerous shareholders may own shares while directors and professional managers make operational decisions. The board sets direction and oversees management; shareholders retain ownership rights such as voting under the ownership arrangements, but most do not run individual shifts. This is separation, or divorce, of ownership from control. It can bring specialist management and free owners from daily supervision. It can also create a conflict of interests: managers might favour expansion, status or their remuneration when owners prefer a higher return. Reporting, oversight and appropriate performance objectives can help align the interests, though they do not make every conflict disappear. Growth does not inevitably remove an owner from control; a founder with a controlling interest may retain strong influence. Explain who owns and who actually decides in the given situation.

### Putting the idea to work

Ombutu Packaging has many small shareholders. A professional manager chooses daily work schedules and proposes a larger head office. Shareholders want improved returns. Day-to-day control lies with management although ownership remains with shareholders. A larger office might benefit coordination, but it could also reflect managerial preferences. The board should assess its business benefit against cost and owners’ objectives instead of assuming all expansion serves shareholders.

## Consequences of changing ownership form

### Consequences of changing ownership form

A change of form can alter finance, control, liability, continuity and administrative demands. Adding a partner may bring skills and capital but requires sharing decisions and profit. Incorporating as a company normally separates the enterprise’s identity from its owners and limits shareholder liability, while creating more formal reporting and administration. Moving from a private limited company to a public limited company can open a wider potential market for shares but may dilute existing owners’ control. Private and public limited companies differ in access to share ownership; both belong to the private sector when privately owned. Taking a franchise can offer a tested format but commits the outlet to fees and conditions. A change should solve a stated problem rather than follow the belief that a larger-sounding form is better. Check transition costs, the owners’ willingness to accept new obligations and whether finance will actually be available. Limited liability does not generate cash or remove a lender’s risk. It concerns an owner’s normal liability and is separate from any additional personal commitments expressly assumed.

### Putting the idea to work

Assume fictional Onka Printing is an ordinary partnership that needs new machinery. A private investor is prepared to subscribe for shares only if it becomes a private limited company. The partners would share ownership with the investor but wish to avoid an unrestricted personal liability for future company debts; assume no separate guarantees. Conversion would make the stated share investment possible and normally limit shareholder liability. The partners would give up some control and accept company administration. Convert if the investment and continuity justify those costs and the proposed voting arrangement is acceptable. Without a willing investor, conversion alone would not pay for machinery. A public limited company would widen potential ownership further but is unnecessary for this one-investor proposal.
