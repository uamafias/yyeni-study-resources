**What this topic asks of you**

- Chemical masses at this level are relative numbers. They compare an atom, molecule or formula unit with one twelfth of the mass of one carbon-12 atom. Because both masses are divided by the same reference mass, relative masses have no unit. The carbon-12 reference gives chemists a consistent scale for atoms that are too small to weigh individually in grams.
- A mole is a counting amount, just as a dozen means twelve objects. One mole contains the Avogadro constant number of specified entities, approximately 6.02 × 10²³. The entity must be named: atoms, molecules, ions, electrons or formula units. One mole of oxygen atoms and one mole of oxygen molecules contain the same number of named entities, but the molecules contain twice as many oxygen atoms.
- A mass spectrum provides peak positions and relative intensities. For an atomic spectrum, a peak position corresponds to an isotopic mass and its intensity shows relative abundance. Interpretation begins with the species named in the question. A spectrum of atoms is not read in the same way as the molecular-ion region of a diatomic element.
- An empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. A molecular formula gives the actual number of each kind of atom in one molecule. Ethene has molecular formula C2H4 and empirical formula CH2. A molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula, but the multiplier is not known from percentage composition alone. Later organic work also distinguishes structural condensed, displayed and skeletal formula representations; those show connectivity or shape in ways an empirical formula does not.
- A chemical equation communicates identities, states and reacting ratios. First write correct chemical formulae. Then change coefficients until every element has the same atom count on each side. Do not alter formula subscripts. Add state symbols when the substances' states are known: (s), (l), (g) and (aq), where aqueous means dissolved in water.

## What relative mass means

### Four related quantities

Relative isotopic mass refers to one specified isotope. Carbon-13 therefore has a relative isotopic mass close to 13. Relative atomic mass, Ar, is different: it is the weighted mean for the naturally occurring isotopes of an element. Its value can therefore be non-integral. Relative molecular mass, Mr, adds the Ar values of all atoms in one molecule. For CO2, Mr = 12.0 + (2 × 16.0) = 44.0. Relative formula mass is found in the same way for substances described by formula units rather than separate molecules. For MgO it is 24.3 + 16.0 = 40.3. Calling this a molecular mass would wrongly imply that ionic magnesium oxide exists as separate MgO molecules.

### Choosing the correct mass

A sample contains chlorine atoms, Cl2 molecules and sodium chloride. Use relative atomic mass for one chlorine atom or for the average mass of chlorine atoms in nature. Use relative molecular mass for Cl2 because it consists of molecules: Mr = 2Ar(Cl). Use relative formula mass for NaCl because the formula represents the simplest ratio of ions in its lattice. The arithmetic is similar, but the name records what kind of chemical entity the formula represents.

### Where this leads

Relative masses become molar masses when expressed in g mol⁻¹. That connection is used throughout stoichiometry. Atomic structure in topic 1.2 explains isotopes more fully, while the ideal-gas topic 1.4 develops gas calculations beyond the fixed molar volumes used here.

### Consolidation and checks

A relative mass answers a comparison question, so it is written without g or kg. The numerical value becomes a molar mass when the quantity is one mole and the unit g mol⁻¹ is attached. For example, Ar(Na) is 23.0, while the molar mass of sodium is 23.0 g mol⁻¹. Keeping the quantity and unit separate prevents a relative-mass answer from being given a mass unit.

## Counting chemical particles

### Amount of substance

Amount of substance has symbol n and unit mol. The particle relationship is n = N/NA, where N is the number of specified particles and NA is the Avogadro constant. Conversely, N = nNA. If a sample contains 0.250 mol of sodium ions, the number of ions is 0.250 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.505 × 10²³, reported as 1.51 × 10²³ ions to three significant figures.

### From mass to moles

Molar mass is the mass of one mole. Its numerical value follows from relative mass but it carries the unit g mol⁻¹. Water has Mr = 18.0, so its molar mass is 18.0 g mol⁻¹. For 4.50 g of water, n = m/M = 4.50 ÷ 18.0 = 0.250 mol. This amount contains 0.250 mol of water molecules, 0.500 mol of hydrogen atoms and 0.250 mol of oxygen atoms. The formula decides how particle counts are related.

### Moles connect measurements to equations

A balance measures mass, a gas syringe measures volume and volumetric apparatus measures solution volume. The mole converts each measurement into the common amount used by a balanced equation. That is why later calculations first convert the given quantity to moles, apply the equation ratio, then convert to the requested quantity.

### Consolidation and checks

The specified entity controls the conversion. One mole of Na2CO3 contains one mole of formula units, two moles of sodium ions and three moles of oxygen atoms. A question asking for ions therefore needs the formula subscript after the amount of formula units has been found. If 0.100 mol Na2CO3 is present, there are 0.200 mol Na+ ions, equal to 0.200 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²³ sodium ions. State the entity with the answer so that the count is meaningful.

## Reading isotope evidence

### Atomic and molecular peaks

Suppose an element X has isotopes of mass 35 and 37. Its atomic spectrum has two isotope peaks. Molecules X2 can contain 35-35, 35-37 or 37-37, producing molecular-ion masses 70, 72 and 74. Three molecular peaks therefore arise from two isotopes. The middle mass can be formed in two arrangements, so its expected relative intensity also reflects the number of combinations. The discriminating question is whether the peak represents one atom's ion or a molecule made from two atoms.

### A weighted mean from percentages

An element has isotopes of mass 24, 25 and 26 with abundances 79.0%, 10.0% and 11.0%. Multiply each mass by its abundance, add, and divide by the total abundance: Ar = [(24 × 79.0) + (25 × 10.0) + (26 × 11.0)] ÷ 100. The numerator is 1896 + 250 + 286 = 2432. Then 2432 ÷ 100 = 24.32. The answer lies close to 24 because mass 24 is the most abundant isotope.

### Peak heights that are not percentages

A spectrum shows isotope peaks at 63 and 65 with relative heights 9 and 4. The heights total 13, not 100. The correct mean is [(63 × 9) + (65 × 4)] ÷ 13 = (567 + 260) ÷ 13 = 827 ÷ 13 = 63.615..., so Ar = 63.6 to three significant figures. Dividing by 100 would give 8.27, which is outside the two isotopic masses and cannot be their weighted mean. A quick range check catches the error.

### Percentage by mass

For an element E in a compound, percentage by mass = (total Ar of E in the formula ÷ Mr or relative formula mass of the compound) × 100%. The word total matters because a subscript counts repeated atoms. In ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3, nitrogen contributes 2 × 14.0 = 28.0 while the relative formula mass is 80.0. The nitrogen percentage is 28.0 ÷ 80.0 × 100% = 35.0%. Counting only one nitrogen atom would halve the numerator incorrectly.

### Comparing composition

Calcium carbonate, CaCO3, has relative formula mass 40.1 + 12.0 + (3 × 16.0) = 100.1. Oxygen contributes 48.0, so its percentage by mass is 48.0 ÷ 100.1 × 100% = 47.952...%, or 48.0% to three significant figures. Percentages compare masses, not numbers of atoms: oxygen is three of the five atoms in the formula unit, but it is not 60% of the mass because the atoms have different relative masses.

### Reasonableness checks

A weighted mean must lie between the smallest and largest isotopic masses. A percentage by mass must lie from 0% to 100%. The mean should be pulled towards the more intense peak, and an element with a larger total mass contribution should have a larger percentage. These checks do not replace working, but they reveal an inverted ratio, a missed subscript or the wrong abundance total before the answer is accepted.

### Scope and later use

Only the masses and abundances shown by a spectrum are needed here; an account of the instrument is outside this objective. Weighted means and percentage composition feed directly into empirical-formula work in 1.1.4 and into reacting-mass calculations in 1.1.5.

### Consolidation and checks

Consider chlorine atoms with isotope peaks at 35 and 37 in a 3:1 intensity ratio. Using the heights directly, Ar = [(35 × 3) + (37 × 1)] ÷ 4 = 142 ÷ 4 = 35.5. If the same information is written as 75% and 25%, the result is [(35 × 75) + (37 × 25)] ÷ 100 = 3550 ÷ 100 = 35.5. The two methods agree because a weighted mean depends on relative proportions, not on whether the abundance scale totals 4, 100 or another value. For Cl2 molecular ions, the possible mass combinations are 35 + 35 = 70, 35 + 37 = 72 and 37 + 37 = 74. The existence of the mass-72 peak does not identify a third isotope of mass 36; it is the mixed-isotope molecule. Identify the species represented before interpreting the number of peaks.

A percentage-by-mass calculation uses the complete formula. For hydrated or bracketed formulae, expand every multiplier before adding contributions. In Al2(SO4)3, aluminium contributes 2 × 27.0 = 54.0, sulfur contributes 3 × 32.1 = 96.3, and oxygen contributes 12 × 16.0 = 192.0. The formula mass is 342.3. Aluminium therefore contributes 54.0 ÷ 342.3 × 100% = 15.776...%, or 15.8% to three significant figures. A learner who counts four oxygen atoms has ignored the multiplier outside the brackets. As a final check, the separate element percentages for a pure compound should total about 100%, allowing for rounding.

Interpretation questions may give a graph, a peak table or a list of intensities. Translate each representation into the same two ideas: mass and relative abundance. A taller peak means a larger proportion, not a heavier isotope. A peak farther to the right has greater mass, not necessarily greater abundance. Relative atomic mass combines both axes through the weighted-mean calculation. It is not normally a whole number because naturally occurring samples contain isotopes in proportions. If a calculated value lies outside the mass range, revisit the denominator and the multiplication before rounding. Recalculate from the original heights rather than from rounded intermediate percentages, because early rounding can move the final weighted mean. Keep at least one extra digit during working and round after the weighted sum has been divided by the abundance total.

## Two kinds of formula

### From composition to empirical formula

Treat percentage data as masses in a 100 g sample. Divide each mass by the element's Ar to obtain moles. Divide every mole value by the smallest. If the ratios are close to whole numbers, round only after considering the precision of the data. If a ratio is close to 1.5, 1.33 or 1.25, multiply all ratios by 2, 3 or 4 respectively. Multiplying only one ratio destroys the relative composition.

### Empirical formula from percentages

A compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen by mass. For 100 g: carbon moles = 40.0 ÷ 12.0 = 3.333; hydrogen moles = 6.7 ÷ 1.0 = 6.700; oxygen moles = 53.3 ÷ 16.0 = 3.331. Divide by 3.331 to obtain approximately 1.00 : 2.01 : 1.00. The simplest ratio is 1 : 2 : 1, so the empirical formula is CH2O. Formulae do not include decimal subscripts.

### From empirical to molecular formula

The empirical-formula mass of CH2O is 12.0 + 2.0 + 16.0 = 30.0. If the molecule has Mr = 180, the multiplier is 180 ÷ 30.0 = 6. The molecular formula is therefore C6H12O6. The multiplier must be a whole number because a molecule contains whole atoms. A non-integral result signals inconsistent data, incorrect relative masses or a wrong empirical formula.

### Using combustion data

Complete combustion converts all carbon in an organic sample to carbon dioxide and all hydrogen to water. If 0.880 g of CO2 forms, n(CO2) = 0.880 ÷ 44.0 = 0.0200 mol, so n(C) = 0.0200 mol. If 0.450 g of water forms, n(H2O) = 0.450 ÷ 18.0 = 0.0250 mol, so n(H) = 2 × 0.0250 = 0.0500 mol. The ratio C:H is 0.0200:0.0500 = 2:5. Any oxygen in the original compound must be found from a mass difference before its mole amount is included.

### Formulae and equations

A correct formula is fixed before an equation is balanced. The empirical formula describes composition; equation coefficients describe reacting amounts. Keeping those roles separate prevents the common error of altering a subscript in order to balance a reaction.

### Consolidation and checks

Ratios close to simple fractions need careful scaling. If division by the smallest amount gives 1.00:1.50, multiply both values by 2 to obtain 2:3. For 1.00:1.33, multiply all values by 3 to obtain about 3:4. Do not round 1.50 to 2, because that changes the composition. The precision of the measured composition decides how close a ratio must be before it is recognised as a simple fraction.

When the compound contains oxygen, its mass may be measured directly or obtained by difference. Suppose a 1.50 g sample contains 0.600 g carbon and 0.100 g hydrogen. The oxygen mass is 1.50 − 0.600 − 0.100 = 0.800 g. The amounts are C: 0.600 ÷ 12.0 = 0.0500 mol; H: 0.100 ÷ 1.00 = 0.100 mol; O: 0.800 ÷ 16.0 = 0.0500 mol. Dividing by 0.0500 gives 1:2:1 and empirical formula CH2O. This subtraction is valid only when the sample is stated to contain those three elements.

The empirical formula cannot by itself distinguish compounds that share the same simplest ratio. CH2O could correspond to CH2O, C2H4O2 or C6H12O6. Mr supplies the multiplier. Conversely, if the calculated multiplier is 2.98, experimental rounding may justify 3; a value such as 2.4 does not. Check the original mole ratios and data rather than forcing a molecular formula.

## Equations as amount relationships

### Balancing with states

Magnesium burns in oxygen to form magnesium oxide. The correct formulae are Mg, O2 and MgO. Oxygen is diatomic, so begin with Mg + O2 → MgO. Put 2 before MgO to give two oxygen atoms on the right, then 2 before Mg to restore magnesium balance: 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s). The coefficients give the mole ratio 2:1:2.

### The stoichiometric route

For reacting-mass, gas-volume and solution calculations, use the same route. Convert the given quantity to moles. Use the balanced-equation coefficient ratio. Convert the resulting moles to the requested mass, gas volume, concentration or solution volume. Units are part of the data: mass uses molar mass, gas volume uses the molar volume at the stated temperature and pressure, and solution work uses c = n/V with V in dm³.

### Reacting masses

Calcium carbonate decomposes: CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). The mole ratio CaCO3:CaO is 1:1. For 25.0 g CaCO3, n = 25.0 ÷ 100.1 = 0.24975 mol. Therefore n(CaO) = 0.24975 mol. With M(CaO) = 56.1 g mol⁻¹, mass = 0.24975 × 56.1 = 14.011... g, so 14.0 g to three significant figures. Rounding the intermediate amount to 0.250 mol happens to be close here, but retaining digits protects the final answer.

### Gas volumes at r.t.p. and s.t.p.

Methane burns as CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l). At r.t.p., 0.350 mol CH4 needs 2 × 0.350 = 0.700 mol O2. Using 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at r.t.p., V(O2) = 0.700 × 24.0 = 16.8 dm³. If the same amount were specified at s.t.p., the value would be 0.700 × 22.4 = 15.68 dm³. The condition named in the question selects the molar volume; it is not chosen from memory without checking.

### Solutions

A sodium hydroxide solution has concentration 0.150 mol dm⁻³ and volume 25.0 cm³. Convert the volume: 25.0 cm³ ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 dm³. Then n = cV = 0.150 × 0.0250 = 0.00375 mol. Conversely, if 0.00600 mol is present in 40.0 cm³, c = n/V = 0.00600 ÷ 0.0400 = 0.150 mol dm⁻³. Substituting a volume in cm³ directly would make the result 1000 times wrong.

### Limiting amounts and significant figures

When amounts of two reactants are supplied, compare moles divided by their equation coefficients. The smaller quotient identifies the reactant that runs out first and therefore limits the product. Carry extra digits through the calculation, then round the final answer to a precision justified by the data. State the unit and, for gases, the temperature and pressure. A bare number does not communicate the measured quantity.

### Connections

Topic 1.4 replaces fixed molar-volume relationships with pV = nRT when pressure and temperature are supplied. Later inorganic and organic topics provide many reactions to which the same balancing and mole-ratio method applies.

### Consolidation and checks

Take a mole ratio from a balanced equation. For N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g), one mole of nitrogen reacts with three moles of hydrogen and forms two moles of ammonia. If 0.400 mol N2 and 0.900 mol H2 are mixed, compare 0.400 ÷ 1 with 0.900 ÷ 3. The smaller value is 0.300 for hydrogen, so hydrogen limits the reaction and produces 2 × 0.300 = 0.600 mol NH3. Nitrogen is in excess.

Direct gas-volume ratios are valid when the gaseous quantities are compared at the same temperature and pressure. Otherwise convert each volume to moles using the temperature and pressure supplied. In solution work, distinguish concentration from amount: concentration describes moles per dm³, while amount is the actual number of moles in the chosen volume. Reporting c = 0.150 mol without dm⁻³ confuses the two quantities. At the end, read the question again: the requested substance, temperature and pressure, unit and significant figures belong to the answer.
