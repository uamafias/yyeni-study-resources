## Carboxylic acids and derivatives — 4.7.1

### Reading the carboxyl group

A carboxylic acid contains the carboxyl group, –C(=O)OH. One oxygen is double-bonded to carbon and the other is single-bonded to that same carbon and to H. The carbon therefore has four bond contributions: two to carbonyl oxygen, one to hydroxyl oxygen and one to its remaining attachment. In methanoic acid the remaining attachment is H; in ethanoic acid it is CH3.

The saturated open-chain monocarboxylic acids with no other functional group follow CnH2nO2. The count n includes the carboxyl carbon. CH3COOH, ethanoic acid, has two carbons altogether, not a two-carbon alkyl group plus an extra carboxyl carbon. In structural condensed notation COOH groups the two oxygens clearly, but a displayed drawing must show C=O, C–O and O–H as separate bonds.

Molecular, empirical, structural condensed, displayed and skeletal forms convey different information. A molecular formula gives actual atom counts, an empirical formula their simplest ratio, and the connection forms retain bonding detail. For ethanoic acid these counts are molecular C2H4O2 and empirical CH2O. In a skeletal structure both oxygen labels and the OH hydrogen must be shown; an unlabelled line end is otherwise interpreted as carbon.

The OH in COOH is part of a carboxyl group, not a separate alcohol functional group on a saturated carbon. Likewise the C=O here does not make the whole compound an aldehyde or ketone, because its other attachment includes oxygen. Functional-group classification depends on the complete local arrangement. Topic 4.6 explains aldehyde and ketone connections, and this distinction prevents applying their names to a carboxylic acid merely because all contain C=O.

### Naming from the acid end

Replace the parent alkane ending with –oic acid and include the COOH carbon in the parent. The straight-chain one-to-six-carbon members are methanoic acid, ethanoic acid, propanoic acid, butanoic acid, pentanoic acid and hexanoic acid. The carboxyl carbon is carbon 1 in these open-chain names. Do not number from the opposite hydrocarbon end just because that end is printed on the left.

CH3CH2CH2COOH has a four-carbon parent, including COOH, and is butanoic acid. CH3CH(CH3)COOH is 2-methylpropanoic acid: its parent route has three carbons and its branch supplies another. The whole molecule still has four carbons. Calling it methylbutanoic acid would imply a longer parent and a different atom count.

To construct a named acid, draw the parent chain with its first carbon as C(=O)OH. Attach branches at the stated numbered positions and complete the remaining carbon valencies. Do not put a hydrogen on the carboxyl carbon as well as a carbon chain, a C=O bond and an OH bond: that would exceed four bond contributions. The H written at the end of COOH belongs on oxygen.

As a quick practice comparison, CH3CH2COOH is propanoic acid whereas CH3CH2CHO is propanal. Both names include the terminal carbon, but the acid has two oxygens and the aldehyde one. The endings communicate that functional-group difference; they are not interchangeable spellings for the same molecule. Topic 4.1 introduces shared parent stems, and Topic 4.5 explains how a changed skeleton or functional group can create a structural-isomer relationship when total composition is held constant.

### Counting the acid atoms

Start with the carbon skeleton, including the carboxyl carbon and any branches. Count the two oxygens of COOH separately. Then count carbon-bound hydrogens and add the H bonded to oxygen. The carboxyl carbon of RCOOH has no H of its own when its remaining bond goes to R. In HCOOH, the simplest acid, its remaining bond goes to H instead.

For CH3CH2COOH, the methyl group supplies three hydrogens, the CH2 supplies two and OH supplies one. There are three carbons, six hydrogens and two oxygens, giving C3H6O2. For (CH3)2CHCOOH, the two methyl groups supply six hydrogens, CH supplies one and OH one, giving C4H8O2. The open-chain saturated monocarboxylic-acid relationship gives the same check for n=4.

In skeletal notation, double lines joining carbon and O represent one C=O bond to one oxygen atom, while the separate OH label identifies the second oxygen and its hydrogen. Do not count each line stroke as an atom or mistake a labelled O endpoint for an omitted carbon. A drawing's visual length does not determine its molecular size; vertices, unlabelled ends and printed atom labels do.

If requested to give a molecular formula, use actual counts. Molecular, empirical, structural condensed, displayed and skeletal representations answer different requests: empirical C2H4O describes the simplest ratio for C4H8O2, while the structural forms can distinguish butanoic acid from 2-methylpropanoic acid. Neither molecular composition nor Mr can distinguish that pair by itself. Topic 1.1 provides the arithmetic for Mr once the correct atom count is known; connectivity remains necessary to assign the acid's name.

### Oxidation routes to acids

A primary alcohol can be oxidised through an aldehyde to a carboxylic acid. For ethanol the sequence is CH3CH2OH → CH3CHO → CH3COOH. The carbon skeleton retains two carbons throughout. The first stage removes hydrogen to make C=O, while the further stage converts the aldehyde group to COOH. An extra oxygen does not imply an extra carbon.

Organic equations can represent the oxidation with [O]. Ethanol to ethanoic acid is CH3CH2OH + 2[O] → CH3COOH + H2O. Ethanal to the same acid is CH3CHO + [O] → CH3COOH. Distinguish the number of oxidation equivalents needed from the two starting materials; an aldehyde has already undergone the first oxidation stage.

Heat a primary alcohol with excess acidified dichromate(VI) under reflux when the intended endpoint is the acid. The condenser returns volatile material, allowing continued contact with oxidant. In contrast, removing the aldehyde as it forms is used to limit further oxidation when that intermediate is wanted. The oxidant changes from orange towards green as it is reduced, but the colour change alone does not establish a particular isolated organic product.

For example, complete oxidation of 0.0500 mol ethanol can give at most 0.0500 mol ethanoic acid. With Mr(acid)=60.0 the theoretical mass is 3.00 g. Actual recovery can be lower because of incomplete conversion or separation losses. A secondary alcohol normally gives a ketone under the corresponding mild oxidation route, so do not predict a same-skeleton acid from any alcohol merely because it contains OH. Topic 4.4 explains alcohol classification; Topic 4.6 explains the aldehyde intermediate and why its carbonyl attachments matter.

### Salt formation and ester formation

A carboxylic acid forms a carboxylate salt by losing its acidic hydrogen. With magnesium, 2CH3COOH + Mg → (CH3COO)2Mg + H2. The ethanoate ion has charge −1, so two are needed for Mg2+. The hydrogen gas arises from the acid's hydrogen, not from conversion of carbon into gas. Suitable reactive metals are needed; do not infer identical behaviour for any metal.

With sodium hydroxide, CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O. This neutralisation retains the acid's carbon skeleton. With sodium carbonate, 2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2. A carbonate therefore gives carbon dioxide effervescence, whereas the hydroxide equation does not. Balance the two acid hydrogens against the carbonate group's conversion to water and carbon dioxide.

Heating a carboxylic acid with an alcohol and an acid catalyst can form an ester. CH3COOH + CH3OH ⇌ CH3COOCH3 + H2O gives methyl ethanoate. At fixed temperature this esterification is reversible, so mixing equal amounts does not guarantee complete conversion. The catalyst speeds approach to equilibrium without determining its position at that temperature. Product amounts also depend on the starting proportions and whether a product is removed.

For an unfamiliar acid, keep RCOO together in its salt and check the metal charge. For an unfamiliar ester, identify the acid-derived carbonyl part and the alcohol-derived group attached through oxygen. These two bookkeeping methods preserve the connections rather than treating the overall composition as a list of freely rearrangeable atoms. Topic 2.3 provides equilibrium reasoning, while the next unit explains how ester names and hydrolysis reverse the connection between acid and alcohol components.

## Carboxylic acids and derivatives — 4.7.2

### Ester and primary-amine structures

An ester contains the connection R–C(=O)–O–R′, with a carbon group attached through the single-bonded oxygen. It differs from a carboxylic acid because that oxygen bears a carbon group instead of H. For methyl ethanoate, CH3C(=O)OCH3, the carbonyl carbon and its methyl group form the acid-derived part; the methyl group after oxygen is the alcohol-derived part.

A primary amine has one carbon-containing group attached to nitrogen and two N–H bonds: RNH2. In ethanamine, CH3CH2NH2, nitrogen makes three single bonds and has a lone pair. A molecule such as CH3CH(NH2)CH2CH3 is still a primary amine because nitrogen has one carbon-group attachment, even though the carbon bearing NH2 is attached to two other carbons. Alcohol and amine classification count different centres.

Molecular, empirical, structural condensed, displayed and skeletal forms must preserve the requested information. Molecular and empirical formulae give actual counts and simplest ratios respectively. Structural condensed, displayed and skeletal representations retain connections. In skeletal notation write O, N and their attached H atoms explicitly. A printed NH2 endpoint is nitrogen, not an extra carbon with an omitted label.

For saturated open-chain primary monoamines without other functional groups, composition is CnH2n+3N. Saturated open-chain monoesters without other functional groups follow CnH2nO2, with n counting both sides and the carbonyl carbon. These relationships require the stated class restrictions. Equal acid and ester atom counts do not mean equal functional groups: inspect whether the single-bonded oxygen has H or carbon attached. Topic 4.3 shows primary-amine formation from a halogenoalkane; detailed additional amine reaction schemes are outside this unit's task.

### Naming the two parts correctly

Name an ester by identifying the group attached through the single-bonded oxygen first. That alcohol-derived group supplies an alkyl name, such as methyl or ethyl. The carbonyl-containing part supplies the alkanoate name from its parent acid. CH3COOCH3 is methyl ethanoate; CH3COOCH2CH3 is ethyl ethanoate. Count the acid's carbonyl carbon when choosing ethanoate, propanoate and related names.

CH3CH2COOCH3 is methyl propanoate, not propyl ethanoate. The three carbons on the acid side include C=O, while the one after oxygen gives methyl. Swapping the words without checking the connections can produce a different molecule even when the total number of carbons happens to match. To draw from a name, construct the named acid-derived C(=O)O part and then attach the specified alkyl group to its single-bonded oxygen.

The syllabus uses names such as 2-aminobutane for CH3CH(NH2)CH2CH3: choose the carbon parent and number the amino attachment. The equivalent suffix style butan-2-amine identifies the same connectivity. For CH3CH2NH2 the simple name ethanamine is also used. Retain the primary NH2 arrangement regardless of naming style.

Check an amine drawing by giving nitrogen one carbon bond and two H bonds, rather than adding an extra carbon group because the carbon parent is branched. For an ester, check both the acid-derived and alcohol-derived carbon counts separately before adding them. These two habits distinguish a correct systematic name from a plausible-looking string of stems. Topic 4.1 introduces family naming, and the hydrolysis section below uses the same two ester parts to identify the products.

### Counting across oxygen or nitrogen

For an ester, count the carbonyl carbon, its attached carbon group and the group attached through oxygen. Do not stop counting when the chain reaches O. In CH3COOCH2CH3 there are four carbons: one methyl before C=O, the carbonyl carbon, then CH2 and CH3 after oxygen. Hydrogens total 3 + 0 + 2 + 3 = 8, and two oxygen atoms give C4H8O2.

For CH3CH2NH2, the carbon groups supply five hydrogens and nitrogen supplies two more, giving C2H7N. For CH3CH(NH2)CH2CH3, the carbon-bound hydrogen total is 3 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 9; add the two N–H hydrogens to obtain C4H11N. Omitting NH2 hydrogens would incorrectly describe the composition of the stated neutral amine.

At nitrogen, draw three single bonds and a lone pair for the neutral primary examples. At an ester carbonyl carbon, C=O uses two bond contributions and the two remaining connections use one each. The single-bonded ester oxygen has two bonds and no OH hydrogen. These local checks are more reliable than guessing counts from the name's longest stem alone.

If requested for a molecular formula, give actual totals. Molecular, empirical, structural condensed, displayed and skeletal forms remain different choices: empirical C2H4O is the simplest ratio for C4H8O2, while structural representations locate the atoms. To demonstrate the count, expand a supplied skeletal ester or amine into a labelled connectivity drawing, then tally atoms once each. Topic 1.1 covers relative mass calculations; the structural count must be correct before that arithmetic can give a meaningful result.

### Hydrolysis gives different acid and base products

Hydrolysis breaks an ester connection through reaction with water. Heating with dilute aqueous acid gives the parent carboxylic acid and alcohol. For methyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH3 + H2O ⇌ CH3COOH + CH3OH. At fixed temperature this acid-catalysed process is reversible, corresponding to the reverse of esterification. Excess water can favour hydrolysis, but an acid catalyst alone does not guarantee total conversion.

Heating the ester with aqueous sodium hydroxide gives sodium ethanoate and methanol: CH3COOCH3 + NaOH → CH3COONa + CH3OH. The immediate acid-derived product is the carboxylate salt, not free ethanoic acid. If acid is added after hydrolysis, the carboxylate can be protonated to recover the acid. That separate step must be stated when free acid is requested from a base-hydrolysis route.

For an unfamiliar ester, draw a division between the carbonyl carbon and the single-bonded oxygen. The portion R–C(=O) becomes RCOOH in the acidic route or RCOO− in the basic route. The portion O–R′ becomes HO–R′, the alcohol. Retain the oxygen-to-alkyl connection instead of separating the ester into arbitrary equal-carbon fragments.

For CH3CH2COOCH2CH3, acid hydrolysis gives propanoic acid, CH3CH2COOH, and ethanol, CH3CH2OH. Base hydrolysis with NaOH gives CH3CH2COONa and the same ethanol. Both routes conserve the five original carbons across their organic products. Check water or hydroxide in the balance, product charges and any separately added acid. Topic 2.3 supplies equilibrium language for the reversible acidic route; Topic 4.4 identifies the alcohol product.

### Linking ester properties to uses

Suitable esters are used as solvents because their polar ester group and carbon-containing portions can interact favourably with a range of organic solutes. Some also evaporate readily, allowing a dissolved coating or ingredient to remain after the solvent leaves. Ethyl ethanoate is an example of an ester solvent. Solubility depends on both the particular ester and solute; the presence of an ester linkage does not make a substance dissolve every material.

Many simple low-molecular-mass esters have characteristic pleasant or fruity odours. Enough molecules enter the vapour to reach the nose, making suitable compounds useful in perfume formulations and flavouring mixtures. The property belongs to particular structures and concentrations rather than to the mere word ester. Larger or differently substituted esters can have very different volatility and odour.

An original formulation comparison might specify that solvent A dissolves a resin and evaporates at a useful rate, while solvent B leaves the resin undissolved. Those observations support A for that stated task; they do not demonstrate that A is suitable for every formulation or direct consumption. Similarly, a pleasant smell alone does not establish that an unknown liquid is an ester or that it is appropriate as a food ingredient. Chemical identity and suitability require evidence beyond odour.

Distinguish a physical use from a reaction. Dissolving resin involves solute–solvent interactions; it does not imply ester hydrolysis. A perfume's scent likewise does not require breaking its ester bond during application. Topic 1.3 provides intermolecular-interaction reasoning, Topic 4.4 describes related solvent behaviour in alcohols, and the preceding section explains the deliberate aqueous acid or base settings that actually produce hydrolysis. Keep the useful property, application and relevant limitation linked in an explanation.
