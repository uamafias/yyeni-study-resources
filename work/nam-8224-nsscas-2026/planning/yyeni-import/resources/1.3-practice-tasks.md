# 1.3 Chemical bonding — practice tasks and worked answers



## ITEM-8224-1.3-P01

12 marks · Bloom: Apply

Identify the correct option in each original question. One mark per question. [12]

1. Which particles make up a sodium chloride crystal?
A. Neutral NaCl molecules
B. Na+ and Cl− ions
C. Sodium atoms in chlorine molecules
D. Free protons and neutrons

2. Which change produces Mg2+ from a neutral Mg atom?
A. Gain two electrons
B. Lose one proton
C. Lose two electrons
D. Gain two neutrons

3. What holds opposite ions together?
A. Electrostatic attraction
B. A shared proton
C. A hydrogen bond
D. Repulsion between like charges

4. Which charge-balanced ratio describes CaF2?
A. One Ca+ to one F−
B. Two Ca2+ to one F−
C. One Ca2+ to one F2−
D. One Ca2+ to two F−

5. Why does solid NaCl fail to conduct in the simple model?
A. It has no charged particles
B. Its ions cannot migrate through the lattice
C. Its electrons all leave the solid
D. Its ions have become neutral atoms

6. Which state permits the ions of an ionic compound to carry current?
A. A stationary dry crystal only
B. An isolated neutral molecule
C. The molten liquid
D. A solid with no mobile carriers

7. A sample conducts after melting but not as a solid. Which interpretation is strongest?
A. An ionic model with mobile liquid ions
B. A molecular gas with no charged particles
C. A metallic solid that has lost all electrons
D. No charged particles in either state

8. Which statement about ionic solubility is defensible?
A. Every ionic compound dissolves equally in water
B. No ionic compound dissolves
C. Ionic charge alone fixes exact solubility
D. Solubility depends on the balance of lattice and solute–solvent interactions

9. One Ca atom supplies two electrons when CaF2 forms. How many does each F atom receive?
A. Two
B. One
C. Four
D. Zero

10. A solid resists gentle heating and its melt conducts. Which paired explanation fits?
A. Weak molecular attraction and no carriers
B. Neutral molecules and fixed electrons
C. Strong lattice attraction and mobile liquid ions
D. No attraction and stationary ions

11. What is wrong with calling one transferred electron an ionic bond?
A. The transfer helps form ions, while the bonding is attraction between opposite ions
B. Electrons cannot move between atoms
C. Ionic compounds contain no electrons
D. The donor remains neutral after losing an electron

12. A dissolved ionic solute supplies fewer mobile ions per unit volume than another otherwise comparable solution. Which statement is reasonable?
A. The more dilute solution must be a metal
B. Both solutions contain no charges
C. The less concentrated solution cannot contain ions
D. The number of available carriers can affect conductivity



### Worked answer

Key: 1 B, 2 C, 3 A, 4 D, 5 B, 6 C, 7 A, 8 D, 9 B, 10 C, 11 A, 12 D.

1. B gives ions. A imposes discrete molecules; C retains the elemental particles; D names nuclear particles rather than the lattice ions.
2. C gives a 2+ charge. A gives a negative ion; B changes the element; D changes the isotope without the required charge.
3. A is ionic bonding. B invents a nuclear shared particle; C names a different interaction; D is repulsion rather than cohesion.
4. D balances +2 with two −1 charges. A uses the wrong calcium ion; B has excess positive charge; C assigns the wrong charge to a fluoride ion.
5. B identifies immobility. A denies the ions; C invents electron loss; D incorrectly removes ionic charge.
6. C permits ion migration. A keeps lattice ions fixed; B has no lattice ions; D explicitly lacks mobile carriers.
7. A accounts for both observations. B lacks liquid ionic carriers; C contradicts ordinary solid metallic conduction; D cannot account for liquid conductivity.
8. D identifies the relevant balance. A and B are opposing unsupported universals; C ignores the solvent and the full interaction balance.
9. B: two fluorine atoms each gain one. A doubles the required transfer; C exceeds the supplied total; D cannot form fluoride ions.
10. C connects heating and conductivity. A and B cannot explain ionic liquid conduction; D removes both cohesive attraction and carrier mobility.
11. A distinguishes formation from attraction. B denies transfer; C removes electrons from ions; D violates charge counting.
12. D recognises carrier concentration. A changes structure without evidence; B denies the supplied ions; C confuses lower concentration with absence.



### Marking guidance

Key and correct option: one mark per question. Every distractor error is explained in the canonical answer; an incorrect option earns no credit.



## ITEM-8224-1.3-P02

8 marks · Bloom: Apply

Explain the following linked comparisons.
(a) Describe a dot-and-cross representation of the ions in MgO, including their charges. [2]
(b) Explain why MgO requires substantial heating to disrupt its solid lattice. [2]
(c) Explain the molecular shapes and approximate bond angles of CO2 and H2O using their central electron regions. [3]
(d) State why the phrase “the atoms transferred electrons” does not fully define ionic bonding. [1]



### Worked answer

(a) [Mg]2+ and [O(••)(••)(•×)(•×)]2−, with the crosses supplied by magnesium. (b) The extended lattice contains strong electrostatic attractions between opposite ions, so disrupting the solid requires substantial energy. (c) CO2 has two bonding regions and no central lone pair: linear, 180°. H2O has two bonds and two central lone pairs: bent, about 104.5°, because lone-pair repulsion compresses the bond angle. (d) Transfer explains ion formation; ionic bonding is the attraction between the resulting opposite charges.



### Marking guidance

Part (a): correct ion charges, one mark; oxygen outer electron accounting with two transferred electrons, one mark. Part (b): opposite-ion attraction, one mark; extended strong lattice requiring energy to disrupt, one mark. Part (c): CO2 count/shape/angle, one mark; water count and shape, one mark; water angle and lone-pair explanation, one mark. Part (d): attraction distinguished from transfer, one mark. Each mark credits a distinct creditworthy point.



## ITEM-8224-1.3-P03

5 marks · Bloom: Apply

Calculate the quantities for 1.50 g of pure CaF2. Its molar mass is 78.1 g mol−1. Use Ca2+ and F− as the constituent ions.
(a) Use charge balance to calculate the number ratio Ca2+:F−. [1]
(b) Calculate the amount of CaF2 in the sample. [1]
(c) Calculate the amount of fluoride ions represented by the sample. [1]
(d) Calculate the total amount of ions represented by the sample, then state why those ions can carry current in a molten sample but not through its ordinary solid lattice. [2]



### Worked answer

(a) One Ca2+ charge balances two F− charges, giving 1:2. (b) n(CaF2) = m/M = 1.50/78.1 = 0.0192061 mol, reported as 0.0192 mol to three significant figures. (c) n(F−) = 2n(CaF2) = 0.0384123 mol, or 0.0384 mol. (d) Total ionic amount = 3n(CaF2) = 0.0576184 mol, or 0.0576 mol. The ions are mobile in the liquid and can transport charge, while ions in the solid occupy fixed lattice positions. The formula ratio is exact; measured mass and molar mass justify three significant figures. No change of mass unit is needed because grams match the supplied molar-mass unit.



### Marking guidance

Equation and working: (a) neutral total charge gives 1:2, one mark; (b) m/M, one mark; (c) twice formula amount, one mark; (d) three times formula amount, one mark, plus solid/liquid carrier-mobility explanation, one mark. Answer and unit: amounts in mol; number ratio dimensionless. Significant figures: three for calculated amounts, using the unrounded intermediate. This original calculation also makes the strong fixed lattice versus mobile molten ions explicit.
