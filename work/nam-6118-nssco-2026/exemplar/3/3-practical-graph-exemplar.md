## Gradients and evidence-based conclusions

### Reading what a gradient means

A gradient compares a change on the vertical axis with a change on the horizontal axis. Subtract the two vertical coordinates, subtract the two horizontal coordinates in the same order, then divide. The sign matters. A descending line has a negative gradient when the horizontal variable increases to the right. The name of a physical quantity must follow the axes: a gradient is not automatically density, stiffness or speed.

### Using the fitted line

Plot the readings, choose suitable scales and draw a line that represents their trend. For a straight line, mark a large triangle on the fitted line. Choose separated locations so that small reading errors have less influence. Use the line itself, not a pair of scattered experimental points that happen to be convenient. Label the coordinates used so that another reader can follow the calculation. The unit is obtained by dividing the vertical-axis unit by the horizontal-axis unit.

### An original practice data set

These values are invented for graph practice. A model trolley has positions of 2, 6, 10, 14, 18 and 22 cm at times of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 s respectively. Plot position on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. Between the fitted points at zero and five seconds, the position change is 20 cm and the time change is 5 s. The quotient 20 ÷ 5 = 4 gives a gradient of 4 cm/s. In this context the slope describes the rate of position change.

### A conclusion supported by values

The position increases by 4 cm during each successive second. This supports a constant rate of position change over the interval shown. It does not show that position is directly proportional to elapsed time measured from this zero, because the position begins at 2 cm. A straight trend and a proportional relationship are different statements. A useful conclusion states which is supported and points to the intercept or the repeated equal changes.

### Connections

Use topic 1 to check units and subtraction. Apply the same graphical method to extension versus load, volume versus mass and the motion graphs in topic 5. The calculation process transfers, but the physical meaning of its result changes with the plotted quantities.

### A full investigation plan

For a spring investigation, secure the spring vertically and record its unloaded length against a nearby ruler. Add a calibrated force and measure the new length after the spring settles. Subtract the unloaded length to obtain extension, then repeat at several safe forces. Use the same spring, the same reference points and the same reading method. Keep falling loads away from feet and avoid overloading the spring. Record force in newtons, unloaded and loaded length in millimetres, and calculated extension in millimetres. Plot extension vertically against force horizontally. Describe the trend from the plotted data; if a fitted straight line is suitable, its gradient has units of millimetres per newton. Do not call that gradient a spring constant: the spring constant uses force per extension.
