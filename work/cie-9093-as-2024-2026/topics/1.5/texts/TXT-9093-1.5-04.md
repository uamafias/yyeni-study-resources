THE PASS AT KLEIN BRANDBERG

The pass was marked on the map as maintained. I mention this because for the first forty minutes of the descent it was true, and because by then it was too late for the word to matter.

It was past nine and the fog had come down the way it does in those mountains, not gradually but all at once, like a curtain someone drops to end an act. My headlights made a room about four metres wide, and the rest of the world was a rumour. The radio played, which I record here as a fact about my own stupidity: I had chosen, in a maintained pass, in fog, to have a voice in the car.

The first warning was the bend that arrived before the map said it would. The second warning was the absence of the third marker stone, which I did not notice, because I was not counting. I have replayed this many times, in daylight, at leisure, and the honest inventory is this: one wrong bend, one missing stone, fog, an ordinary speed, a radio, and a drop on my left that the guard rail had been holding back for fifteen years.

The rail did not fail. The ground under it did.

There is a sound a car makes when one wheel leaves the world, and I cannot describe it, and I will not try. What I remember with the useful clarity of terror is my own hands: they did something correct, something no part of me had decided, and the car stopped across the road with its nose against the rock and its tail over the drop, and the radio, impossibly, kept playing.

I sat with the engine off for a while. The fog came in through the window and my hands, on the wheel, were shaking at a speed I have never seen hands shake, and the silence when I turned the radio off was total, and the darkness on my left was not darkness but absence, which is a different thing, and I understood that I was being given a second chance to be a person who counts marker stones.

I got out on the passenger side, which is the side that mattered, and stood in the fog with my back to the rock, and did the arithmetic of the tail: the two rear wheels were on the edge of the drop and holding, and I understood, standing there, that I would remember the edge for the rest of my life and that the car would not.
The second car came up the pass twenty minutes later, going too fast, as I had been going, its headlights making the fog into walls. I stood in the road with my phone light on and waved it down, and the driver, a young man with his whole family in the car, leaned out and asked me the question you ask in the mountains: is the road all right?

I said what I had learned in the fog to say, which is the only thing that could be said: "Not this one. Take the long way. I’ll wait here and tell the others."

I have driven the pass twice since, both times in daylight, both times counting, and I want to record something about the counting: it is not fear. It is the ordinary respect you pay to a road that let you keep your family, your radio and your uncounted stones, and gave you the rest of your life to notice what you were driving through.
