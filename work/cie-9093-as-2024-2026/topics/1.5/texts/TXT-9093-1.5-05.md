THIRTY DAYS WITHOUT THE PHONE

I want to start with the most embarrassing thing, so the rest of the month sounds brave: I lasted four hours. On day one, I reached for a phone I had given to a friend, and when my hand found nothing, I felt — and I am choosing this word carefully — grief.

The rules were simple. Thirty days, no smartphone. A cheap phone for calls and texts, a paper map in the car, a watch, and one hour on a friend’s laptop every Sunday for actual admin. The phone itself left with a friend of a friend, three hundred kilometres away, which turned out to be the only enforcement method that worked, because willpower, I can now report, is a rumour.

Here is what the first week was like, because everyone asks. It was like giving up a food you don’t remember tasting. I kept touching my pocket, the way people describe after an amputation, and I kept having the thought — not the decision, the thought — "I should look that up", about forty times a day, and then having to sit in the question like an adult. I checked the paper map at a red light, in front of other drivers, and felt the shame of a man reading a newspaper on a bus in a movie.

The second week changed the questions. This is the part nobody told me. When you cannot look things up, you have to know them, or not know them, and "not knowing" turns out to be a room in the mind with furniture in it: you notice you are in it, you get bored in it, and boredom, I am now convinced, is where thinking comes from. I finished three books. I invented two solutions to a work problem that had been sitting on my phone’s to-do list since March. I called my mother twice, and — this is the statistic of the whole month — we talked, on the second call, for fifty minutes, which is longer than the previous year of Sundays combined.

The third and fourth weeks were, I am sorry to report, not dramatic at all, and that is the finding: the drama is all at the start. By week three the phone was a weather, and the weather had changed. I ate slower. I looked at the middle distance in queues. I noticed that the person who most needed to reach me had my number, and that everyone else could wait, and that they did, and that nothing happened.

Let me say what I did not find. I did not find enlightenment, a better job, or the ability to be happy in silence — silence is still boring, it is just usefully boring. I did not give up my smartphone for ever, because I need it for work like everyone, and the month did not make me better than anyone who uses one, which is a sentence I write on day twenty-eight, sitting by a river.

What I found was a number: forty. Forty times a day, my hand had been reaching for a machine to answer a question I had not finished thinking. That number, more than any calm or any book, is what I have kept. The phone is back now, and it is a tool, and tools go in a drawer when I am not using them, and I count, some days — I am at eleven. That is not enlightenment. That is arithmetic, and it will do.
