WHY TEENAGERS ARE TIRED ALL DAY — AND WHY IT IS NOT THEIR FAULT

Your teenager is not lazy. Their body clock is simply living in a different time zone, and no alarm clock has ever been built that can argue with biology.

The science is younger than you think. Until the 1990s, the received wisdom was that teenagers sleep late because they stay up late, and that they stay up late because screens, phones and a tragic devotion to bad television. Then adolescent-medicine researchers began measuring melatonin — the hormone that tells the body it is night — and found something the received wisdom had missed: in adolescents, melatonin is released up to two hours later than in children or adults. The teenager’s body genuinely does not feel sleepy until eleven at night, and it is not sulking about it.

The consequences are written across every classroom in the world. A school starting at 07:30 takes a body whose night began at eleven and asks it to be fully awake at six and a half hours of sleep — when the same body needs nine. The result is not mild: sleep researchers describe chronic school-day sleep deprivation as one of the largest natural experiments ever run on children, and the findings include impaired memory consolidation, weakened emotional regulation, and — the detail every parent will recognise — an inability to feel alert until mid-afternoon.

So what is a school to do? The evidence from districts that have moved their start times later is consistent: attendance rises, grades rise by measurable margins, and reported car accidents among student drivers fall. One widely studied American district saw a 70 per cent drop in teen crash rates in the two years after it pushed its high-school start past eight o’clock. The bus timetables complained, and the sports teams complained, and then everyone adjusted, which is what communities do.

And yet the 07:30 start survives, in most of the world, like a monument to an argument nobody remembers winning. Why? The honest answer is logistics. School start times are arranged backwards from adult working hours, and the adults are the ones who write to the newspaper.

What the later-start districts also found, and what the objectors rarely mention, is that the change is free. No teacher is hired, no building is built, no curriculum is rewritten; the same school simply begins its day when its students’ bodies arrive. The costs are the buses and the bells, and both adjust, as the districts’ transport managers discovered with visible surprise.
There is a smaller, stranger reason too, and it is worth saying out loud: we treat tiredness in teenagers as a moral failing. A sleepy child is pitied; a sleepy sixteen-year-old is lectured. "You would not be tired if you went to bed earlier" is a sentence that feels true and is, biologically, a demand that a body clock run backwards.

The researchers are careful about what they recommend — later starts, yes, but also honest bedtimes, dark rooms, and no lectures about willpower. The one thing they do not recommend is the argument at six in the morning, which every parent in the world has already lost.

Your teenager is not lazy. They are, quite literally, living two time zones away — and the ferry between the zones does not run on the school’s timetable.
