DOWN THE OKAVANGO, SLOWLY

There is a way to travel the Okavango that involves engines, and there is a way that involves poling, and I have now done both, and I am here to tell you that one of them makes you a tourist and the other makes you unemployed: unemployed in the best sense, with no power over anything, moving at the speed of the river’s choosing.

The mokoro is a dugout canoe, poled from the stern, and the poler — ours was named Boro, which is also the name of a region and a word for the river people, and he enjoyed this coincidence more each day — stands for hours, pushing off the bottom with a long pole. You sit at water level, in a canvas chair, with your bag, your hat and your nerve, and the reeds go past at the speed of a slow walk.

The first hour is about fear. I am being honest, because the brochures are not: there are hippos in the Okavango, and hippos are the most dangerous large animal in Africa, and your first hour in a mokoro is spent conducting a diplomatic negotiation with your own imagination. Boro poled and said nothing, and after a while I understood that his silence was the information: he was listening, and what he was listening for was not there.

The second hour is about the water, which is a different thing. At eye level, at walking speed, the river stops being scenery and becomes a place you are in. There are frogs with a call like a finger on a wet glass. There are reed frogs, which are the size of your thumbnail and sit on the reeds like punctuation. There is a fish that jumps in a way that suggests it has somewhere to be. None of these are visible from a boat with an engine, because engines close the doors that walking speed opens.

The third hour, and this is the part I would travel again for, is about the absence of hours. The river bends. The poler does not announce the bends; he simply turns, and the world turns with him, and at some point — I cannot tell you when, because the third hour has no minutes in it — you stop being a person who is travelling and become a person who is being carried, which is the oldest and least purchasable feeling in the world.

We camped on an island, and Boro made tea over a fire of dead wood, and he said the thing that I have decided to take home: "The river is not slow," he said. "The river is exactly as fast as itself. It is us who are fast." I have used this sentence on three people since, all of whom looked at me with pity, and it does not survive retelling, and I am putting it here anyway, because some sentences belong to the place they were said.

Would I recommend the mokoro? Reader, I have already priced the next one. Take the three-day trip and not the day trip: the third hour exists, and the day trip does not reach it. Go in the dry season, when the channels narrow and the animals gather. And when the poler says nothing, be quiet too. He is listening to the river, and the river is exactly as fast as itself.
