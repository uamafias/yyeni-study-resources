MY GRANDMOTHER’S KITCHEN

My grandmother measured nothing, and by that I mean that she measured everything, with instruments the shops do not sell.

The kitchen was at the back of the house in Ovamboland, a room of smoke and authority, and I entered it the way you enter a country: with a passport, and no right to be there. The passport was willingness. A child who wanted to help was given work; a child who wanted to watch was given the door.

She baked on Fridays, and the recipe — I use the word in the loosest sense in the history of cooking — was conducted with her hands as the only instruments. Flour was poured in a spiral, the shape of which, I learned much later, was not superstition but speed: a spiral spreads the flour into the warm air of the kitchen and brings it to the temperature the yeast wants. Salt was measured in her palm, "because a spoon", she said, "is for people who don’t know their hands". She had opinions about measuring spoons that I have since heard repeated by professional bakers, who have also, all of them, been laughed at by their grandmothers.

The dough was kneaded until it "stopped being loud", by which she meant the sound of the kneading changed, and I want to record this, because it is the only piece of technical knowledge in this piece, and it is true: wet dough on wood makes a slap; ready dough makes a thud, and the thud is when you stop.

And then — this is where the learning happened — she would look at the window.

The window faced east, over the mahangu field, and on baking mornings she would pause, mid-thud, and look at it, and after a silence she would open the oven, or she would not. I asked her once what she was looking for. "The light," she said. "The bread is ready when the light says so." I thought, at nine, that this was religion. At thirty-five, standing in my own kitchen, I caught myself looking east out of my own window before I preheated, and understood at last what she had been doing: the east window told her whether the morning sun was in the right place for the oven’s temperament. The light was a clock, a thermometer, and thirty years of Fridays.

She is ninety-two now, and the kitchen has a modern oven with numbers on it, and she still looks at the window. My daughter, who is nine, asked her last month what she was looking at. My grandmother said, "the light", and my daughter has started doing it too, and neither of them knows I have written this down, and neither of them, if they read it, will think it was about the light.

It was never about the light. It was about the window, and the person at the oven, and the fact that some knowledge will not go into words, and so goes into Fridays, and into the hands, and east into the window, and down to me and now to a nine-year-old who does not know why she looks.

The bread, for the record, is still perfect. I have checked the temperature, the time, and the flour. The bread is right when the thud happens, and the light says so, and that is all I know, and it is everything my grandmother taught me, which was nothing, which was the point.
