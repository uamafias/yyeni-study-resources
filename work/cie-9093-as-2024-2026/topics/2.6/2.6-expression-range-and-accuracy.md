# 2.6 Expression, range and accuracy

**What this topic asks of you**

- Use a range of vocabulary, sentence structures and literary features chosen for their effect, all controlled; write clearly and coherently with a range of language you can command.
- Write accurately: sentence boundaries, agreement, tense, punctuation and spelling; and the proofreading passes that catch each kind of error.

---

## Range of language

### Precision and range

The precise word names the exact thing; the general word gestures at a family of things, and the difference is what the reader has to work with. A range of language means varied vocabulary, sentence structures and literary features — all controlled: range is measured in what the writing keeps accurate, not in what it attempts. The syllabus asks for ideas expressed clearly, coherently and accurately with an appropriate range — which is the whole of this topic in one line, and the writing marks in both papers rest on it.

### Worked example: the precise word at work

Take "the animal moved quickly across the road". The general word gestures at a family of animals and a family of movements; the precise word names the thing — "the jackal trotted across the road", and now the reader sees one animal doing one thing at one speed, and the sentence has stopped being a description and become a sighting. Precision is a service to the reader: every exact noun is one the reader does not have to build.

### Control before complexity

Control comes before complexity because the range is judged inside accuracy: a sentence the writer can hold is worth ten the writer cannot, and the table rewards expression the writer keeps command of. A varied range of sentences means different shapes held deliberately — simple, compound, complex, minor, fronted — each chosen for its effect; long complex sentences in which the grammar breaks down are range attempted and lost, and the reader is lost in the same place.

### Worked example: two sentences, one choice

Watch a writer choose between range and control at the level of one sentence. Ambition: "The thunder, which had been circling the valley all afternoon like a debt collector, finally arrived with the rain." Control: "The thunder had circled the valley all afternoon. When it arrived, it came with the rain." The first is fine — held, punctuated, the simile earning its clause — and a writer who can hold it should. The second is the same content in two shapes the writer can hold at speed, under time, tired. The range is real in both; the difference is the price of each on a writing day, and knowing your own handwriting’s speed is part of knowing your range.

### Ambition without command

Ambitious vocabulary used in the wrong sense, or informality that slides into error, costs the accuracy the range was meant to serve: an unusual word must be a word the writer can use, not just find. "The sunset was ephemeral at six and very nice by seven" has spent its ambition on the wrong half of the sentence; the reader who knows the word winces, and the reader who does not is misled. The rule is simple and unsparing: use the range you own, own the range you use, and add words to the owned set by checking each new one before it carries a sentence.

### Clarity and coherence

Writing clearly and coherently means the reader follows the sentences without re-reading them: clarity is the sentence’s work being done, and coherence the paragraph’s. Clarity is won at the level of the sentence — the subject where the reader expects it, the verb where the sentence needs it — and coherence at the level of the paragraph, where each sentence hands the next its subject. Neither is a style: they are the minimum the reader is owed, and the range rides on top of them, never instead of them.

## Accuracy at sentence and word level

### The comma splice

A comma splice is two complete sentences joined by a comma alone — "the gates opened at six, the crowd was already waiting" — and it is the commonest sentence-boundary error. It is fixed three ways: a full stop makes two sentences, a semicolon joins two that belong together, or a conjunction with its comma does the joining the comma cannot. The splice is worth catching first in any proofread, because everything else is easier to see once the boundaries are honest.

### Fragments: error and choice

An accidental sentence fragment is an error; a deliberate minor sentence is a choice — and the difference is whether the grammar broke or the writer decided. "Not this time." is a choice: a minor sentence held for effect, and the writing around it shows the decision. "Because the gates opened." is an error: a subordinate clause with nothing to belong to, and the reader is left holding it. The proofread asks each fragment the same question — did I decide this? — and the answer decides whether it stays.

### Direct speech, punctuated and paragraphed

Direct speech is punctuated by its own rules: the marks around the words, the reporting clause and its comma, and a new paragraph for each new speaker. "There it is," she said, and pointed — the comma inside the marks, the reporting clause after, the action attached. When speech runs into the narrative without its punctuation or new paragraphs, the reader loses the map of the conversation: who said what becomes a crowd, and the paragraphing is the difference between a dialogue and a brawl.

### Drift: tense and capitals

Two errors of consistency are caught in the same proofread. Tense that drifts between past and present loses the reader’s sense of when the piece stands — "She opened the door and the hall is full of people" — and the fix is to choose one tense and check every verb against it. Missing capitals for sentence openings and proper nouns are the small, visible errors a final pass exists for: cheap to fix, costly in the impression they leave, and never worth the marks they lose.

### The proofreading passes

Proofreading is done as one read for each kind of error, because each pass has one thing to look for and finds it. First pass: sentence boundaries — splices and fragments. Second pass: agreement and tense — the verb against its subject, the tense against its neighbours. Third pass: capitals and proper nouns, speech punctuation, and the small rest. Three reads sound slow and are faster than one vague read, because the finding is what takes the time, and one thing at a time is findable.

### Worked example: three passes on one paragraph

A draft paragraph: "The market opens at six, by seven the stalls are full. she was selling oranges. the price was good and the oranges was sweet." First pass: "six, by seven" is a splice — full stop or semicolon; "she was selling oranges." is fine. Second pass: "the oranges was sweet" — agreement, "were". Third pass: "she" and "the price" need their capitals, and the tense — opened, was — wants one past throughout: "The market opened at six; by seven the stalls were full. She was selling oranges. The price was good and the oranges were sweet." Three passes, four fixes, one honest paragraph.
