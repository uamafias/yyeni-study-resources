EVERY INVENTOR HAS A STORY ABOUT THE MOMENT, AND TULI SHIKUary’s begins with a queue.

She was fourteen, in the settlement of Omutiya, standing in the line at the village borehole on a morning in the dry season of 2023, and the line was long because the pump was slow, and the pump was slow because the water table had fallen and the borehole’s old diesel engine worked like a tired man climbing stairs. "We were there two hours," she says now, "and I did my homework standing up, and I thought: the engine drinks more than we do."

She is seventeen, and this month she is in Windhoek, where the Polytechnic’s engineering department has just finished testing the device that grew out of that queue.

It is worth being clear about what the device is, because the word "invention" makes people imagine a machine with lights. Shikuary’s borehole monitor is a box the size of a lunch tin, bolted to the pump head, that measures the water table and the engine’s workload and does two things with the information: it tells the water committee, by SMS, when the table is falling — "so they can ration before the pump is drinking air" — and it throttles the engine to the depth, because "the engine does not need to climb stairs at the same speed when the stairs are lower."

The engineering department tested it against a control borehole for four months. The monitored borehole used 23 per cent less fuel and its pump needed no maintenance in the period; the control borehole’s pump failed twice. The department’s report, which the ministry has, calls the results "promising", which is the driest word a report can use and still be a compliment.

Tuli — everyone calls her Tuli, including the professor who tested her work — built the first version out of parts she describes with the affection of introductions: a pressure sensor from a washing machine, a microcontroller she bought with the money from selling her late father’s radio (her mother knows, and has forgiven her), and a phone older than the project. She taught herself the programming from a manual she read in the regional library, two bus rides away, because the settlement’s school had no computer access at the time.

"I want to be honest about the hard part," she says, when I ask what to tell other learners who read this. "The hard part was not the electronics. Electronics you can learn from a book, and the book is patient. The hard part was asking adults to test a thing a girl made. The first water committee said no. The second said no. The third said yes because their engine had just failed, and I have learned that hope is a renewable resource, but you have to be there when something breaks."

She has a patent application filed, with help from a Windhoek law firm that took the case without a fee, and she has orders — actual orders, with deposits — from eleven water committees across two regions. The price is N$2,400 per unit, which she arrived at by costing the parts, the bus fare to install them, and "one rand of profit, because profit is not a dirty word in Omutiya."

Her mother, Anna Shikuary, sells fat cakes at the crossroads and has opinions about all of this. "The radio I forgave in one week," she says. "The buses to Windhoek I forgive every week."

Tuli matriculates this year, and the engineering department has made it known she would be welcome. She plans to study, and to keep the business, "because the business is the part that teaches me what the engineering is for." Asked what she wants learners to take from her story, she thinks in the way that interviewers learn to wait for.

"The queue was the education," she says. "Everything I built, I built to shorten it."
