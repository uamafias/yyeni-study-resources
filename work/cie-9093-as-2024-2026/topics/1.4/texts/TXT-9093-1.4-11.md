MY STALL IS THE SECOND FROM THE GATE, WHICH IS THE BEST POSITION IN the market and I will tell you why, because everyone thinks it is the first and they are wrong.

The first stall gets the people who have just arrived, who are still holding their money and their list, who have not yet remembered that this is a market and markets are for looking. The second stall gets them after they have looked at one stall, which is the correct amount of looking, and their shoulders have come down, and their money is now in the hand, and they are ready for me.

I sell second-hand clothes, sorted and mended, and I have sold them for nine years, since the year the factory closed and I discovered that the market is a machine that runs on Saturdays.

A market day has a shape, and I learned the shape the way you learn a river: by falling in. The first hour is the housewives with lists, who buy what they came for and do not chat, and you love them like rain. The second hour is the browsers, who touch everything, and you love them like weather in November — they bring the crowd, they buy nothing, and the crowd behind them buys.

By ten, the whole machine is hot. My sister’s children, who I borrow on Saturdays because they are fast and shameless, run the mending basket and make change, and I do the deals, which are conversations with arithmetic in them. The aunt who wants the blue dress for half: the answer is no, but you say it in a way that keeps her there, because the crowd watches a stall where a crowd is already standing. The man buying for his daughter and pretending it is for his wife: you keep his secret and throw in the belt, and he is yours for the next nine years, which is how long my customers stay.

There is a woman who comes every second Saturday with a list she has already spent, and we have an arrangement: she tells me the prices she was quoted elsewhere and I tell her they are terrible, and then she buys two things and feels she has won. I charge her what the clothes cost. She is my favourite customer, and this is not sentiment, it is economics: a customer who feels she has won comes back, and the machine runs on return.

By noon the shade moves, and my stall has the second-best shade in the market, because I pay for the shade and not for the position, which is the thing nobody tells you about markets. The sun comes round to the first stall, which is why the man at the first stall leaves at one, and I stay until three, and the afternoon crowd is mine: the men, mostly, who come for shirts and stay to argue about the football, and who buy shirts.

My mother sold from a blanket on this ground before the shelters were built, and she says the market has not changed, only the roof. The same three conversations, she says: the price, the quality, the family. She is right, and I will not tell her, because the price of telling my mother she is right is that she comes and sits at the stall, and my stall is the second from the gate, and there is a limit to what even the best position can sell.

At three I pack. The children count the tin, and I count it again, which is a kindness they know about and forgive, and the takings go into the pocket in the dress, the grey one, second from the left, because a market is a machine that runs on Saturdays and a woman who sells in it does not keep her money where the crowd can see.

Tomorrow is Sunday. On Monday I mend, and on Tuesday I buy, and on Saturday the gate opens at six and I am at the second stall, and the river comes through it, and I am in it, which is where a person who knows the river stands.
