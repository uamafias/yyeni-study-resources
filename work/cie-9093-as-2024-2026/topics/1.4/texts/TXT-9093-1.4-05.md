I HAVE HIKED WITH PEOPLE MY WHOLE LIFE. MY FATHER HIKED US AS children, my friends and I hiked the Sperrgebiet in our twenties, and when I tell you I had never done a long trail alone, I am not confessing a lack — I am explaining the size of the silence I walked into on day one of the Naukluft trail.

The plan was the ordinary one: eight days, the route the map calls the Mountain Trail, water at the marked tanks, my pack at eleven kilograms before the water. I told three people the itinerary. I checked the tanks were reported full. I did the checklist the way you do anything you have done competently a hundred times: quickly, and mostly from memory.

Here is what the silence is, so you know before you go. It is not the absence of people, because for two days I heard exactly what I always hear — wind, birds, my own breathing, which turns out to be a companion with opinions. The silence that arrives on day three is the absence of anyone to turn to when you see the thing you are seeing. I stood on the ridge above the Tsondab at sunset on day three, and the light did the thing it does in that country — went gold, then violet, then a blue I do not have a word for — and I opened my mouth to say it to somebody, and there was nobody, and the moment closed like a door. That is the deal you make walking alone. You see more, and you keep all of it.

What went wrong went wrong on day five, and it is the reason I am writing this instead of posting the photographs I meant to post. The tank at the fifth waypoint, marked full on the report I checked, was dry. Not low — dry, with cracked mud at the bottom and the smell of a thing that has been empty longer than a season. I had come down off the ridge with a litre and a half, which is a day’s walking in that heat and no more.

I am not a dramatic person, so I will tell you what I did in the order I did it. I checked the map. The next waypoint was eleven kilometres, and the one behind was fourteen. I sat for ten minutes, which is the most valuable thing I did all day, and then I turned around, because fourteen kilometres on a litre and a half, downhill and in the afternoon, is a calculation; eleven across a saddle in the midday sun was a gamble, and I had stopped gambling by then.

The walk back is the part I would not have written in the group-version of this blog, because it was not brave. It was slow, and hot, and I rationed water in sips I could hear myself counting, and I talked to myself the whole way, not for company but because the sound of a voice, even mine, keeps a mind on its task. I reached the fourth waypoint at dusk with a quarter of a litre and a new respect for the word "reported", as in "tanks reported full".

The lessons, in the order you should take them. Reports are reports; carry what you would need if the report is wrong — for me, that is now two litres beyond the calculation, always, even in the weight. Tell people where you are going, and give them a time to worry, not a date. And the silence is worth it. I have come back to the trail and I will go again, alone, with the two litres, because the moment on the ridge, the one the door closed on, is also the moment I would walk eight days for, and there is no version of it with company.

My father, when I told him all this over the phone, laughed for a long time and then said the thing he says: "Now you are a person who knows something." He is eighty. He has never hiked alone in his life. I have decided not to tell him that.
