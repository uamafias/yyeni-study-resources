THE SOLARIS 40 ARRIVED WITH THE KIND OF PACKAGING THAT SUGGESTS you have bought a small spaceship: moulded cardboard, a sleeve, a leaflet in four languages, and the panel itself, which folds to the size of a paperback and unfolds, with a satisfying series of clicks, into four linked leaves of matte black.

I tested it for thirty-one days, the way I test everything for this column: by living with it, not by admiring it. It went into my daypack, where I keep the things I promise to use — the good pen, the first-aid kit, the reusable cup — and, unlike most of that company, it earned its weight.

The claim on the box is "full phone charge in four hours of good sun", and I will do the reviewer’s arithmetic for you, because the box will not. In thirty-one days of Namibian autumn sun, mostly cloudless, my phone (a mid-range model, three years old, not a fast-charging one) went from twenty per cent to full in an average of four hours and forty minutes, with the panel angled at the sun and moved twice a day. Flat on a table, unattended, the same charge took six and a half hours. The box’s four hours is the best case in a month of good cases, and you should read it the way you read a car’s fuel figure.

The good — and it is genuinely good — is the portability and the reliability. At 640 grams it is the lightest panel I have tested at this output, and it survived a month in a schoolbag, a fall onto gravel at a campsite, and a rain shower in week three that the leaflet said would be survived and I did not believe. The two USB ports charge two devices, and while they share the total, my phone and a small power bank charged together in an afternoon.

The bad is the same bad that afflicts the whole category, and the Solaris 40 does not escape it: it is furniture, not a magic box. If you do not hang it, angle it, and move it, you get the six-and-a-half-hour version of this review, and I watched a fellow camper give up on a competitor’s panel entirely because he left it flat on the sand and expected enthusiasm. Solar charging is a relationship, and this panel is only its most agreeable partner yet.

The price, N$1,299 at the time of writing, is the point at which the arithmetic gets interesting, and I will give you my honest sums. A comparable panel a year ago cost N$1,900, and the Solaris 40 outperformed the one I tested then. Against mains charging it pays for itself, in a household’s sense, only if you are away from mains regularly; if you are a twice-a-year camper, buy a power bank and a sandwich. If you are off the grid one weekend in three, or if load-shedding visits you the way it visits most of us, the panel is the better tool and the price is fair.

The leaflet’s four languages, by the way, do not include the region’s most spoken, which is a choice the company should examine before the next print run.

Verdict: four stars out of five, and the star it loses is the box’s four-hour claim, not the panel. The Solaris 40 is honest hardware in a box that flatters it, and the honest version — a phone charged by lunchtime if you give it a morning — is worth the money.
