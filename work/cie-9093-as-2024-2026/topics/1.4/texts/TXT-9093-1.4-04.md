THE RADIO HAD SAID THE RIVER WAS PASSABLE, AND THE RADIO HAD SAID it with the voice of a man reading a list, so we believed it the way you believe weather at a distance: completely, and without checking.

We were eleven on the north bank by six that morning, waiting for the pontoon that the district ran in the dry months and, in the wet ones, ran when it felt like it. The pontoon was on the south side. That was the first fact of the day, and it was Meme Kanyemba who noticed it, because Meme Kanyemba noticed everything and said half of it.

"They are not coming," she said, at half past six. "The cable is singing."

The cable was the steel rope that pulled the pontoon across, and in high water it hummed — the sound of a note that is being stretched. I had heard it before and did not like it, and it is the kind of sound you get used to the way you get used to a bad tooth, by deciding not to listen.

By eight there were twenty-two of us, and a bakkie with a family’s whole household on it, and a man named Shipanga who had crossed "a hundred times, in worse than this." He said that from the top of the bank, dry, with his hands in his pockets. The rest of us were lower down, closer to the water, because that is where the pontoon landed when it came, and there is a way of standing near a thing you are waiting for that is half hope and half hypnosis.

The water rose through the morning the way it does in that country — not a wave, but a slow, muscular shrug, the river adding a centimetre at a time to a decision it had already made. By ten it was touching the loading ramp, which it had no business doing, and by half past ten it had begun to take the ramp’s sand away from under it, one shelf at a time.

I want to be fair to Shipanga, because everyone at the inquiry was not. He went onto the pontoon at eleven, to help his brother start it — the brothers who ran it had gone to the funeral at Okaloko, which is the fact this whole day turns on — and he did start it, and the cable did hold, and the pontoon did come, walking across the water like an old man on a bad floor.

The pontoon reached us at noon with thirty centimetres of water on its deck, and at thirty centimetres you are not carrying people, you are carrying people on a raft.

Shipanga’s brother took the bakkie first, because the family had a baby, and the crossing took four minutes, and those four minutes were the quietest I have ever known twenty-two people to be. The cable sang one long note the whole way, and the baby slept, and the bakkie’s exhaust, half under water, made a noise like a kettle.

They came back empty for us, and the cable did not hold.

It did not break, either. What it did, the inquiry called "partial de-tensioning", which means it went slack for three seconds — a sound like a held breath let go — and in those three seconds the pontoon swung downstream with nineteen people on it, and the deck went under, and the water that had been thirty centimetres was suddenly at the knees of everyone standing on it.

I cannot tell the rest in order, because order is not what the water kept. A man gave me his hand and I did not take it, because I was holding a child who was not mine and had become mine. The deck came back up, and the people on it came back with it, all but two, and one of them was Shipanga’s brother.

The pontoon was repaired by August. The cable was replaced with a heavier one. The funeral was held where the brothers had gone, which is a sentence I cannot end properly, and will not try to here.

Shipanga crosses the river in the dry season now, by the pontoon, and I have seen him do it. He crosses standing up, with his hands out of his pockets, watching the cable the whole way across, like a man reading a list he wrote himself and no longer believes.
