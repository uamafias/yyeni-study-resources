THE BUS FROM KEETMANSHOOP DROPS YOU AT THE FUEL STATION AT AUS at eleven in the morning, and it does not drop you anywhere else, because Aus is one street long and the fuel station is the middle of it. This is the first thing to understand about the town, and I understood it, in a manner of speaking, by noon.

The second thing to understand is the wind. Everybody told me about the wild horses of the Namib, and nobody told me about the wind, which arrives from the south-west at a speed that feels personal and rearranges your plans around it. My plan had been to walk to the viewpoint. The wind’s plan was for me to lean into it at an angle and be grateful for the fence.

I had one day in Aus, because that is what the itinerary allowed, and I want to say first that one day is enough — not for the area, which would take a week — but for the town, which gives you what it has generously and at the pace of its own weather.

I ate at the little shop by the fuel station, which does not look like a restaurant from the road and is one anyway, and I ate the best mopane worms of my life, which I mention because it is a sentence I did not expect to write and I believe in giving a place its sentences. The woman who runs it asked me where I was from, and when I said Windhoek she nodded as if that explained the mopane thing, and we left it there, and it was a better conversation than many I have had in cities.

The wild horses are the reason most people stop, and I drove out to the waterhole at Garub in the afternoon, which is the right time, and I saw three of them: lean, wind-combed, standing at the water with the indifference of animals who have been photographed more than most celebrities. A visitor next to me whispered that there used to be forty. There are, I have since read, somewhere between eighty and a hundred, and they are fine, and the whispering is just what we do at waterholes.

What I did not expect, and what I would go back for, is the graveyard on the hill behind the town — the old German one, from the colonial era, with its iron crosses and its dates. I went up at four o’clock when the light was long, and there was nobody there, and the wind had dropped, and the crosses made their small shadows on the gravel. Whatever your politics, and mine are not the point, a graveyard from 1905 on a hill above a desert is a place that will make you stand still, and I did.

Practical things, because I promised. There are two places to stay in Aus and I stayed at the one on the east side, which was clean, cheap and run by a family whose dogs escort you to your room as a matter of protocol. There is fuel, there is a shop with slightly more than it looks like from the road, and there is no bank machine, which the town assumes you know. Bring cash, bring a windproof layer, and bring more time than I had, because the one-day version of Aus is a taste, and the taste is the reason people come back.

I left on the evening bus, and the driver — the same one who had dropped me — asked how the day had gone. I said the wind had taken my viewpoint. He said the viewpoint had been there for a million years and would wait, which I have decided is the official motto of Aus, and I left it at that.
