THE TOWN OF ARANOS HAS CUT ITS WATER USE BY 38 PER CENT IN TWELVE months, and it has done so without a single fine, by reorganising itself around a simple idea: the town watches its own water, in public, every week.

The scheme, called the Water Ledger, was proposed by the municipality’s engineer, Loide Kambonde, after the second failed rainy season. It has three parts, and none of them is a new pipe.

The first part is measurement that people can see. Every school, hotel, and municipal building — and, since March, every household that volunteers — receives a weekly card with its own consumption on it, alongside the town’s total and the dam’s level. The cards are also posted at the fuel station, the clinic and the two shops. "People cannot save a number they never see," Kambonde says. "We had a tariff table nobody read. Now we have a noticeboard the whole town argues about."

The second part is the repair brigade — twelve residents, trained by the municipality and paid from the scheme’s budget, who fix leaks reported on a free line. In the first six months the brigade logged 437 repairs, and Kambonde’s figures attribute roughly a third of the total saving to them: "The town was losing water it had already paid to pump, through washers that cost fifty cents."

The third part is the tariff’s shape, which was the scheme’s hard politics. The first block of household water is cheaper than it was before the scheme; the second block is more than double the old rate. "We made the first block cheap so that nobody could say the poor were paying for the drought," says the mayor, Simeon Tjipuka, "and we made the second block expensive so that a green lawn in Aranos would cost what a green lawn in Aranos actually costs." Six complaints about the tariff reached the council in the first month; two reached it last month.

The results, at the one-year mark, are these. Town consumption is down 38 per cent against the year before, a saving the municipality puts at 61 million litres. The dam, at 41 per cent at the last reading, has fallen more slowly than the three-year average for the same rainfall. The brigade has a waiting list of residents wanting to join, and the schools’ consumption has fallen fastest of all — 44 per cent — which the head teacher of the secondary school attributes, with visible pride, to "a generation that now knows the weekly number for its own school, and treats it like a score".

The scheme is not free, and the town knows it. The budget for the year, N$420,000, pays for the brigade, the cards and a part-time administrator, and the council has agreed to fund it for a second year from the rates revenue the tariff changes have produced — the one part of the arithmetic Kambonde calls "the part that persuaded the council".

Aranos is now being visited by officials from two other regions, and Kambonde has given the same presentation eleven times, which she has stopped polishing: "The scheme is three ideas and a noticeboard. If a town cannot copy three ideas and a noticeboard, the water was never the problem."

Asked what happens if the rains fail a third year, Kambonde answers with the number she watches: "At the current rate of use, the dam reaches its critical level in nineteen months instead of eleven. That is what we bought. It is not safety. It is time, spent deliberately, and it is the most valuable thing a town in a drought can purchase."
