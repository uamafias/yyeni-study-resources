MY GRANDFATHER WORKED THE RAILWAYS FOR THIRTY-ONE YEARS, AND HE never once called the train by its destination. It was always "the 06:12", even after the timetable changed and it left at 06:20, because the name had stopped being a time long before and become a colleague.

He started in 1978 as a "lad porter", which meant he carried what the goods yard could not be bothered to carry, and he learned the railway the way you learn a person: by its noises first. The yard spoke in buffers and distant steam, and every wagon had a complaint. By the end of the second year he could tell a loaded wagon from an empty one in the dark, by the sound of the coupling.

"I was afraid of the engines at the start," he told me last month, at his kitchen table, with the tea he still makes too strong. "A big engine coming at you in the night, that is a lot of iron with a plan. You learn to be small and correct, and then you stop being afraid. Being correct is what the railway pays you for, and it is also how you survive."

He was made up to guard in 1984, and for twenty-six years he worked the 06:12 south and the 15:40 back, six days a week in the early years, and the journey that he can still recite is the one that made him. He married my grandmother because she took that train on Tuesdays to the market town, and the guard’s van smelled of the oranges she carried, and he has never eaten an orange since without some part of him waiting for the doors to close.

I asked him what the work actually was, and the answer was smaller and larger than I expected. "You are responsible for the train being right," he said. "The driver looks ahead. You look behind, and behind is where the people are. You check the doors, you check the brakes, you sign the book, and if anything is wrong on the line behind you, your name is the one in the book. That is not a small thing to be paid for."

He is proud of the book. Every guard kept one, and his thirty-one years of them are in a box in the wardrobe, in a script that gets looser as the decades go on. "A railway is a promise," he said. "The timetable is a promise you make to a town, and the town does not care what the weather did. Thirty-one years, I made that promise good."

He does not romanticise it, which surprised me until I understood it was the romance itself he distrusted. He saw two colleagues injured on the yard — one badly — and he kept the rule his first foreman gave him: never walk between a moving wagon and anything that is not moving. "The railway forgives nothing," he said, "and that is exactly why you can trust it. It never once surprised me in thirty-one years, because I never once gave it the chance."

He retired in 2009, on a Friday, and he rode the 06:12 one last time as a passenger, in a seat, "which was strange, because I kept standing up to check the doors." He has kept one photograph of himself on the platform at the start, twenty-one years old, in a uniform two sizes hopeful, and he says the boy in it is the only part of the railway he misses.

I asked him, finally, what he would tell that boy, and he thought about it while the tea went cold, and then he said: "I would tell him: it is a good life, if you are a person who likes being correct. And then I would tell him to learn the oranges are heavier than they look, because there is a woman on the Tuesday train, and you are going to need both hands."
