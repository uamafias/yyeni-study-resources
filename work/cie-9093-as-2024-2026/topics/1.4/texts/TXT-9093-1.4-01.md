KATIMA SOUTH SECONDARY SCHOOL DIDN’T SET OUT TO FEED ITSELF. IT SET out to mow less grass.

That is how principal Doreen Nakuta tells it, and three years on, the joke has become the school’s proudest fact. The three hectares behind the sports field, once a stretch of lawn that cost more to cut than to water, are now a working farm: maize in the wet months, cabbages and spinach year-round, a chicken project that started with sixty birds and now counts four hundred, and a irrigation pond that collects the roof runoff from the whole classroom block.

The numbers have earned the school a provincial award, but the change Nakuta points to first is quieter. "Our kitchen used to buy vegetables from town, twice a week, and the budget for them went up every year," she says. "This year the kitchen bought nothing. Nothing. I check the ledger myself."

The ledger matters because Katima South is not a wealthy school. Forty-two per cent of its learners receive fee support, and the kitchen’s feeding scheme — one cooked meal a day for every learner — was funded by a patchwork of donors that changed annually. The farm has steadied it. The meal is now cooked from the school’s own fields, and the donors’ money has shifted from buying cabbages to buying seeds, tools and the pump that lifted the project off waiting lists.

The learners run more of the farm than visitors expect. The agricultural science class plans the rotation; a learners’ co-operative manages the egg sales to the community and banked N$31,000 last year; and the dreaded "garden duty" of the early days — a punishment roster, in effect — has become a lunchtime queue. "You can tell a project is working when it stops being a punishment," says Tobias Iipinge, seventeen, who joined for the eggs and stayed for the agronomy. "I know the nitrogen cycle now because I have seen it."

The curriculum has followed the farm. The maths department uses the egg ledger for percentage work; the business studies class does its costing unit on the seed budget; and the writing class, somewhat to its teacher’s alarm, keeps a popular blog of pest observations that has been quoted twice by the regional extension office. "We did not build a farm," Nakuta says, standing in the shade of the pond’s pump house, "we built a reason."

Challenges remain, and Nakuta lists them without being asked. The borehole is ageing; the chicken project lost forty birds to heat one December week; and the teacher who ran the horticulture rotation has retired without a replacement in post. The award money — N$50,000 — is earmarked, but not yet spent, because "we promised the donors a plan before the money moves".

At the launch of the harvest week, the school’s neighbours were invited to walk the fields, and the mayor’s representative, arriving late in a white sedan, promised the school "the municipality’s full support", which Nakuta received with a nod that has greeted every such promise since the second year. The learners, meanwhile, were selling bunches of spinach at the gate for N$5 each, and by the end of the afternoon the bunches had gone.

What happens next, nobody in the story will predict, which is how the learners have learned to like it. "The farm teaches you the weather does not read your plan," says Iipinge, picking up the day’s egg crates. "And the weather is the one teacher you cannot appeal to." He walks back up the path between the maize rows, past the classroom block, where the afternoon’s last class is studying a ledger the farm produced, and the arithmetic is real.
