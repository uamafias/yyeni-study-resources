# 1.4 Directed response and comparison

*cie-9093-as-2024-2026. Offline study notes, rendered from the approved content units on 2026-09-29. Everything here is also what the flashcards are built from.*

**What this topic asks of you**

- Re-use a source text’s content in a different form and style in your own words; write in the manner of a given text.
- Decode the task’s role, form, audience and purpose; write 150–200 words whose vocabulary, style and structure fit them.
- Identify and compare the characteristic features of the source and of your own response; explain each difference by purpose, audience and context.
- Write a comparison that keeps both texts in view in every paragraph.

---

## Re-using a text for a new purpose

### The directed response

A directed response is a new text written from a source for a form, audience and purpose the task sets, re-using the source’s content in a new style. It is not a summary of the source, and it is not a copy of its wording: the task sets a new form for a new reader, and the content arrives in the clothing the task asks for. Paper 1’s Section A sets it at 150–200 words from an unseen text of about 550–750, and the task carries ten marks — five for the reading of the source, five for the writing.

### Reshaping the source

Reshaping a source runs in two steps. First select: from everything the source contains, keep the content the new purpose needs — the facts a diary writer would know, the viewpoint a reply must answer — and leave the rest. Then change: the register, the voice and the structure go over to the new form, and the content arrives in your own words. A report of a flood for the safety officer keeps dates and figures and sheds the memoir’s images; the same flood as a note to a neighbour keeps the warmth and sheds the sections.

### Re-use and lifting

Re-using content means keeping what the source says — its facts, its viewpoint, its people — while putting it into your own words. Lifting phrases or sentences from the source is copying, not re-use: a response that carries the source’s sentences into the new form has changed its clothing without changing its voice, which is the one thing reshaping must do. The test is the sentence: could the source’s writer have written it? If yes, it is theirs, not yours.

### Worked example: one source, two responses

A source tells of a school that turned unused land into an urban farm: yields, a teacher’s quote, the launch day, the mayor’s promise of funding. As a speech to the school assembly, the response selects the pupils’ achievement and the teacher’s pride, addresses the hall directly ("Good morning — you did this"), and saves the mayor’s promise for a close that asks for applause. As a report to the education office, the same content arrives in sections — background, outcomes, recommendation — with the yield figures first and the mayor’s promise as "funding pledged". Two forms, one source: selection and register did the whole work.

### In the exam

The directed response is the first task of Paper 1, and it sets the pattern for the whole paper: read the unseen text of about 550–750 words accurately, then write to a brief. Ten marks, five for the reading and five for the writing, and the two are inseparable — the reading marks are for drawing on the source’s content perceptively, which a lifted or summarised response does not do. Practise the two steps until they are one: select for the new purpose, then write every sentence for the new reader.

### Two ways to lose the source

The first error is lifting: phrases and sentences copied from the source into the response, which is copying rather than re-use and fails the writing half of the task at the level of the sentence. The second is summarising: the source retold in its own order, which produces a shorter source rather than a new text. Both have the same cure: select for the new purpose, then write every sentence afresh for the new reader. A third error shadows both — adding material the source does not support — and the rule is that every fact in a directed response traces back to the source.

## Writing the directed response

### Writing in a given style

Writing in the style of a text means taking on its register, its sentence patterns, its voice and its signature features, so a continuation reads as if the same writer wrote it. The style is found by naming it: its register (formal, conversational), its typical sentence (long and subordinated; short and clipped), its voice (ironic, confiding), and two signature features (the aside in dashes; the rule-of-three close). Held in mind together, they are the style; guessed at, they are a costume.

### Decoding the task

A directed-response task is decoded before the first sentence is written, and the decode runs in order. The role: who am I — a resident, the head learner, a visitor? The form: what kind of text — a speech, a letter, a blog? The audience: who reads it — the hall, the editor, friends? The purpose: what must it do — persuade, inform, celebrate? And what must come from the source: which facts, which viewpoint. Writing as the role is different from writing about it: the role’s voice speaks, sees from its own position, and never steps outside to report itself.

### Sharing out the words

The 150–200-word range is part of the task, and the words are shared out: an opening that establishes the form’s convention and the role’s voice; a body that carries the source’s content in two or three developed moves; a close that lands the purpose. The form’s conventions are the skeleton — a speech addresses its audience and closes memorably, a letter salutes and signs off — and a response missing them is a poorer fit to the task whatever its content.

### Worked example: the role decides the voice

A task sets a letter to a railway manager proposing a school visit, from a teacher, re-using a brochure’s content. The role — a teacher — decides the voice: practical, fond of the class, and accountable ("our Year 10 group of thirty-one, including two learners with mobility needs"). The audience — a manager — decides the register: polite, specific, easy to answer. The purpose — to secure a visit — decides the close: dates offered, a request for a reply. Every sentence could only have been written by that teacher to that manager, which is the test of a directed response.

### Where the task sits

The directed response is Section A of Paper 1, and its part (b) — the comparison taught in the next two sections — is written about the text this task produces, which is why the response’s choices matter twice: once for the ten marks of this task, and once as one half of the fifteen that follow. A response with clear conventions and a chosen register gives the comparison something to analyse; a lifted summary gives it almost nothing. Write the response as a text, not a paraphrase, and the comparison thanks you.

### Length and conventions

Two errors cost the writing marks. Writing far more, or far less, than 150–200 words is a poorer fit to the form and purpose — the range is judged as part of the task, not as a rule with a penalty attached, and an answer well outside it has less room to develop or has sprawled past its reader. And leaving out the form’s conventions — the speech with no address to its audience, the letter with no salutation — asks the reader to guess the form, which the task asked the writer to show.

## What to compare

### What the comparison is

The comparison task sets your own Question 1(a) response beside the source, with the instruction Compare, and analyses form, structure and language. It is worth fifteen marks — five for reading, ten for analysis — and the analysis is of similarities and differences, each explained by the texts’ purposes, audiences and contexts. Two texts, one pair: the source that informed your response, and the response you wrote from it, which makes the comparison uniquely answerable — you know one of the texts because you made it.

### Planning the comparison

Planning a comparison starts from each text’s purpose, audience and form, and then matches features in pairs so each point holds both texts at once: the source’s headline beside the response’s salutation, both doing the job of first contact with their readers. The pairs are built on the three grounds the task names — form, structure, language — and a plan that lists one text’s features and then the other’s has not planned a comparison, only two descriptions.

### Connectives that carry the pair

Comparative connectives carry the pairing across both texts: whereas, similarly, in contrast, both, unlike. "Whereas the article opens with a headline that teases, my letter opens with a salutation that names its reader" holds both texts in one sentence. A sentence that uses none has described one text, not compared two, and the connectives are the visible machinery of the comparison — which is why they belong in every analytical paragraph, not only at the joins.

### Comparing what they say

The comparison is about how the two texts are written — form, structure and language — and not about what they say. "Both texts talk about the farm" compares content and earns little; "both texts open by promising the reader something — the article with a headline, my speech with a greeting — but the article teases while the speech assures" compares writing and earns it. The content matters as the thing carried, not the thing judged.

### Every difference has a reason

Each difference between the two texts has a reason, and the comparison names it: a different purpose, a different audience or a different context. A change of audience changes register — the same content for officials and for friends arrives in different vocabulary, contractions and sentence patterns — and the comparison shows the change at word level, quoting the Latinate verb from one and the contraction from the other. A named tone is a claim that must be proved by the words that create it: "urgent" is shown in the imperative verbs and short sentences, or it stays a guess the reader cannot check.

### Audience change at word level

The same bridge, for two readers. For the officials: “Inspection of the northern abutment revealed structural compromise requiring closure.” For the friends: “Honestly, the bridge is worse than we thought — they’ve shut it.” The officials’ version hides its agent in the passive and reaches for Latinate verbs, which suits a body that informs without alarming; the friends’ version contracts, intensifies and names nobody, because a friend wants the news rather than the procedure. A comparison that quotes these two side by side has already shown its reader the whole mechanism of audience: the difference is in every word, and the analysis only has to point.

### Forgetting half the pair

A comparison that writes mostly about the source and little about your own response abandons half its subject — and half the marks. The habit that cures it is the paired point: no paragraph stays on one text longer than a sentence before referring across. The reader should be able to cover either column of the answer and still find both texts in every paragraph, because the comparison is the relation, not the inventory.

## Writing the comparison

### The comparative paragraph

A strong comparative paragraph holds both texts in every sentence: a point about one, the paired evidence from the other, and the explanation by purpose, audience or context. "My response opens with direct address — ‘Good morning’ — whereas the source opens with a headline, because a speech must find its hall before it can make its case, while an article must find its reader." Three moves in one sentence, and the paragraph repeats the shape until the analysis ends.

### Comparing throughout

Comparing throughout is an analysis where both texts are in view in each paragraph; two separate commentaries placed side by side compare nothing, because no sentence refers across. The test is the cover-up: hide one column of the answer, and if the visible column still makes sense, it was describing its text rather than relating the pair. The organisation by form, structure and language is available — the task names all three — and so is any other arrangement that keeps the pair in view; the structure is judged by whether the relation stays present in each paragraph.

### Worked example: a paragraph that compares

From a feature article and the diary written from it: "Both texts put the same morning in front of their readers, but the article spreads it across a frame that opens at dawn and closes at dusk, whereas the diary compresses it into the hour its writer lived — the structural choice suits each audience, since the magazine reader has chosen an arc and the diary’s page is a life’s fragment. The article’s closing image of the boy at the tank returns to its opening fog, a cyclical shape my diary never attempts, because a diary ends where the day did." Two texts in every sentence, three grounds covered, and the explanation in the purposes: the paragraph is the shape to build.

### In the exam

The comparison is the second task of Paper 1, and it follows the response it analyses: fifteen marks, five for reading and ten for analysis, opened by the instruction Compare. Form, structure and language need not be taken in separate sections — the task names them as grounds, not as headings — and any organisation that keeps both texts in view serves. The three habits that carry the marks: quote briefly from both texts, join every pair with a connective, and end each point with the purpose or audience the difference serves.

### Two texts, no bridge

The structural failure of comparison is discussing each text in turn without ever referring across to the other: a paragraph on the source, then a paragraph on the response, repeated, with no sentence holding both. The reader is left to do the comparing the task asked for. The fix is the paired point, built on a connective, in every paragraph — whereas, similarly, in contrast — and the check is mechanical: find the connective, or find the paragraph that needs rewriting.
