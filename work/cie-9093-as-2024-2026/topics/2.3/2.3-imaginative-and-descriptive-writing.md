# 2.3 Imaginative and descriptive writing

*cie-9093-as-2024-2026. Offline study notes, rendered from the approved content units on 2026-09-29. Everything here is also what the flashcards are built from.*

**What this topic asks of you**

- Shape a story through exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution; build suspense by withholding and then releasing key information.
- Create a scene through precise, original imagery and sensory detail; tell a narrative task from a descriptive one, and write what each needs.

---

## Narrative writing

### Freytag’s Pyramid

Freytag’s Pyramid is the story arc in five stages: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution. Exposition establishes the situation and the people before the trouble starts — it gives the reader the ordinary that the rest of the story will disrupt. Rising action complicates it. The climax is the story’s turning point, where the conflict meets its decisive moment. Falling action carries the consequence. The resolution shows what changed and closes what the exposition opened. The imaginative/descriptive task is one of Section B’s categories, set at 600–900 words and twenty-five marks, and the pyramid is the plan a story variant starts from.

### Planning a story on the pyramid

Planning a 600–900-word story on the pyramid means one conflict, few characters and one setting — the arc has room for nothing else. Choose one conflict a reader can hold in one sentence. Give it two or three people, and one place. Mark the exposition (about 100 words), the rising action (about 300), the climax (about 150, slowed), the falling action and resolution (about 200). A crowded arc is no arc: every extra plot is the main plot’s thief.

### Contained plot against chain of events

A contained plot is one conflict followed to its consequence; a chain of events is several conflicts interrupting each other, and the reader is left holding none of them. "The storm took the roof, then the teacher left, then the dog ran away, then the town voted" is four stories in one, each robbed of its ending; "the storm took the roof, and the family had to decide what the house still was" is one story with its decision to travel to. The word count does the arguing: 600–900 words holds one arc, properly.

### Overloading the plot

The error is the plot overloaded with events and characters, or built on events that cannot be believed: it loses the reader’s trust and the story’s shape at once. In 600–900 words, one believable conflict beats three inventions, because the reader’s trust is the story’s currency — spent on the third coincidence, there is none left for the climax. The companion error is dialogue that runs on, carries no story and is badly punctuated: speech earns its lines by moving the conflict or showing a person, and it is punctuated and paragraphed by its own rules — new speaker, new paragraph, punctuation inside the marks.

### Worked example: withholding in practice

A story opens with a narrator locking a door and refusing to say what is behind it — the withholding starts in the first line. The rising action lets three details of what is behind it escape (a sound, a smell, a name said once) without releasing the fact. The climax releases it — one sentence, the withheld thing said at last — and the story’s power is the gap between the reader’s three guesses and the one truth. The withheld thing is a debt the story repays, and the repayment lands harder for the wait: that is the mechanism of suspense, used on purpose.

### Slowing the pace at the climax

Slowing the pace at the climax — more detail of smaller time-steps, the moment broken into beats — stretches the decisive seconds so the reader lives in them rather than passing through: the rope is taken in three movements, and each movement gets its own sentence. The technique is the opposite of summary: the climax is the one place where fewer events per line makes the scene faster, because the reader stops counting events and starts standing in them.

### Missing the named effect

A story that never creates the effect the question names — drama, suspense, mystery, calm — has answered a different question: the named effect is part of the task, and the writing is judged by it. The check is to name the effect at the top of the plan and test each section against it: if the task asks for suspense, the plan withholds something; if it asks for calm, no section ends on a cliff. A well-written story that creates the wrong effect is a poorer fit to the task than a plainer one that creates the right one.

## Descriptive writing

### Imagery and the senses

Imagery is language that makes the reader see, hear, feel, taste or smell what is not in front of them; it works by choosing the one precise detail over the several approximate ones. Sensory detail places the reader inside a scene by reaching the senses the eye cannot: the smell of a market, the heat of a kitchen, the texture of a rope, the sound under the words. The best sensory writing does not decorate — it chooses: one true smell is worth three adjectives about atmosphere.

### Atmosphere

Atmosphere is the mood a scene’s details accumulate into — built from weather, light, sound and the behaviour of objects — and it is created by choice, not by naming the mood. The room is not "eerie"; the lamp gutters when the door opens, and the reader assembles eerie themselves. One precise image sets a mood better than a paragraph of mood-adjectives, because the image makes the reader assemble the mood — and what the reader assembles, they feel.

### Precise detail against adjective stacks

A precise detail is one exact thing noticed exactly; a pile of adjectives is several approximate things stacked in front of a noun, and the stack blurs what the one detail would have shown. "The old, cracked, grey, weathered, tired bucket" says nothing any of its five adjectives did not already half-say; "the bucket with the wire handle mended twice" makes the reader see the two mendings and, with them, the whole history of the yard. Precision is noticing; stacking is gesturing.

### The vague descriptive failure

Stacking adjectives in place of one precise detail is the descriptive failure of the vague: it multiplies the saying and never delivers the seeing, and the reader is handed effort where they wanted a picture. The cure is substitution — for every stack, find the one exact thing and keep only it — and the test is the deletion: remove an adjective, and if the noun’s picture survives, the adjective was decoration; if the picture improves, the adjective was noise.

### Describing and narrating

Descriptive writing builds a picture of a place or moment — a fixed point with a moving focus — and tells no story of events; narrative writing is a story with a character, a conflict and a change. A descriptive piece needs a fixed place or moment, a moving focus that carries the reader’s eye, precise detail, and no plot; a narrative piece needs a character, a conflict and a change. The imaginative/descriptive question has two variants — narrative and descriptive — and the variant the task’s wording signals decides what the piece must contain.

### The description that becomes a story

A description that turns into a story of events abandons its own kind: the reader arrived for a picture and is handed a plot, and the task’s named kind is part of its specification. The test is the event question — did anything happen? — and in a description the answer is no: the focus moves, the light changes, the crowd thickens, but nobody does anything that leads to anything else. A change of weather is not an event; a decision made in it is, and the description that wants one should not.

### Using a given sentence

A given opening or closing sentence is a fixed point of the brief: the piece is built out from it, if it opens, or built towards it, if it closes — and it is used word for word. The opening sentence is the exposition’s first beat: let it establish, not summarise. The closing sentence is the resolution’s last: let everything before it earn the exact words it says. The planning move for a closing sentence is to write it first — the target exists, so aim the whole piece at it.

### The negotiable given sentence

Leaving out or changing the sentence the question says the story must begin or end with breaks the brief in its most visible part: the given sentence is not a suggestion and its wording is not negotiable. The error is common because the given sentence is usually awkward — that is why it was chosen, to see what the writer builds from awkwardness. The fix is mechanical: copy the sentence at the top of the plan, build the piece to or from it, and check the draft’s first or last line against the copy, word for word.

### In the exam

The imaginative/descriptive question is one of the three Section B categories — 600–900 words, twenty-five marks, one of three questions to choose from, and the order changes from paper to paper so the question number never identifies the category. Read all three questions, choose by the kind you can write, and plan on the variant: the narrative plan is the pyramid, the descriptive plan is the moving focus. The table rewards the same five strands as the rest of Section B — and the effect the question names is part of the task, so write it at the top of the plan and keep it in view.
