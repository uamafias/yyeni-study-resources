# 1.3 Evidence and analytical writing

**What this topic asks of you**

- Select and interpret words and phrases from a text with care and precision, so that every analytical point stands on evidence.
- Quote briefly and aptly, join evidence to comment with precise language, and build analytical paragraphs into one cohesive argument.

---

## Selecting evidence

### Evidence

Evidence in analysis is the exact words from the text that show the feature a point is about; without it a point is only assertion. A claim that a writer "makes the flood feel alive" needs the words that do it, and "the river shrugged off its chains" is the proof. Evidence is not decoration at the end of a sentence: it is the thing the sentence is about.

### Selecting the words that matter

Selecting evidence means taking the shortest stretch of words that carries the feature: three words that do the work beat three lines that surround them. If the point is about a verb, quote the verb; if it is about an image, quote the two or three words that build it. Every extra word is padding a reader must re-read to find nothing in.

### Quotation and paraphrase

A quotation is the text’s own words; a paraphrase is the writer’s retelling. As evidence for a feature only a quotation will do, because the feature lives in the exact words: "shrugged off its chains" shows the personification, and "describes the water breaking free" merely asserts it. A paraphrase can be useful to set up a point, but as evidence for a feature it cannot do the job the exact words do.

### Interpreting a word

Interpreting a quoted word means saying what it means in this place and what it suggests beyond that, because a word can carry a literal sense and an attitude at once. "The shelter ‘survived’ the storm" means the shelter was still standing; the inverted commas or the analysis can add that it was expected to fail, so the word suggests an ordeal survived rather than a fact of engineering. Both parts — the meaning in place and the suggestion beyond — are the interpretation.

### Worked example: three words, one attitude

Take this sentence, written for the purpose: "Officials have promised to ‘review’ the crossing." The three words that carry the writer’s attitude are "promised to ‘review’". "Promised" implies a history of undertakings, so the reader hears an institution with a record; "review" in inverted commas suggests a process that delays rather than decides, so the reader is led to expect nothing to change. Those three words, quoted and interpreted, are worth a paragraph; the other twelve are context.

### Where selection meets analysis

The words chosen here are the raw material for the quoting and commenting taught next: the paragraph in topic 1.3.2 embeds them, and the analysis of Paper 1’s questions, taught in topics 1.4 and 1.5, stands on them. Select with the feature in mind — the verb, the image, the structural move — and the rest of the analysis has something to hold.

### Points with nothing under them

An analytical point with no words from the text to support it cannot be checked by a reader, so it carries no weight: "the writer makes the reader feel the danger" names an effect and offers nothing that produces it. The guard runs the other way too: a detail misread, and built on, sinks the answer, so check each quoted word against the sentence it came from before it carries a paragraph.

## Quotation, comment and argument

### Embedded quotation

An embedded quotation is a short quotation worked into the grammar of your own sentence, so the analysis and its evidence arrive together: "The verb ‘shrugged’ gives the river a body." The quotation is not dropped in as a standalone line; it is part of the sentence’s grammar, which is why the reader never has to leave your argument to find the evidence.

### Embedding a quotation

To embed a quotation: first choose a short phrase, the two or three words that carry the feature. Then fit it into your sentence’s grammar so it reads as part of your sentence — "The noun ‘rumour’ makes the rain sound half-believed" rather than "The rain is described in an interesting way. ‘Rain here is a rumour.’" Then comment on the words inside it, because the embedding exists so the comment can begin immediately.

### Why short works

Short quotations work because every word quoted can then be analysed: a two-word quotation leaves nowhere to hide, and the analysis either engages with those words or visibly fails to. A quoted sentence is mostly padding, and only two or three of its words were ever doing the work. A line reference is a location, not evidence: it tells a reader where to look but does not show the choice, and a range of lines ("lines 12–18") makes the reader do the finding that the analysis was supposed to do.

### The analytical paragraph

An analytical paragraph has four parts: a claim about the text, the evidence that shows it, the analysis of what the evidence does, and the link to the text’s purpose. In four moves: state the claim, quote the few words that show it, analyse what those words do to this reader, then say how that effect serves what the text is for. The four parts are a unit — a claim without evidence is assertion, evidence without analysis is quotation, and analysis without the link to purpose stops one sentence short of the mark.

### Verbs that join evidence to effect

Analytical verbs join evidence to effect precisely: suggests, conveys, evokes, positions, undercuts each say something different about how the choice works on the reader. "Suggests" leaves room and suits an implication; "positions" names what the writer does to the reader’s stance; "undercuts" says a later choice undoes an earlier one. The verb is where imprecision first creeps in, and where precision first shows.

### Precise effects and stock phrases

A stock phrase is one that could follow any quotation anywhere — "this engages the reader", "this creates a picture" — and it analyses nothing, because it was written for every text and so for none. A precise statement of effect could have been written for these words only: "the noun ‘rumour’ makes the rain sound half-believed, so the reader measures the dryness by the town’s scepticism." The reader it names is the text’s own audience — the worried resident, the browsing shopper — not a generic "reader" who exists nowhere.

### Worked example: a paragraph built properly

From a feature on a night market, four sentences: "The writer opens with the market’s noise rather than its name. ‘Frying onions, arguing, a baby somewhere’ places the reader inside the crowd before knowing where they stand. The list of three is all sense-impressions, so the reader is made to hear the market before being told about it. That sensory opening serves the piece’s purpose: it sells the market as an experience, which is what a reader deciding whether to visit is being asked to buy." Claim, evidence, analysis, link: the paragraph’s four parts in their order.

### From paragraphs to an argument

A coherent line of argument across a whole analysis is a single view of the text that each paragraph advances from a different side, joined by links that carry the reader from one to the next. An argument about a text organises its observations as evidence for a view; a list of observations sets them side by side and lets them add up, or not, by accident. The opening of the analysis states the view — the text’s purpose and its overall manner — and each paragraph after it is a witness for that view, so the reader leaves convinced of something rather than informed of many things.

### In the exam

The three analysis questions — Paper 1 Question 1(b), Paper 1 Question 2 and Paper 2’s reflective commentary — all reward the same habits taught here: short embedded quotations, precise effect statements named for the text’s own audience, and paragraphs that build one view of the text. The commentary on your own writing asks you to be your own analyst: quote your piece to yourself, and say what the words do. Practise the paragraph shape until it is the shape reached for under time pressure, because it is the unit every level table rewards.

### The observation string

The error is a string of separate observations with nothing joining them into a view of the text: "The writer uses a metaphor. The writer uses short sentences. The writer uses direct address." Each sentence may be true; together they say nothing, and they read as though the writer stopped at the collecting stage. The fix is the argument: decide what the text is doing to its reader, state it, and make each observation evidence for it — the metaphor, the short sentences and the direct address may all be witnesses to the same urgent purpose, and the analysis is what shows it.
