# 2.5 Review and critical writing

**What this topic asks of you**

- Structure a review: an introduction, an account of what is reviewed, an evaluation, a verdict; support every judgement with specific evidence from the thing reviewed.
- Use precise, graded evaluative vocabulary; give a balanced verdict the reader can act on.

## The shape of a review

### What a review is

A review is an informed judgement of a work, product, event or place, for readers deciding whether to try it. The sections of a review are the introduction, the account of what is reviewed, the evaluation, and the verdict — and the balance leans evaluation, because the reader can see the thing; they cannot see the judgement. The task is set as a review in every paper of the corpus: one of Section B’s category options, at 600–900 words for twenty-five marks.

### Planning a review

Planning a review means fixing five things, in order. The context: what it is, where, when, for whom. The thing itself: what actually happens, what is actually sold, what the event contains. The criteria: the standards it will be judged by, chosen before the evidence. The evidence: what happened, quoted or described specifically, for each criterion. The verdict: the judgement, graded and named for whom it suits. Criteria chosen after the evidence are criteria the evidence chose, which is why the order is the plan.

### Reviewing against recounting

Reviewing is not recounting: a review judges, and a piece that tells the day’s events in order without weighing them has written an itinerary. The account section of a review does recount — briefly, and to give the evaluation something to stand on — but the recounting is scaffolding, not the building. The test is the balance: in a 700-word review, the reader expects the timetable to take 150 words and the weighing to take the rest, and any piece built the other way round has answered a question nobody asked.

### Two balance failures

The first balance failure is the itinerary: recounting the experience in the order it happened, instead of judging it — the reader deciding whether to come wants the verdict, not the timetable. The second is the side-detail piece: spending the words on the journey there, the snacks, the parking, starves the evaluation the reader came for, and the detail belongs to the review only when it carries evidence. The cure for both is the plan’s balance: the account gets its paragraphs, the evaluation gets three times as many, and the verdict keeps its room — the shape is taught here, the judging habit is taught beside it, and together they are the review.

### Criteria, chosen and declared

Criteria are the standards a review judges by, and they are chosen by the reviewer, stated in the review, and shared with the reader before the verdict is asked of them. Judging whether a verdict is justified means checking the evidence given against the claim made: the strongest verdicts state their terms, show their workings, and are big enough to admit what fell short. A verdict of "outstanding" resting on one good song and a queue is a five-star claim on a two-star paragraph, and the reader falls through the gap — which is why the checking habit, run on your own draft before anyone else does, is part of writing reviews and not only reading them.

### Worked example: evidence doing its job

A review of a cookery course judges the teaching by one moment: "When my sauce split, the instructor did not remake it — she made me remake it, talking me through the whisk and the heat, and the second attempt was mine. That is the difference between a demonstration and a lesson, and it is worth the course fee on its own." The judgement ("worth the fee") is followed by the evidence (the split sauce, the remaking), and the reader can check the reasoning rather than borrow the verdict — which is what evidentiary logic looks like at the level of a paragraph.

### The contents page

Listing features or activities without evaluating any of them is the review’s empty version: the reader is given the contents page and no verdict. "The resort has a pool, a restaurant, a spa and a games room" is a fact sheet; "the pool is the resort’s best hour — deep enough to swim properly, warm enough to stay in past the point of wrinkling, and guarded by a man who watches the water the way my grandmother watched the tea" is a review, and the difference is the weighing. Every item named in a review costs words it must pay back in judgement.

## Evaluative language

### Evaluative lexis

Evaluative lexis is the vocabulary of judgement: graded words that say how good, how bad, and in what way, rather than the general praise that could attach to anything. Graded vocabulary runs from the adequate to the outstanding and from the disappointing to the dire, and the grade names the level — a review that only says "good" and "bad" has a scale with two rungs, and a reader climbing it learns almost nothing.

### The balanced verdict

A balanced verdict is a weighed judgement: it names the best and the worst, and says which the reader should weight — for whom the thing is worth it, and for whom it is not. A list of likes and dislikes is a tally, and a tally is not a judgement: the reader of a tally must do the weighing themselves, which is the job they came to the review to have done. The verdict’s last sentences are its terms: who should come, who should not, and why.

### Taste without an argument

Offering personal likes and dislikes with no criteria and no critique is taste without an argument: the reader learns what the reviewer enjoyed and nothing about whether they would enjoy it. "I loved the music, I hated the queue" is a diary; "the music was the evening’s best half-hour — a five-piece band playing to the room rather than the stage — and the queue for it was badly managed, one door for three hundred tickets" is a review, because the criteria are in the sentences and the reader can weigh them. The cure is the same at every level: criteria first, evidence with them, and the verdict earned rather than asserted.

### Worked example: grading the lexis

Watch the grades do their work in one sentence: "The set was outstanding — a two-storey invention the audience kept discovering — the second act dragged to adequate, and the third rescued the evening from the second." "Outstanding" names the top rung; "adequate" names the middling one, and the drop is the judgement; "rescued" is evaluative lexis doing narrative work, because the evening’s shape is part of its worth. A reader holding this sentence knows what to pay for, what to endure, and in which order — which is the whole review, compressed.

### In the exam

The review question is one of the three Section B categories, set as a review in every paper of the corpus — 600–900 words, twenty-five marks, the same five strands as the rest of Section B. The reader the brief names is the review’s audience: a website’s browsers, a magazine’s subscribers, and the register and the criteria are chosen for them. Plan the five — context, thing, criteria, evidence, verdict — write the evaluation three times the length of the account, grade every judgement with the ladder this section teaches, and end on terms the reader can act on, which is the last service a review owes and the one its reader came for.
