## Words: classes, vocabulary, figurative language, sound and form

### Word classes

The main word classes do different jobs in a sentence: a noun names (river, council, patience), a verb states an action or a being (floods, seemed), an adjective describes a noun (muddy river), and an adverb says how, when or where something happens, or modifies another word class (badly, tomorrow). Pronouns stand in for nouns already known (I, she, it, they); determiners point a reader to which one is meant (the, this, some). Naming the class matters only as a step towards what the class is doing there.

### Lexis, fields and connotation

Lexis is the vocabulary of a text; a lexical choice is the choice of one word over its near-neighbours, and analysis asks why this word rather than that one. A semantic field is a group of words from one area of meaning, and a text draws on a field to keep an idea in front of the reader. Denotation is what a word means; connotation is what it suggests beyond that. A connotation is analysed only when the specific association and its effect on the reader are named: "the noun ‘leak’ suggests carelessness and neglect, so the reader blames the maintenance, not the rain" does that; "leak has negative connotations" does not.

### Modifiers

A modifier adds detail to a noun or another word: a premodifier comes before it (a dry season), a postmodifier after it (a season without rain). The choice between them changes the pace of the noun: stacked premodifiers slow the reader before the noun arrives, a postmodifier makes them wait for it afterwards. Precise modifiers are what turn a noun the reader has met a thousand times into one they can see.

### Figurative language

Metaphor says one thing is another (the town is a furnace); simile says it is like another, using like or as (the town lies like a furnace); personification gives a non-human thing human actions or feelings (the fog swallows the ridges). Pathetic fallacy is a kind of personification in which weather or landscape is given human feeling, so the setting carries the mood: a sullen sky does the atmosphere’s work before any person speaks.

### Sound and word-building

Phonology in written texts is sound a reader can hear in the mind: alliteration repeats initial consonants, sibilance repeats s sounds, plosives (b, p, t, k) strike sharply, and onomatopoeia imitates a sound. Morphology is how words are built: prefixes and suffixes adjust meaning, compounds join two words, and a coinage invents one, often signalling informality or novelty, as when a blogger complains of being "doxxed" or describes a "staycation" and the reader hears the freshness of the coinage as part of the voice.

### Worked example: words at work

Take this sentence, written for the purpose: "The river shrugged off its chains and came for the lower fields." The concrete nouns "river" and "fields" give the reader things to see, and the personification "shrugged off its chains and came" gives the water intention: the verb "came" is a choice over "rose", and the effect is that the flood reads as a pursuer rather than a measurement. The field of restraint (chains) followed by release (came for) makes the reader brace for consequences, which suits a text warning a village at night.

### Concrete and abstract

Concrete nouns name things the senses can reach (river, bread, needle); abstract nouns name ideas (patience, loss, freedom). A shift from one to the other changes what the reader is given to hold: a paragraph of concrete nouns lets the reader build a picture, and a turn to abstractions asks them to think about what cannot be seen. "The needle, the thread, the hem" is followed by "what remained was patience", and the sentence moves from hand to idea — which is the shift to analyse when it happens.

### Two word-level errors

The first error is stopping at "positive" or "negative": a connotation is analysed only when the specific association and its effect on the reader are named, so replace "negative connotations" with the association itself, danger, warmth, officialdom, and what it makes the reader feel. The second is naming the wrong feature, a simile called a metaphor, an adjective called an adverb. The guard is the same for both: test the example against the definition before the label is written, because a word named with the wrong feature undermines the point built on it.

## Grammar: voice, aspect, tense, modality and sentence structure

### Voice, aspect, tense and modality

Grammatical voice is whether a clause is active, with the doer as subject, or passive, with the thing acted on as subject; it is not the same as a writer’s voice, which is the sense of a particular person speaking. Aspect is how an action is seen in time: progressive presents it as ongoing (is rising), perfect as completed with present relevance (has risen). Tense places an action in time, and the present tense used for past events pulls the reader into the scene as if it were unfolding. Modality is how far a writer commits: modal verbs and adverbs express certainty, possibility or obligation; high modality asserts, low modality leaves room.

### What grammar does

The passive in a sentence can hide or remove the agent, which suits a writer who wants the action without the actor: "mistakes were made" leaves the reader with nowhere to put blame. High modality asserts a writer’s authority, low modality sounds open and advisory, and each earns its effect against the text’s purpose and audience: a doctor writing "this will pass" reassures; one writing "this may pass" has said less, honestly or not. A shift from past to present tense makes events feel immediate, as if the reader were beside them rather than hearing a report: "Then the lights fail. Someone shouts." That is the historic present doing its work.

### Sentence types

A simple sentence is one clause; a compound sentence joins clauses of equal weight; a complex sentence carries one main clause with one or more dependent clauses. A minor sentence is a deliberate fragment without a finite verb — "Not this time." — and is not the same as an accidental fragment, which is an error. Syntax is the order of words in a sentence, and that order decides what the reader meets first; fronting moves an element to the start, so the reader meets it first and gives it focus.

### Worked example: the passive and the agent

Compare two sentences, written for the purpose: "The council repaired the bridge" and "The bridge was repaired." The active sentence names the doer, so the reader knows who to credit; the passive hides the agent, so the repair stands alone, and the reader cannot tell who acted. If the writer of the second sentence works for the council, the passive is doing a job: the action is on record and the actor is not. The analysis says that, which is what a label alone never does.

### Length and order as choices

A short sentence creates emphasis or tension by contrast with the longer sentences around it; a list of three builds weight through its rhythm and its sense of completion. Fronting shifts the reader’s focus to whatever has been moved first: "Down came the rain" makes the rain the news, where "The rain came down" makes the coming the fact. The moves are not decoration: they are how a writer tells the reader where to look.

### Sentence length in action

Watch a paragraph of a report move: "The inspection covered four tanks on Tuesday." A simple sentence opens, and the reader receives one fact at walking pace. "Because the third tank had been drained for repair, the inspector climbed inside, checked the seams and photographed what he found." A complex sentence follows, and the reader receives cause, action and evidence in one breath, which is the report telling them the fact is anchored. "Nothing." A minor sentence closes the sequence, and its brevity against the sentences around it performs the emptiness the inspector found. Three types, three effects: the analysis is that easy to find and that easy to miss by labelling.

### Labels are not analysis

The error to avoid is labelling sentences simple, compound or complex and saying nothing about what the structure does. After the label, ask what the structure makes this reader notice or feel at that point: a complex sentence at the moment of a flood carries the water’s cause and its consequence in one breath, which is the point. If the label is deleted and nothing about meaning or effect is left, nothing was analysed.

## The whole text: structure, perspective, rhetoric, tone and pragmatics

### Structure and cohesion

Structure is how a whole text is organised: its opening, sequence, shifts of focus, paragraphing and ending. Sentence structure is a feature of language; "structure" alone means the whole text. Cohesion is the ties that link sentences and paragraphs — discourse markers, reference and repetition. A discourse marker signals a step in the talk (however, meanwhile, first) rather than contributing content. Anaphoric reference points back to something already mentioned; cataphoric reference points forward, creating an expectation the reader carries until it is met.

### What to track in a whole text

The structural features worth tracking are the opening, shifts of focus, paragraph length, sequence, repetition across the text, and the ending. Two moves repay attention. A one-sentence paragraph stands out against the paragraphs around it, so a writer can use one to mark a turning point: after three long paragraphs of account, "That was the last time we trusted the gauge." lands as a door closing. A cyclical structure returns to its opening image or idea at the end, which makes the ending feel like an arrival and frames what changed in between: the same river at the start and the close, in a different light.

### Location is not structure analysis

Leaving structure out, or saying where things are without saying what the arrangement does, is the commonest structural error. A structural point names the arrangement (an opening, a shift, a return) and its effect on the reader: "the account of the rescue is held back until the final paragraph, so the reader spends the piece asking whether it succeeded, which is how the piece creates suspense." That sentence could not be written by someone who only knows where the paragraph is.

### Narrative perspective

First-person narration is told by an "I" inside the story; it gives closeness and limits what the reader knows to what that narrator knows, so the reader can be surprised with the narrator, or misled by them. Second-person address puts the reader inside the text as "you", involving them directly. Third-person narration is limited when it stays with one character’s knowledge, omniscient when it moves freely and reports what no single character could know. The narrator is the voice inside the text; the author is the person who wrote it — a first-person narrator who is wrong about something is not the author being wrong.

### Rhetorical devices

A rhetorical question expects no answer, because the answer is assumed; it works by making the audience supply the answer the writer wants, so the analysis names the answer it invites. Direct address uses you or your to put the reader in the sentence. A tricolon is a list of three with a rhythm that builds to a close; anaphora repeats a word or phrase at the start of successive clauses; antithesis sets opposed ideas in balance; hyperbole exaggerates for effect.

### Worked example: a question and its answer

A writer asks: "Do we want a town where the last cinema is a parking lot?" The question expects no answer; the answer it invites is "no", and the reader supplies it, which is the mechanism: having said "no" in their own head, the reader has joined the writer’s side before the argument begins. The analysis names that invited answer, or it has said nothing the label "rhetorical question" did not already say.

### Questions and loose terms

Two errors recur. A question is rhetorical only when the text does not answer it and no real answer is being waited for; a question that the text goes on to answer, or that genuinely seeks information, is not rhetorical. And a narrative term is used only when the example has the feature the term names: stream of consciousness is a continuous unpunctuated flow of a mind, not any first-person account, and a loose term undermines the analysis, because the point built on it falls with the label.

### Tone, register and pragmatics

Tone is the attitude a text conveys towards its subject or reader, and it is a result of choices, so the analysis names the choices that create it. Register is the variety of language suited to a situation, marked chiefly by formality and vocabulary; a formal register is right or wrong only for an audience and a purpose — a formal blog is as mismatched as a casual report to officials. Formality is marked by contractions, slang, first person and spoken markers on the informal side, and by nominalisation and Latinate vocabulary on the formal side. Pragmatics is meaning that comes from context and implication rather than the words alone; implicature is what a writer implies without stating it.

### Three separate questions

How common a word is, how formal it is, and the tone of the text are three separate questions. An everyday word can sit in a formal text ("the committee met"), and a rare word can be slang. Ask them separately: is the word everyday or rare? is it formal or informal? what attitude do the choices add up to? A shift of register inside a text signals a change in the writer’s relationship with the reader: a report that ends "and honestly, we were tired" has admitted the reader to confidence, and the drop is the point.

### Worked example: one paragraph, all three levels

Take a two-sentence opening from a feature on a drought year: "The river we swam in as boys is now a line of cracked mud. You can walk where the water was, and the fish you used to chase lie in the shade of the bridge that no longer crosses anything." The language level is personified absence — the river that "no longer crosses anything" makes the bridge the failure, and the second person "you can walk" puts the reader inside the dry bed. The structure level is an opening that plants an image and a promise: the piece will be about what the loss did to the town, and the reader has been handed the before ("as boys") and the after in two sentences. The whole-text level, which the analysis reaches for next, is the question of what the piece does with this opening — whether it returns to the image, whether it blames, whether it ends in the mud or the memory. One extract, three levels: an analysis that keeps them distinct is already organised.
