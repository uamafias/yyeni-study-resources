## The macroeconomic aims of government

### The five aims

The macroeconomic aims of government are the economy-wide outcomes a government works towards: low inflation, low unemployment, steady growth in the output of the economy, a current account close to balance, and redistribution of income towards a fairer distribution. They are macroeconomic because each describes the whole economy rather than one market: the general price level, not the price of one bag of maize meal; the share of the workforce without jobs across the country, not the staff of one shop in Katutura; the spread of incomes across all households, not the pay of one family. A ministry in Windhoek and a ministry in Tokyo list the same five aims, because they describe conditions an economy can be in rather than policies one government favours. The aims are destinations; the policy tools that pursue them come in topics 4.3 to 4.5.

### Reading the aims in figures

Each aim is read off an indicator. Low inflation is measured by the rate of inflation, the percentage rise in the general price level over a year. The jobs aim is measured by the unemployment rate, the percentage of the labour force without work but seeking it. Growth is measured by the annual percentage rise in real output of the whole economy, external balance is read from the current account balance, and progress towards a fairer distribution of income is read from whether the gap between high and low household incomes narrows. So a learner in Walvis Bay reading that inflation slowed to 4.1%, unemployment stood at 12.6%, output grew by 3.0% and the current account deficit reached 3.4% of export value can say which aims the economy moved towards and which it missed.

### Whom each aim serves

Low inflation tends to protect the buying power of wages and savings, so a worker in Gobabis whose pay rises by 5.0% while prices rise by 3.0% is better off in real terms, and savers and people on fixed pensions hold their ground when price rises stay slow. Low unemployment raises household incomes, keeps skills from fading and lifts tax revenue while cutting support payments. Steady growth raises the output and average incomes the economy can sustain, where the gains are shared out across households. A current account close to balance means a country is not persistently spending more abroad than it earns, which steadies its repayments to foreign lenders. A fairer distribution of income narrows the gap between rich and poor households, so more families can afford basics such as food, schooling and clinic visits. The importance of each aim varies with circumstances: where unemployment is high, jobs tend to lead the ranking; where prices are rising fast, inflation tends to lead.

### Why governments choose these aims

Each aim is chosen because missing it harms groups a government can name. Economic growth is chosen because more output means more goods and services to share. Firms expanding to meet demand tend to take on workers, which also serves the jobs aim, and higher incomes and spending raise tax revenue, which a government can use for schools, clinics and roads without raising tax rates. Growth tends to raise living standards where the gains are shared widely and not offset by pollution or other external costs; that condition is why growth is an aim to be steered rather than chased at any cost. Balance of payments stability is chosen because persistent current-account deficits require sustainable financing. Foreign borrowing can add to debt and interest payments, but investment inflows can also finance a deficit. Under a fixed exchange rate, reserves may fall if other inflows do not cover the external gap; a deficit alone does not prove reserve loss. A large surplus is not automatically desirable either: it may reflect strong exports or weak domestic spending and imports. Redistribution of income is chosen because very unequal incomes leave many households in poverty, unable to keep children in school or pay for clinic visits, which wastes talent the economy could use and can strain social stability. Namibia's income gap is among the widest in the world, so redistribution ranks high on its government's list, and progressive taxes and transfers such as the old-age pension are used to move income towards poorer households. In Sweden, by contrast, incomes are spread far more equally, and redistribution works mainly to keep them so.

### The targets governments set for each aim

An aim becomes usable when a government turns it into a measurable target - a criterion its results can be judged against. For stable prices the criterion is an inflation rate: the United Kingdom's government sets its central bank a target of 2% a year, measured by the consumer prices index, and some countries set a target range, such as 3% to 6%, so that small swings are accepted. Because the Namibian dollar is pegged to the rand, Namibia's inflation tends to follow South Africa's, so its policy aims to keep price rises close to its neighbour's. For employment the criterion is the unemployment rate, for example cutting it from 20% to below 15% within five years. For growth it is the annual rise in real GDP, such as 4% to 5% a year sustained over several years, set above the rate at which the population grows so that output per person rises. For balance of payments stability a common criterion is a current account deficit kept below a stated share of GDP, such as 3%. For redistribution it might be a rise in the share of national income received by the poorest fifth of households, or a fall in the number of people living below a poverty line. Targets do three jobs. They show whether policy is working, because a figure either meets the target or misses it. They make the government accountable, because voters and parliament can compare results with promises. And an inflation target that people believe tends to steady expectations: if workers expect prices to rise by about 2%, they tend to ask for modest pay rises, which helps keep inflation near the target.

## Conflicts between the aims

### What a conflict of aims is

A conflict between macroeconomic aims is a situation in which the conditions that serve one aim make another harder to achieve, so pushing towards one destination means moving away from another at the same time. The clearest case runs through how much the whole economy spends. When spending rises quickly across the economy, firms take on more workers and unemployment tends to fall, which serves the jobs aim. Where workplaces are near full, the same pressure tends to push prices up, straining the inflation aim, and part of the extra spending goes abroad on imports, widening a current account deficit. Chasing the growth aim hard in these conditions tends to pull the other aims out of reach.

### A conflict in numbers

One year spending across an economy grows fast. Its unemployment rate falls from 16.0% to 13.5%, which moves it towards the jobs aim. Its inflation rate rises from 3.8% to 6.9%, which moves it away from the inflation aim. Spending on imports grows by 9.0% while export earnings grow by 4.0%, so the current account moves towards a bigger deficit. Brazil's boom years showed the same shape: quick growth and low unemployment at first, then faster price rises and strain on the country's external accounts. The government reading these faces a ranking decision: easing the pressure on prices would mean cooling the spending that created the jobs.

### The three conflicts to know

Three conflicts between aims stand out. Full employment versus stable prices: as unemployment falls, firms compete for the workers who remain, so wages tend to rise faster, and where output cannot keep up with the extra spending from those wages, prices tend to rise too. Economic growth versus balance of payments stability: as output and incomes grow, households and firms buy more of everything, including imports - for Namibia, vehicles, fuel and machinery come largely from abroad - so where export earnings do not rise as fast, the current account deficit widens. Full employment versus balance of payments stability: more people in work means more income spent, and part of it goes on imports; and where a tight labour market pushes wages up faster than in trading partners, the country's exports tend to become dearer abroad, so export earnings may grow more slowly. In each pair the link is the same - more spending across the economy - working on two aims in opposite directions. Whether a conflict appears depends on conditions: where there is spare capacity and many people out of work, spending can rise without much pressure on prices, and where the extra output is sold abroad, growth can strengthen the current account rather than weaken it.

### Cause or consequence?

A common error is labelling a possible cause as a consequence, or the reverse, when writing about conflicts between aims. The boundary runs along the direction of the arrow: a quick rise in spending across the economy can be a cause of faster rises in prices; faster rises in prices are the consequence of it. The test is to ask whether the named item sets the change in motion or follows from it. If a figure or event starts the process, it is a cause; if it results from the process, it is a consequence. A learner who writes that price rises cause spending to grow has reversed a relationship that runs the other way in this analysis.
