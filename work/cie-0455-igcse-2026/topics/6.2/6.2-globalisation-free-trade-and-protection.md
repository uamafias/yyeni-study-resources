## What globalisation means

### Globalisation, defined

Globalisation is the growing integration of the economies of the world. Goods, services, finance, information and people move between countries more widely than before, and companies spread their production and their sales across several countries at once. The word names a process rather than a single event: the ties tend to grow a little closer each year, and each shock travels a little further.

### One bottle, four countries

Kaoko Juice presses marula fruit at Opuwo. In a year it sells 12 000 bottles through Namibian shops and exports 4 000 bottles to Botswana. The glass bottles come from a works in Germany and the labels from a printer in Portugal. One small firm, one bottle, four countries: trade across borders, a company buying and selling in several of them at once, and production split between them. A learner meets the same picture in any shop in Oshakati - rice from Vietnam on the shelf beside mahangu from the fields outside town.

### What it includes

When a question asks what globalisation involves, four features answer it: goods and services produced in one country and sold in others; companies with operations in more than one country; information and finance moving worldwide; and people travelling, studying and working across borders. The four travel together, because a firm that sells abroad tends to move money, people and know-how across the same borders.

## Multinational companies and their role

### What a multinational company is

A multinational company (MNC) is a firm that produces goods or services in more than one country, through plants, offices or other operations it runs there. A firm that sells abroad from its home base is exporting; it becomes multinational when it produces abroad. The country of its head office is its home country; each other country where it operates is a host country. Most products a learner can name - drinks, phones, clothing - are made and sold this way, and the spread of these firms is one of the engines of globalisation: the same product, sold in many countries, often assembled in several of them.

### A plant opens in Oshakati

Haneda Electronics keeps its head office in Nagoya, Japan, and has opened an assembly plant in Oshakati. The plant pays 300 workers an average of N$6 500 a month, and the 60 technicians it trained now also maintain machinery for other firms in the town. Its profit, about N$12 million a year, is paid to the head office. The host country, Namibia, gains jobs, wages and skills that spread beyond the plant; the home country, Japan, gains profit and a market it did not serve before, though the assembly jobs, and the tax on their wages, went to Namibia rather than to a Japanese town; and the cost that travels with them is that local electronics sellers now face a competitor with far larger resources behind it.

### Costs and benefits to host and home countries

The Oshakati plant shows the pattern; the full set of costs and benefits runs as follows.

Benefits to the host country. An MNC brings investment the host may not be able to fund from its own saving: buildings, machinery, sometimes roads and power lines. It creates jobs and pays wages, often above what local firms pay. Training and new technology spread to local firms as workers move on, as with the 60 Oshakati technicians. The government collects tax on profits and wages, and where the MNC exports, the host earns foreign currency. Consumers may gain wider choice and lower prices.

Costs to the host country. Profits are usually sent back to the home country, so part of the income created leaves the economy: Haneda's N$12 million a year is income Namibia does not keep. Local firms may be forced out by a competitor with far greater resources. Where rules are weak, wages and working conditions may be poor, and mining or processing may damage the environment or use up natural resources. An MNC is often footloose: if costs rise or a cheaper location appears, it may close the plant and move, leaving workers and suppliers without income. A large MNC may also use its size to press the government for tax breaks or lighter regulation.

Benefits to the home country. Profits sent back from abroad raise the home country's national income and may be taxed there, and shareholders gain. The firm reaches larger markets and cheaper inputs, so it can grow and keep its skilled head-office jobs - design, research, finance - at home.

Costs to the home country. Production jobs, and the investment behind them, move abroad: the assembly work at Oshakati could have been done in a Japanese town. Tax revenue is lost where profits are booked in a lower-tax host, and exports may fall where the foreign plant now supplies a market that home factories once served.

### Multinational is not a kind of ownership

Learners mix up three questions that sound alike. A public limited company sells its shares on a stock exchange - that is ownership traded to the public. A state-owned company belongs to a government - that is who owns it. A multinational company produces in more than one country - that is where it operates. One firm may be any combination of the three: a state-owned airline that flies to and keeps offices in other countries is state-owned and multinational at once. The test: ask which question the sentence answers - ownership traded, who owns it, or where it operates.

## The benefits of free trade

### Free trade, defined

Free trade is international trade without government barriers: no tariffs on imports, no import quotas, no embargoes. The word free refers to the barriers, not to prices - a good imported freely still carries a price, and the buyer still pays it. Free trade is a policy a country chooses, or an agreement countries make with each other.

### Free trade does not mean free goods

A learner hears free trade and reaches for free goods. The boundary between the ideas: free trade is about the border - goods cross without taxes, quantity limits or bans, though buyers still pay for them. A free good is about abundance - a good so plentiful relative to wants that taking it costs nothing, like sunshine in the Namib. The test that tells them apart: is the sentence about what happens at the border, or about what the good costs?

### Who gains

The benefits of free trade land on three parties. Consumers tend to face lower prices and wider choice, because imports compete with home goods and each seller has to win buyers. Producers may buy cheaper inputs from abroad and sell into markets far larger than the home one. The economy as a whole tends to shift its resources towards what the country produces best.

### Benefits for producers and for the economy

Producers gain from free trade in three ways. They can sell into larger markets: a beef producer near Gobabis that can sell freely into Europe and China faces far more buyers than Namibia's 3 million people, so it can produce on a larger scale at a lower cost per unit. They can buy cheaper or better inputs from abroad - machinery, fertiliser, packaging - which lowers their costs. And foreign competition pushes them to become more efficient, since a firm that falls behind may lose its market.

The economy as a whole gains as these effects add up. Resources shift towards the industries where the country produces at lowest cost, so total output tends to rise. Exports earn foreign currency and create jobs; competition from imports holds prices down, which eases inflation; and trade brings in new technology and foreign investment. Namibia belongs to the Southern African Customs Union and the African Continental Free Trade Area, which aim to widen these gains across the continent.

### Free trade in different kinds of country

The benefits of free trade are not the same everywhere.

High-income countries such as Germany or Japan tend to gain large export markets for high-value manufactured goods - cars, machinery, electronics - and for services such as banking and insurance, while importing raw materials, food and labour-intensive goods such as clothing more cheaply than they could produce them at home.

Low-income and middle-income countries tend to gain larger markets for their primary products and for labour-intensive manufactures. Bangladesh, for example, built a large clothing export industry that employs millions of workers, many of them women. Trade also brings foreign investment and technology that these countries could not easily fund themselves.

The gains may be unequal, however. A low-income country whose exports rest on a few primary products, such as copper or coffee, sees its earnings swing with world prices, and its young industries may be forced out by established foreign firms before they can grow. A landlocked country such as Botswana or Zambia pays more to move goods to the coast, which reduces what it gains. The size of the benefit depends on what a country can sell, how varied its exports are, and how well its firms can compete.

### Name who the question asks about

Answers about these benefits go astray at the seam between two levels. A benefit to individuals names a person - a consumer paying less for a blanket, a worker hired by an expanding exporter. A benefit to the country names the economy as a whole - resources shifting towards what it produces best, wider choice across its markets. The test: name who the question asks about - person or country - and answer at that level, each benefit labelled by who receives it.

## Methods of protection

### Protection, and its four methods

Protection is government action that shelters home producers from import competition. The syllabus names four methods. A tariff is a tax on imported goods. An import quota is a limit on the quantity that may be imported in a period. A subsidy is a payment to home producers that lowers their costs. An embargo is a ban on trade with a country or in a product.

### How each method works

The four work differently. A tariff raises the import price at the border, so buyers who mind price tend to drift towards home goods, and the government collects the tax as revenue. A quota caps the quantity directly, whatever buyers would pay - no revenue reaches the government, and the shortage itself may push prices up. A subsidy lowers home producers' costs without touching the import, so home goods may price closer to it, with the cost landing on taxpayers instead of buyers. An embargo stops the trade entirely, and serves its purpose where home production or another supplier can fill the gap.

### The blanket tariff at Walvis Bay

Namibia places a tariff of N$50 on each imported blanket. The border price of an imported blanket rises from N$200 to N$250, since N$200 + N$50 = N$250. Over the following year imports fall from 8 000 blankets to 6 000, and the government collects 6 000 x N$50 = N$300 000 in revenue. Home blankets selling at N$230 now sit N$20 below the import, and home makers lift output from 3 000 blankets towards 3 800. Each number names who gains and who pays: buyers pay more, home producers sell more, the government collects revenue.

### A subsidy and an embargo at work

Figures invented for teaching. Home boot makers in Keetmanshoop produce a pair at a cost of N$300 and need to charge N$320 to cover costs and earn a profit, while imported boots sell at N$280, so buyers tend to choose the import. The government pays home makers a subsidy of N$50 a pair. Their cost per pair falls to N$300 - N$50 = N$250, so they can charge N$270, below the imported price, and win back buyers. The import price is untouched and no tax is charged at the border; instead taxpayers pay N$50 for each pair made at home - 10 000 x N$50 = N$500 000 a year if the makers sell 10 000 pairs.

An embargo works by law, not price. If the government bans imports of a product, or all trade with one country - for example in a political dispute, or because goods are unsafe - none may enter, however low their price. Home producers and other suppliers fill the gap, but buyers lose that choice and, where few other suppliers exist, may pay much more.

## Why governments protect

### Four reasons

Governments give four reasons for protection. The infant industry: a new domestic industry, not yet big enough to produce at the low cost per unit of established rivals, is sheltered until it grows towards that scale. The declining industry: an industry losing ground to cheaper imports is sheltered so the decline is slower, giving workers and equipment time to move. The strategic industry: food, fuel or defence goods are kept going at home so that conflict or shortage abroad cannot cut off the supply. Dumping: where an exporter sells below its cost of production or below its home price to drive rivals out, protection may be used to stop the home market being taken that way.

### The infant-industry chain

The infant-industry argument runs as a chain. A new industry starts small, so its cost per unit tends to sit above established foreign rivals. Protection - a tariff or a quota - raises the import price or caps the import quantity, so home firms keep sales while they grow. As output grows, the cost per unit tends to fall with scale. Where the industry matures, the protection is withdrawn and the industry stands on its own. The caution travels with the argument: where the industry never matures, consumers keep paying the cost of a shelter that has stopped being temporary.

### Dumping, and the other two

The dumping chain runs the same way from a different start. An exporter sells below cost to win the market; home producers lose sales and may close; a tariff or quota raises the dumped price, so home producers keep their market and their workers keep their jobs. The strategic and declining reasons skip the scale argument: their case rests on security in the first and a slower, managed exit in the second, and each is judged by whether the shelter serves that purpose or settles into permanence.

### The declining and strategic industry arguments

A declining industry is one whose sales are falling for good, often because cheaper imports have taken its market. Without protection it may close suddenly, and where one town depends on it - a clothing mill, a meat-processing plant - the region faces a sharp rise in unemployment. A tariff or quota for a limited period slows the decline, giving workers time to retrain and move to other jobs, and giving the region time to attract new employers. The risk is that the protection stays: consumers keep paying higher prices, and the resources tied up in the industry are not moved to more productive uses.

A strategic industry is one the country needs in a crisis: food, fuel, medicines, power or defence equipment. If the country relied fully on imports, a war, a shortage abroad or an export ban by a supplier could cut supply when it is most needed, as when several countries restricted exports of medical supplies and some foods during the COVID-19 pandemic. Protecting a strategic industry keeps the ability to produce at home, at the cost of higher prices or subsidies in normal times. Namibia's controls on imports of maize and wheat while the local harvest is sold are often defended on these food-security grounds.

## What protection does

### Inside the home country

With the blanket tariff in place for a year, the consequences at home divide by who is asked. Consumers: an imported blanket costs N$250 against N$200 before, and home blankets rise from N$210 to N$230 as import competition eases, so buyers pay more and choose from fewer brands. Home producers: output rises from 3 000 towards 3 800 blankets, and the jobs and income behind them stay. The government: 6 000 x N$50 = N$300 000 of revenue in the year. The wider cost: home firms facing less import pressure may put off cutting their own costs, and the economy keeps resources in an industry that buyers were moving away from.

### Across the border

The exporting country's blanket makers sell 2 000 fewer blankets a year, since 8 000 - 6 000 = 2 000, and the jobs behind those sales tend to go. Their government may answer with barriers of its own on Namibian exports - fish, beef, minerals - and each answer shrinks trade on both sides. Protection is rarely a home-only policy: its costs travel, and so does the retaliation it invites.

### Does protection work?

Judged as a whole, protection tends to help the sheltered industry in the short run while spreading its cost across the economy's consumers. It is likely to justify itself where the infant industry genuinely matures and the shelter is withdrawn on time, or where the dumping is real and temporary. It is likely to fail where the shelter removes the pressure to improve, where the protected industry never reaches scale, or where partners retaliate and both countries end up trading less. The condition that decides it: what the shelter buys, and whether it is taken away once it has bought it.
