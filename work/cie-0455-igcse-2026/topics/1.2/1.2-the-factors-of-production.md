## The four factors and their rewards

### The four factors and their rewards

The factors of production are the inputs used together to make goods and services. Land is a natural resource used in production: soil, grazing, water, minerals, the site a workshop stands on. Labour is the physical and mental effort of the people who work. Capital is a man-made input that aids production - a tractor, a sewing machine, a delivery truck, a chilling tank. Enterprise is the factor that organises the other three, decides what to produce and how, and carries the risk of the venture. Each factor earns a reward: land earns rent, labour earns wages, capital earns interest, and enterprise earns profit. At Otjiwa Dairy near Otavi the pattern is visible: the family pays rent for the grazing land, wages to the twelve milkers, interest on the loan that bought the chilling tank, and the profit left at the end belongs to the owner who risked her savings.

### The same four in a Dutch greenhouse

A tomato greenhouse in the Netherlands shows the same split. The natural gas beneath the province is land, because nature supplied it. The glass panes, the boilers and the climate-control computers are capital, because people made them to aid production. The growers tending the vines supply labour. The owner who chose tomatoes over cut flowers and signed the heating loan carries enterprise. Each earns its reward: rent for the gas field, interest on the loan, wages for the growers, and the profit or the loss for the owner who took the risk.

### Land or capital? The trap to avoid

Candidates lose marks by calling a farm's borehole or its dam capital, because both cost money to install. Money paid is not the test. The test is origin. A borehole taps water that nature placed underground, so the aquifer and its water are land; the drilled casing, the pump and the pipes are capital, because people manufactured them. A dam wall is capital even though the water held behind it is land. Where a resource was supplied by nature it is land, whatever it costs to reach; where people made it to aid production it is capital, even where it stands on land the maker owns.

## Mobility of the factors

### What mobility means

Mobility of a factor of production is the ease with which it moves between one use and another, called occupational mobility, or between one place and another, called geographical mobility. A mobile factor can be shifted to where it is valued most; an immobile one stays where it is. A cutting machine that works steel one week and aluminium the next is occupationally mobile. A borehole cannot be moved from Otavi to Walvis Bay, so land tends to be the least mobile of the factors, and much of a country's capital is bolted to its site. People are capable of moving, which is why mobility is discussed with labour at the centre.

### Land, capital and enterprise on the move

Labour is not the only factor whose mobility matters. Land is geographically immobile: a hectare of grazing near Gobabis cannot be shifted to the coast. It can still be occupationally mobile, because the same hectare can change use - from cattle grazing to a solar farm, or from a maize field to housing. How easily it changes use depends on its nature and on planning rules: rocky desert has few uses, while flat land beside a town has many.

Capital varies. A delivery truck or a laptop can be moved and put to many uses, so it is mobile in both senses. A slaughter line bolted into an abattoir, a mine shaft or a dam wall stays where it was built and suits one task, so it is immobile in both senses. The more specific a piece of capital is to one product and one site, the less mobile it tends to be.

Enterprise tends to be the most mobile factor. The skill of organising land, labour and capital and judging risk is not tied to one product: a woman who ran a taxi business in Oshakati may open a bakery in the same town or a guesthouse in Swakopmund. Its mobility is held back where an entrepreneur lacks local knowledge, contacts or finance in the new market, or where licences and regulations make starting again slow and costly. The influences on mobility therefore differ by factor: physical fixity for land, specificity for capital, and knowledge, finance and regulation for enterprise.

### Why a worker stays put

Take a fish-cutter at Walvis Bay whose cannery closes. The jobs that remain are in hospitality and at the new fuel depot. For the depot job she must retrain, because her knife skills do not transfer to maintenance - that is a question of occupational mobility. If the depot posts sit inland, she must also relocate - a question of geographical mobility. Retraining takes months and costs money she may not have while unemployed; moving house means leaving family, a language community and a children's school. Barriers like these are what hold labour in place. The result is a town where workers queue for too few jobs while vacancies sit unfilled a few hundred kilometres away, and the wage the depot offers has to rise before enough workers clear the barriers.

### The Ruhr coal mines

A case outside Africa makes the same point. When the last deep coal mines of Germany's Ruhr region closed, some miners retrained as installers of solar panels and heating systems and found work in other towns; others stayed in the mining settlements, because their skills were specific to the pits and their families were rooted there. The ones who retrained changed occupation; the ones who moved city changed geography; the ones who did neither stayed without work although jobs existed elsewhere in the country.

## Quantity and quality

### Counting units against weighing units

The quantity of a factor is how much of it is available: the number of workers, the hectares of land, the count of machines. The quality of a factor is how productive each unit is in use: the skill and health of a worker, the fertility of soil, the age and precision of a machine. Quantity counts units; quality measures what each unit yields. An economy can have many workers and low output per worker, or few workers and high output per worker - both patterns are found among real economies, and the difference between them is quality, not quantity.

### What changes the quantity and quality of labour

The quantity of labour is the number of people available and willing to work. It changes with the size of the working-age population, which depends on past birth rates and death rates. It changes with net migration: workers arriving from abroad add to it and emigrants subtract from it. It changes with the ages at which people leave school and retire - raising a retirement age from 60 to 65 keeps older workers in the labour force for five more years. And it changes with the participation rate, the share of working-age people who work or seek work; more childcare places, for example, can let more parents take paid jobs.

The quality of labour is how productive each worker is. It rises with education and training, which add skills, and with better health care and nutrition, which mean fewer days lost to illness and more energy at work. It can fall when skilled workers emigrate: several southern African health services have lost trained nurses to richer countries, which cuts the quantity and the quality of their health workforce at once.

### What changes land, capital and enterprise

Land. The quantity of usable land can rise when land is reclaimed - the Netherlands has drained large areas of sea and lake to create farmland - or when exploration finds new mineral or energy deposits. It falls when soil erodes, when overgrazing and drought turn grazing into bare sand, which is desertification, or when forests are cleared and the soil washes away. The quality of land rises with fertilisers, irrigation and drainage, which raise what each hectare yields, and falls with overuse and pollution.

Capital. The quantity of capital rises when firms and governments invest in new machines, buildings and infrastructure, and falls through depreciation as existing capital wears out or becomes outdated. In a year when investment is smaller than depreciation, the stock of capital shrinks. Its quality rises with technological progress: a new machine built with newer technology tends to produce more per hour than the one it replaces.

Enterprise. The number of people starting and running businesses tends to rise where registering a business is cheap and quick, where taxes on profit are lower, where small-business loans are available and where people are willing to take risks. It tends to fall where regulation is heavy or the economy is unstable, because the expected reward then seems too small for the risk. The quality of enterprise rises with education, business training and experience, which improve how well entrepreneurs organise the other factors and judge risk.

### The same forty machinists

Oshakati Textiles ran a two-year training programme for its machinists. Before the training, 40 machinists produced 1 200 shirts a week; after it, the same 40 machinists produced 1 600 shirts a week. The quantity of labour did not change: 40 machinists before, 40 machinists after. The quality rose. Output per machinist went from 1 200 divided by 40, which is 30 shirts a week, to 1 600 divided by 40, which is 40 shirts a week, a gain of 40 - 30 = 10 shirts per machinist each week. The firm now fills larger orders without hiring, and the trained machinists command higher wages, because a more productive worker is worth more to any employer who bids for them.

### Two routes to the same goal

The garment industry of Vietnam took the quantity route in its early years: farms released workers into city factories, and output rose mainly because more hands were at the machines. The planners then met the question such economies face - growth from adding hands slows once the flow from the farms thins, so training colleges followed the factories to raise the quality of the workers already there. Adding units and improving units are the two routes an economy has, and the second tends to matter more once the first has run its course.
