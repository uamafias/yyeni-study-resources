## Why countries differ in economic development

### What development means

Economic development is the sustained improvement in a country's living standards - the income its people command and the health, education and services they can reach. One country is more developed than another where its people live longer, read more and command more goods and services. Because living standards have several sides, development is judged by indicators used together: GDP per head for income, life expectancy at birth for health, adult literacy for education, and households' reach of clean water and power.

### GDP per head, worked out

GDP per head is total output for the year divided by the population, and it is the income measure used to compare development. A southern African economy producing N$260 billion of output with 2.6 million people shows N$260 billion ÷ 2.6 million people = N$100 000 per head. Japan shows why the division matters: output of US$4 000 billion against 125 million people gives US$32 000 per head. The same total spread across twice the population would halve output per head, so totals rank size while GDP per head ranks living standards.

### Why one measure does not settle it

GDP per head is an average, so it may sit high while many households stay poor: where income is concentrated in a few hands, the typical household earns far less than the average suggests - Brazil shows how wide that gap can be. The average also skips what income cannot reach where services are missing, and countries with close GDP per head may still rank differently on life expectancy and literacy, so development is judged by several indicators together.

### Why the gaps tend to persist

The chain that keeps a low-income country behind runs through its households and its government. Where income per head is low, households spend most of what they earn on necessities and the government collects little tax revenue, so less is left for schools, clinics and piped water. Children then grow up with less schooling and worse health, so the workers they become produce less in an hour, keeping output per head low into the next generation. Children lose the schooling, households carry the water and the illness, and the government's budget starts small each year.

### Differences in productivity

Productivity is output per worker in a given period - not to be confused with production, which is total output. Productivity differs widely between countries, and it is the main reason incomes differ. A worker produces more where she has more and better capital to work with (tractors, computers, a reliable power supply), more skills, better health, and better technology and organisation.

The impact runs through income. Where each worker produces more, firms can pay higher wages and still earn a profit, so household incomes are higher; the government collects more tax at the same tax rates; and the country's goods can compete on price in world markets. Figures invented for teaching: a machinist in a small Windhoek workshop with older lathes finishes 20 parts a day, while a machinist in a highly automated factory in South Korea finishes 100. The Korean worker produces 100 / 20 = 5 times as much, so the firm can pay far more and still compete. Low productivity tends to keep wages, profits and tax revenue low, which limits the capital and training that would raise productivity - the cycle described above.

### Differences in population growth

Where population grows faster than output, GDP per head falls even while total GDP rises. Suppose a country's real output grows by 3% in a year while its population grows by 3.5%: output per head falls by about half a per cent. Many low-income countries have population growth of 2% to 3% a year, against under 1% in most high-income countries, so they must grow output fast just to hold income per head steady.

Fast population growth also changes how income is used. A large share of young dependants means more spending on schools, clinics and food, and households with many children save little, so less is left for investment in capital. Fast growth can also strain land, water and housing, especially in fast-growing towns. The impact can turn positive later: when the children reach working age and find jobs, a large workforce supporting relatively few dependants can raise output per head - provided there are jobs and the schooling to fill them. At the other extreme, a slowly growing or shrinking population, as in Japan, brings a shrinking workforce and a rising share of pensioners (topic 5.3).

### Differences in the size of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors

An economy's output and jobs can be split into three sectors. The primary sector extracts natural resources: farming, fishing, forestry and mining. The secondary sector turns materials into goods: manufacturing, food processing and construction. The tertiary sector provides services: retail, banking, tourism, transport, education and health care.

The share of each sector differs between countries and is both a cause and a sign of different levels of development. In many low-income countries a large share of workers - in some, over half - farms small plots, often for the family's own use, with little capital, so output per worker is low. In high-income countries such as the United Kingdom or the United States, most workers are in the tertiary sector and a small share in farming, where machinery lets each farmer feed many people. As countries develop, workers tend to move from the primary sector into manufacturing and then into services, because productivity and wages are higher there; China's shift of hundreds of millions of workers from farms to factories since the 1980s is the best-known recent example.

The impacts: a country that depends on the primary sector tends to have lower income per head, and its export earnings swing with world prices for crops and minerals and with the weather. Namibia shows a mixed picture: mining (diamonds, uranium) and fishing earn a large share of its export revenue but employ relatively few people, many rural households farm for their own use, and services such as government, retail and tourism employ a large share of town workers. A large primary sector does not by itself mean low development: Norway earns heavily from oil and fish yet has high income, because its resource earnings are well managed and its workers are highly productive.

### Differences in saving and investment

Saving is income not spent on consumption; investment is spending on capital goods - machinery, buildings, roads, power lines - that raise future output. The two are linked: the savings households and firms deposit in banks are lent to firms and governments to invest.

Where income per head is low, households spend most of what they earn on food, rent and transport, so saving is small. With little saving, banks have few funds to lend, interest rates on loans may be high, and firms invest little. The capital stock then grows slowly, so productivity and income stay low, which keeps saving low again: this is the saving and investment side of the cycle that holds low-income countries back.

Higher saving and investment can break it. South Korea in the 1970s and 1980s saved and invested a high share of its income, building factories, ports and schools, and its income per head rose many times over within a generation. Low-income countries may also draw in investment from abroad - from multinational companies or foreign aid - to make up for low domestic saving, and governments can invest in infrastructure from tax revenue. The impact depends on the quality of the investment: a road or power station that firms use raises productivity, while a project that stands idle does not.

### Differences in education and health care

Education and health care shape the quality of a country's workforce, so they are causes of development as well as part of what development means.

Education: workers who can read, write, calculate and use technology can do more skilled work, so productivity and wages are higher. A well-educated workforce also attracts firms making higher-value goods and services, such as electronics or banking, and adapts more quickly to new technology. Where many children leave school early, the workforce has fewer skills, firms find few qualified workers, and the country tends to stay in low-paid farming and simple manufacturing. Finland and South Korea, which invested heavily in schooling, are often given as examples of education supporting development.

Health care: where clinics, vaccinations, clean water and medicines are within reach, fewer working days are lost to illness, workers are fitter and live longer, and healthy children learn better at school. Better health care also lowers infant deaths, and parents who expect their children to survive tend to have fewer of them, slowing population growth. Where health care is poor, illness cuts output and earnings, households spend scarce income on treatment, and life expectancy is short, so the country gains less from each year of schooling it provides.

Both cost money, so they link back to income: a low-income government collects little tax and can afford fewer teachers, schools and clinics. That is why differences in education and health care tend to persist unless income rises, aid is received or spending on them is given priority.
