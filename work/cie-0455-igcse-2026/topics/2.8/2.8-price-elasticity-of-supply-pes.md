## What elasticity of supply measures

### Supply's own measure

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how strongly the quantity sellers are willing and able to offer responds when the price of the good changes. It is calculated as the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price. A milk depot in Otavi that offered 900 litres a week at N$10 a litre and 1 080 litres at N$11 shows the measure at work: the growers' response to the higher price is what PES puts a number on. Because the measure divides one percentage by another, the figure carries no units - it compares responses, not amounts.

### Supply's side, not demand's

PES is the supply-side twin of price elasticity of demand (PED), and the two are confused because their formulas share the same shape. The boundary: PED measures buyers - how the quantity consumers are willing and able to take responds to a price change - while PES measures sellers - how the quantity offered responds. The test: ask whose behaviour the question is about. When the world price of cork rises, how much more cork Portuguese growers bring to market in Portugal is a supply question; how much less the buyers in Porto take is a demand question. Settle the term once, then: PES tracks the seller's side of the market.

## Calculating and reading PES

### The formula and its reading

PES = percentage change in quantity supplied / percentage change in price. Each percentage is worked on the original figure: the change divided by the starting level, times 100. A value above 1 shows elastic supply - the quantity offered changed by a larger percentage than the price did - and a value below 1 shows inelastic supply, where the quantity offered changed by a smaller percentage.

### Kaoko Juice works one out

Kaoko Juice bottles marula juice at Opuwo. When the wholesale price of a crate rose from N$100 to N$110, the crates it offered each week rose from 200 to 260. Step one, the percentage change in quantity supplied: crates rose by 60, since 260 - 200 = 60 crates, so the change is 60 / 200 = 30%. Step two, the percentage change in price: the price rose by N$10, since 110 - 100 = N$10, so the change is 10 / 100 = 10%. Step three, the ratio: PES = 30 / 10 = 3. The bottler's supply is elastic, and the figure carries no units.

### Reading a supply curve's steepness

A supply diagram shows elasticity without numbers. Price sits on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, and the steepness of the supply curve carries the reading. A steep curve shows inelastic supply - a large price change moves the quantity offered little, as with fresh fish already landed at Walvis Bay. A flatter curve shows elastic supply - a small price change moves the quantity offered a lot, as with T-shirts a Katutura workshop prints within hours. When demand shifts, the shift tends to land mostly on price where supply is inelastic and mostly on quantity where it is elastic: coffee already picked in Brazil cannot answer this season's price.

### Three special values: 0, 1 and infinity

As with demand, three values of PES have names of their own and curve shapes to match.

Perfectly inelastic supply, PES = 0. The quantity offered does not change at all when the price changes, so the supply curve is a vertical line at that quantity. The seats in a stadium on cup-final day, the fish already landed at Walvis Bay that morning and the plots in a fully built city centre are close to this case: however high the price climbs, no more can be offered in the period. A rise in demand then lands wholly on the price.

Perfectly elastic supply, PES = infinity. Sellers will offer any quantity buyers want at one price, but none at all at a lower price. The supply curve is a horizontal line at that price. A printing workshop in Katutura that can print any number of T-shirts at the same cost each, and will sell them at N$80 but not for less, is close to this case over the range of orders it receives. A rise in demand then lands wholly on the quantity sold, and the price does not move.

Unit elasticity, PES = 1. The quantity supplied changes by the same percentage as the price. Any straight-line supply curve drawn through the origin has a PES of 1 all along it, whatever its slope: if 1 000 eggs a week are offered at N$2 and 1 250 at N$2.50, both quantity and price rise by 25 per cent. A useful rule for straight lines follows from this: a supply curve that starts from the price axis is elastic, one that starts from the quantity axis is inelastic, and one that starts from the origin has unit elasticity.

Real supply curves lie between the vertical and the horizontal extremes, and the determinants in the next section explain where.

## What makes supply elastic or inelastic

### Four influences

The determinants usually named are spare capacity - whether the seller has room to raise output without new equipment; the level of stocks - whether finished goods sit in a store ready to sell; the ease of switching production or finding extra inputs - whether workers, materials and machines can be brought in quickly; and the length of the period - how much time the seller has to respond.

### How the influences work

Spare capacity works on costs: a seller with an idle kiln or an idle third of a milling line can raise output using what it already pays for, so the quantity offered follows a rising price quickly - elastic supply. Stocks work on time: a seller with goods in store can meet a price rise this week, while a seller with empty stores must first produce, which delays the response. Both mechanisms fail at full capacity: kilns already firing flat out and yards already empty leave the seller able to change the price but not the quantity, which is the inelastic case.

### A season of mahangu

Time works through the season. A mahangu grower near Oshakati who sees grain dearer in January cannot offer more grain that season, since the harvest was planted months before - the grower's supply is inelastic within the season. Across seasons the same grower can plant more fields, so supply tends to become more elastic as the period lengthens. A Japanese electronics factory shows the other side of the same rule: with parts arriving weekly and lines that run extra shifts, its supply responds within weeks, and over a year it can order new machines - the period sets the ceiling on every other determinant.

## Why the measure matters

### For producers

Producers read the measure as a forecast of their own reach. A firm with elastic supply can usually turn a price rise or a demand rise into extra sales, because it can raise output with what it holds - capacity, stocks, quick inputs. A firm with inelastic supply may take a higher price on the same output, yet it cannot meet the extra orders a boom brings, so queues, turned-away customers and rivals' goods fill the space it cannot.

### For consumers and governments

Consumers feel the measure as price swings. Where supply is inelastic - housing in a growing town, fresh produce after a bad season - a rise in demand tends to land on price, so buyers pay for the shortage rather than see it filled. Governments feel it as a limit on policy: a subsidy or a higher guaranteed price tends to call out extra output where supply can respond, so a government hoping for more food or more housing needs producers elastic enough to answer, and where they are not, it may lean on stocks or imports instead. Flower auctions in the Netherlands show the reward of the opposite case: growers with glasshouses and same-day logistics answer a price rise within days, so demand swings are met with stems rather than with price spikes.

### Elastic is not automatically better

Whether elastic supply is the good case depends on the party. Consumers of a good in rising demand usually gain when supply is elastic - extra output, gentler prices - while sellers of a good in fixed supply may gain more from inelasticity, since the shortage lands on the price they receive. The measure itself is neutral; the judgement belongs to whoever holds the stake.
