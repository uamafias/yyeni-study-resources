## What supply means

### The definition of supply

Supply is the quantity of a good that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at a given price over a period of time. Two parts must both hold. The producer must be willing to sell at that price, and able to offer the good: with stock, capacity or the right to sell it. A fisher in Walvis Bay who would like to sell horse mackerel at N$20 a kilogram but has no catch that week is not supplying at that price; a wish to sell without the ability to offer stays outside supply.

### From schedule to curve

A producer that writes down the quantity it would offer at each price has drawn up a supply schedule. Marula Foods of Gobabis dries marula fruit for shops across Namibia: at N$20 a packet it offers 300 packets a month, at N$30 it offers 450 packets, at N$40 it offers 600 packets. Drawn as a diagram, the price per packet goes on the vertical axis and the packets a month on the horizontal, and the supply curve slopes upward through the three points. A rose grower in the Netherlands books the same upward relationship for bunches sold at auction. The curve as a whole is supply itself, not any single point on it.

### Sellers or buyers

The confusion learners make: supply and demand are mixed up, because both describe a quantity at a price. The boundary: supply belongs to the sellers - the quantity producers are willing and able to offer - while demand, from topic 2.3, belongs to the buyers - the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy. The test: ask whose decision sets the quantity. Where a firm chooses how much to put on its shelf, the quantity is supply; where a household chooses how much to take home, it is demand.

## Price and quantity supplied

### A positive relationship

When the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied tends to rise; when the price falls, the quantity supplied tends to fall. This positive relationship holds where nothing else changes: a higher price tends to make producing the good more worthwhile, so sellers offer more; a lower price leaves some production not worth the effort. The supply schedule writes the quantities down price by price, and the curve slopes upward, price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal.

### Reading the schedule

Kaoko Juice bottles juice in Opuwo: at N$8 a bottle it offers 600 bottles a week, at N$12 it offers 900. Moving along the curve between the two points is an extension in supply - from 600 to 900 bottles a week, since 900 - 600 = 300 bottles - brought about by the price alone. A juice stall in Sao Paulo in Brazil books the same upward direction.

### A movement, not a shift

When the good's own price changes, the market moves along the supply curve. Raise the price from N$8 to N$12 and the market slides up the same curve from 600 to 900 bottles, because each bottle is more worth producing at the higher price. Nothing outside the price has changed, so the curve stays put: quantity supplied, a point on the curve, has changed; supply, the whole relationship, has not. The upward slide is an extension in supply; a slide down is a contraction.

### Quantity supplied is not supply

The confusion learners make: "supply increased" is written when the price rose and the market merely moved along the curve. The boundary: supply is the whole relationship between price and quantity; quantity supplied is the single amount offered at one price. The test: what changed - the good's own price, a movement along the curve; a condition beyond the price, a shift.

### Connections

The demand curve of topic 2.3 moves along its curve the same way when price changes; topic 2.5 brings both together to find the market price.

## From one producer to the market

### One seller, then all of them

Individual supply is the quantity of a good that one producer is willing and able to offer for sale at each price. Market supply is the quantity that all the producers in a market together are willing and able to offer at that price. Bread in Katutura comes from many small bakeries: one bakery's daily offering is individual supply; the loaves all Katutura's bakeries offer together make up the market's supply.

### Adding the schedules

Oshakati Textiles weaves cloth for the northern market. At N$25 a metre it offers 400 metres a week. A second weaver offers 600 metres a week at the same price, and every other producer together offers 1 500 metres. Market supply at N$25 is found by adding across producers at that price: 400 + 600 = 1 000 metres, and 1 000 + 1 500 = 2 500 metres a week. The same addition is done at each other price, and the market supply curve is the horizontal sum of the individual curves. A weaving firm in Kyoto in Japan books its metres into its own market's total the same way.

### Why adding is the whole method

Market supply counts what every seller in the market offers at a price, so the market figure at each price is the sum of the individual figures at that same price. Adding price by price matters: adding one producer's quantities across different prices answers a different question. A new firm entering the market adds its offering at each price, so the market supply curve tends to sit further right than before.

### Connections

## The conditions of supply

### The conditions of supply

The conditions of supply are the influences on supply other than the good's own price. The list to know: the costs of the inputs used to make the good; the technology of production; indirect taxes on the good and subsidies for producing it; the prices of other goods the producer could make instead; the weather, for farm and fishing goods; and the number of sellers. The good's own price stays off the list, because it moves the market along the curve.

### A cost fall shifts the curve

Start with a condition: the price of vinyl falls for Namib Signs, a sign-painting firm in Windhoek, while its price per sign stays at N$150. Because printing becomes cheaper at each price, this causes the firm to offer more signs at N$150: 60 a month instead of 40. The whole supply curve shifts right: an increase in supply, meaning more is offered at every price. A rise in an input cost would shift it left: a decrease in supply.

### Weather on the harvest

The weather is a condition of supply for farm goods. Good rains round Otavi raise the maize offered at each price; drought shrinks it, shifting the curve left. Rice farmers in Vietnam watch the same condition: a strong Mekong delta harvest shifts their market's supply curve right, a weak one left. In each case the good's own price has not changed - the whole relationship between price and quantity has.

### Technology, taxes, other goods and the number of sellers

Technology and productivity. A new machine or method that lets each worker or each hectare produce more lowers the cost of each unit. Producing becomes more worthwhile at every price, so supply increases and the curve shifts right. A bakery in Ongwediva that replaces hand-mixing with an electric mixer can bake 600 loaves a day with the same staff instead of 400, and offers more bread at each price. A fall in productivity, such as when machines break down or many workers fall ill, has the opposite effect.

Indirect taxes and subsidies. An indirect tax, such as a duty charged on each litre of a soft drink, is paid to the government on each unit sold, so it works like an extra cost: at every price the producer keeps less, supply decreases and the curve shifts left. A subsidy is a payment from the government to the producer for each unit, which works like a cost cut: supply increases and the curve shifts right. Topic 2.11 follows what each does to the market price.

Prices of other goods the producer could make. Many producers can switch resources between products. A farmer near Mariental can plant his irrigated land with lucerne or with maize. If the price of lucerne rises sharply, lucerne becomes the more rewarding use of the land, so he tends to plant more lucerne and less maize: the supply of maize decreases at every maize price, although the maize price itself has not changed. A fall in the price of the alternative works the other way.

The number of sellers. Market supply adds up what every seller offers at each price. When new firms enter - three new kapana stands open at a taxi rank, or new fishing companies are granted quotas - more is offered at every price and market supply increases. When firms leave, as when a factory closes, market supply decreases and the curve shifts left. In Germany, the many households and farms that began selling electricity from rooftop solar panels after 2000 added to the supply of electricity in this way.

### Movement or shift

The confusion learners make: a price change and a condition change are both written as "supply changed". The boundary: the good's own price moves the market along the curve; a condition of supply, with the price unchanged, shifts the whole curve. The test: whether the good's own price changed. Where it did, the diagram shows an extension or a contraction along the curve; where it did not, a shift.
