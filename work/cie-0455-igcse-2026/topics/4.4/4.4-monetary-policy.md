## What money supply and monetary policy mean

### Money supply and the policy built on it

Money supply is the total stock of money in an economy at a point in time: the notes and coins in circulation plus the bank deposits that can be spent by card, cheque or transfer. Deposits count because they pay for goods in the same way cash does. Monetary policy is the use of changes in interest rates, the money supply and the foreign exchange rate to influence the economy's performance. In Namibia the Bank of Namibia, the central bank, carries it out, setting the rate at which it lends to commercial banks, and that rate shapes the loan and deposit rates banks offer households and firms. Central banks are often confused with commercial banks: a commercial bank takes deposits and makes loans, while the central bank stands above the banks and steers them. The Bank of Japan plays this steering role, as does the central bank of the Netherlands within the European system.

### One rate rise traced through a bank

In 2024 Oshakati Savings Bank charges households 6% a year on loans. The Bank of Namibia raises the rate at which it lends to commercial banks, so the commercial bank is likely to raise its loan rate to 9%, because its own funds cost more. The interest on a N$20 000 loan at 6% is 0.06 x N$20 000 = N$1 200 a year. At 9% it is 0.09 x N$20 000 = N$1 800. The gap of N$1 800 - N$1 200 = N$600 is what a borrowing household feels, so households tend to borrow less and the stock of money grows more slowly. This is the lever monetary policy pulls.

## The measures of monetary policy

### The three main measures

The first measure is changing interest rates: the central bank raises or lowers the rate at which it lends to commercial banks, which tend to move their loan and deposit rates the same way. The second is controlling the growth of the money supply: the central bank can sell government securities to banks, which pay from their reserves and then hold less to lend, or tighten the rules under which banks create new deposits through lending. The third is changing the foreign exchange rate: a central bank can buy its own currency with its foreign currency reserves, which tends to raise its price, or sell it, which tends to lower it. The Namibian dollar is tied one-for-one to the South African rand, so the Bank of Namibia uses this measure to hold that link steady; how exchange rates are set is developed in topic 6.3.

### A rate rise traced

Suppose the policy rate rises from 4% to 8%. Etosha Gate Lodge plans a N$150 000 kitchen extension on a bank loan: at 4% the yearly interest is 0.04 x N$150 000 = N$6 000, at 8% it is 0.08 x N$150 000 = N$12 000, so the lodge is likely to postpone. Households move the same way - dearer credit, better-rewarded saving - so borrowing falls, fewer new deposits are created, and the growth of the money supply slows.

### A change in the exchange rate traced

The third measure works on the price of the currency itself. Take an invented economy whose currency is the crown. Its central bank sells crowns and buys US dollars with them, adding to the supply of crowns on the foreign exchange market, and the exchange rate falls from 10 crowns = US$1 to 12 crowns = US$1. A bag of coffee its farmers sell for 1 200 crowns used to cost a foreign buyer 1 200 / 10 = US$120; it now costs 1 200 / 12 = US$100, so exports become cheaper abroad. A machine priced at US$50 used to cost a local firm 50 x 10 = 500 crowns; it now costs 50 x 12 = 600 crowns, so imports become dearer at home. A central bank that buys its own currency with its reserves does the reverse: the exchange rate rises, exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home, which tends to hold down the prices of imported goods such as fuel. Switzerland's central bank used the measure between 2011 and 2015, selling Swiss francs to stop the franc rising further against the euro and hurting Swiss exporters. Namibia uses the measure differently: the Namibian dollar is held at one-for-one with the rand, so the Bank of Namibia does not change that rate, and against the US dollar the Namibian dollar moves with the rand.

### Monetary measures or supply-side measures?

The boundary: a monetary measure works on the price and quantity of money - interest rates, the money supply, the exchange rate; a supply-side measure works on the economy's capacity to produce. The test is to ask what the measure changes - the price of money, or what the economy can produce. A grant helping Kaoko Juice buy bottling equipment is supply-side; a policy-rate rise is monetary.

### Interest rates or tax rates?

Interest rates and tax rates are both rates, but they belong to different policies. An interest rate is the price of borrowed money: the central bank changes the rate at which it lends to commercial banks, and their loan and deposit rates tend to follow, so it is a monetary policy measure. A tax rate is the share of income or spending the government takes by law, set in its budget, so it is a fiscal policy measure (topic 4.3). To tell them apart, ask who sets the rate and who receives the payment: interest goes to a lender, tax goes to the government. Cutting a tax rate does not make borrowing cheaper; cutting interest rates does.

### Strength and drawbacks

The strength of interest-rate changes is reach: they work through the whole banking system at once, reaching households and firms across the economy within days. Their drawbacks are that the effects arrive with a delay, spending financed without bank credit responds less, and dearer borrowing may hold back firms' building projects and jobs. A rate rise cools credit-financed spending faster than a cash-only market stall, in Vietnam as in Namibia.

## Monetary policy and the government's aims

### Which aims monetary policy moves

Monetary policy bears most directly on price stability, the aim of keeping the rise in the general price level low and steady. It touches the other aims indirectly. Lower interest rates tend to encourage borrowing, spending and output growth, which supports jobs; higher rates tend to cool them. The external position is touched too, since interest rates move exchange rates, developed in topic 6.3. So one instrument is asked to serve several aims at once, and the aims may pull in different directions.

### A rate rise against a 9% rise in prices

Suppose prices rise 9% a year and the central bank lifts its policy rate from 5% to 9%. Borrowing dearer -> households borrow less and save more -> spending slows -> firms raise prices more slowly, so pressure on the aim of price stability eases. The chain runs on: Marula Foods in Gobabis is likely to postpone a cold-room built on a loan, so output grows more slowly and jobs weaken, against the aims of growth and employment. Higher rates may also draw funds from abroad, tending to lift the currency and make exports harder to sell - developed in topic 6.3.

### One lever, several aims, different households

The same rise lands differently. Savers gain: a deposit of N$10 000 earning 8% instead of 4% brings N$800 a year instead of N$400. Borrowers lose: a N$20 000 loan costs N$1 800 a year at 9% where it cost N$1 000 at 5%. Households on fixed money incomes gain when the rise in prices slows. If Brazil's central bank lifts its rate sharply to slow a rise in prices, it accepts slower growth as the price of steadier prices - the trade-off every central bank weighs.

### Monetary policy and the balance of payments

Monetary policy reaches the balance of payments in three ways, and they can pull against one another. Through spending: a rise in interest rates slows borrowing and spending, and part of that spending is on imports, so imports tend to fall and a current account deficit tends to narrow - at the cost of slower growth and fewer jobs. Through flows of money: higher interest rates tend to attract savings from abroad, which can push the exchange rate up; a higher exchange rate makes exports dearer abroad and imports cheaper, which tends to widen the current account deficit again. Through the exchange rate directly: a central bank that pushes its exchange rate down makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, which tends to narrow the deficit where demand for exports and imports is elastic enough to respond and supply can respond. Where foreign buyers barely change what they buy, or exporters cannot produce more, the current account may barely improve, while the dearer imports raise prices at home. For Namibia the second route matters most: to keep the one-for-one link with the rand, the Bank of Namibia tends to keep its policy rate close to South Africa's, because a much lower rate could lead savers to move funds to South Africa, draining the reserves that support the link.

### Monetary policy and the distribution of income

Monetary policy also moves income between households, although it is not designed for that aim. A rise in interest rates transfers income from borrowers to savers. Take invented figures: when rates rise from 5% to 8%, a household with N$40 000 in savings earns N$3 200 a year instead of N$2 000, while a household with a N$60 000 loan pays N$4 800 a year instead of N$3 000. Savers tend to be older and better-off households, while borrowers include younger families with home loans and low-income households buying on credit, so a rate rise can widen the gap between rich and poor. Where a rate rise cuts jobs, the low-paid tend to be hit first. Against that, a rate rise that holds inflation down protects households on low or fixed incomes, who spend most of what they have on food, fuel and rent and lose most when those prices climb. A rate cut works the other way, easing the burden on borrowers and cutting the income of savers. So the effect of monetary policy on the distribution of income depends on who borrows, who saves and how far inflation and jobs move - which is one reason governments pursue redistribution mainly through taxes and transfers, topic 4.3.
