## The demand for factors of production

### What firms ask for when they hire

The demand for a factor of production is the quantity of a factor - land, labour, capital or enterprise, the four factors introduced in topic 1.2 - that firms are willing and able to employ at a given cost over a period of time. Otjiwa Dairy near Gobabis demands milkers, drivers and refrigeration tanks because it expects to sell dairy products; a cheese maker in the Netherlands demands cheesemakers and ageing cellars for the same reason.

### Derived demand: where it comes from

Factor demand is a derived demand: firms want workers and machines for the sake of the goods those inputs produce, not for the inputs themselves. When Kaoko Juice of Oshakati wins a contract to supply schools, its need for fruit-handlers and bottling staff rises with it; when a lodge near Etosha loses visitors, it takes on fewer guides. The chain runs: consumers demand more juice -> the firm needs more inputs to fill the orders -> its demand for labour rises.

### What shifts it

Four influences move a firm's factor demand. Demand for the product: a stronger market for the good tends to raise the firm's demand for the factors that make it. The price of the factor: a higher wage tends to make a firm cautious about hiring, and where equipment can do the work at lower cost per unit, the firm may employ fewer workers and more capital. Availability: where a factor is scarce in an area - bricklayers in a remote town, flat land near a port, or machines that must be imported and wait months for spare parts - the firm may be unable to employ as much as it wants and may turn to factors that are easier to obtain. A building firm in a town where few skilled bricklayers live may use precast concrete panels that need fewer of them. A factor in plentiful supply tends to be cheaper and quicker to employ. Productivity: a more productive factor produces more for each N$ spent on it, so firms tend to prefer it to less productive alternatives - a trained machinist over an untrained one at a similar wage, a fast machine over a slow one. Yet where each worker produces more, fewer workers may cover the same orders, though a busy firm with growing sales may still take people on.

### Two different demands

Learners often treat the demand for factors as if it behaved like the demand for goods. The boundary: consumers demand goods for the satisfaction they give; firms demand factors to produce output that others buy. The test is to ask who is buying and for what - a household buying juice is demanding a good, whereas the juice firm hiring fruit-handlers is demanding a factor. The driver differs too: a consumer's demand moves with tastes, incomes and price, while a firm's factor demand moves with the demand for its product.

## Labour-intensive and capital-intensive production

### Two ways to organise production

Labour-intensive production relies mainly on workers: most of the work is done by hand or with simple tools, so wages form the greater share of costs. Capital-intensive production relies mainly on machinery and equipment: machines do most of the work, so buying and running capital forms the greater share. Marula Foods in Katutura employs twelve women to sort and pack marula nuts by hand, a labour-intensive process; a Brazilian coffee cooperative dries and sorts its beans on mechanised lines, a capital-intensive one. Most firms use both factors; the label says which carries most of the work.

### The boundary between them

The test is the share of the work, or of cost, carried by each factor. A garage in Walvis Bay that strips engines with hand tools and twelve mechanics is labour-intensive even though it owns tools; a bottling plant in Windhoek that runs its line with six machine-minders is capital-intensive even though it employs people. Intensity is a matter of degree: a firm slides along the line as it replaces hand work with equipment.

### Why a firm chooses each

Choice turns on cost, the product and the market. Where wages are low and funds scarce, a dairy may pasteurise by hand because the machine's price is out of reach. Where output is large and standardised - bottling thousands of litres a day - machines tend to cost less per litre, so the firm turns capital-intensive. A German engineering firm machining precision parts runs capital-intensive lines because uniformity matters; a Katutura tailor stays labour-intensive because each garment differs. High wages push a firm towards capital; abundant labour and craft quality pull it back.

### Is one better?

Neither method suits every firm. Capital-intensive production tends to lower cost per unit where output is high, but the machines must be paid for before the first unit sells, and they stand idle when demand falls. Labour-intensive production costs little to set up and can grow or shrink with orders, but output per worker tends to be lower and quality less uniform. The better choice is the one that fits the firm's market, funds and product.

## Production and productivity

### Two measures of output

Production is the total quantity of goods or services a firm makes in a period: Kaoko Juice of Oshakati produced 6 000 bottles last month. Productivity is output per unit of a factor of production over the same period - most often output per worker. Production counts the bottles; productivity asks how much each worker, or each machine, produced. A Vietnamese shoe factory and a Namibian one can be compared on this common scale even when their total outputs differ.

### Calculating labour productivity

Labour productivity = total output / number of workers. Kaoko Juice made 6 000 bottles with 15 workers, so productivity was 6 000 / 15 = 400 bottles per worker. If the same 15 workers produce 7 500 bottles after a training course, productivity becomes 7 500 / 15 = 500 bottles per worker, a rise of 500 - 400 = 100 bottles per worker. The same arithmetic works for any factor: bottles per machine, loaves per oven.

### Why firms watch productivity

Cost per unit depends on how much each worker produces. Where productivity rises, the same wage bill covers more bottles, so cost per bottle tends to fall; the firm can cut its price, or hold the price and earn more on each sale. A fall in productivity works in reverse: the same wages are spread over fewer bottles, and cost per bottle rises. Productivity, not production, is what tells a firm whether its workforce is turning effort into output well.

### Production is not productivity

Learners often treat the two as one idea. Production is a total; productivity is a rate per factor. The test: two laundries each washing 9 000 loads a month are equal in production, but if one uses 12 workers and the other 18, the first washes 750 loads per worker and the second 500, so the first is the more productive. A firm can even raise production while productivity falls - taking on extra workers whose added output is smaller per worker than before - so a growing total is not on its own a sign of efficiency.

### What raises production, and what raises productivity

Production and productivity respond to different influences. Production depends on how many factors the firm employs - workers, machines, land and materials - on how productive they are, and on demand for the product, because a firm tends to produce what it expects to sell. Kaoko Juice could raise production from 6 000 to 8 000 bottles a month by hiring 5 more workers: if each new worker produces as much as the others, 20 x 400 = 8 000 bottles, but productivity stays at 400 bottles per worker. Productivity rises only when each worker, or each machine, produces more. Its main influences are education and training, which build skill and cut mistakes; more and better capital and technology, such as a faster bottling machine; motivation, through pay, conditions and recognition; management and organisation, such as dividing the work into tasks and arranging the line well; and the quality of inputs, from sound fruit to workers' health. In South Korea, decades of heavy spending on schooling and modern equipment are widely credited with raising output per worker. Training at Kaoko Juice raised output from 400 to 500 bottles per worker with the same 15 workers: production and productivity rose together.
