## The need for business finance

### Why businesses need finance

Finance is money the business obtains and uses. Capital, in this topic, means money invested in or lent to the business by its owners or lenders - one sense of a word with several. It is not capital goods, the machines and buildings that count as a factor of production, and it is not capital employed, the ratio input met later in Unit 5. Whenever a question here says capital, read money.

The main needs are these. Start-up capital is the money needed before trading can begin: a new car wash in Otjiwarongo needs a deposit on premises, pressure-washing equipment, an initial stock of detergent and a municipal licence before its first customer drives in. Capital for expansion pays for growth: an Omaruru tour operator adding a second safari vehicle, or a home bakery moving into a shop front. Additional working capital covers the day-to-day gap between paying out and taking in money - buying stock, meeting wages and rent while customers take weeks to pay.

Working capital deserves its own care. It is the finance held to cover day-to-day operations, and a shortage of it can stop a business trading even when its products sell well. A Cardiff food stall that sells out every lunchtime can still fail if it has no cash to buy tomorrow's ingredients - profit and cash are not the same, and the timing of payments is what working capital bridges.

### Working out a start-up finance need

Kamati plans a coffee corner in Katima Mulilo. Before her first cup is sold she must pay: equipment N$18 000, three months of stock and supplies N$6 000, licence and registration N$1 500, a deposit on the kiosk N$4 000, and opening promotion N$1 000. Adding the items - 18 000 + 6 000 + 1 500 + 4 000 + 1 000 = 30 600 - the start-up need comes to N$30 600.

Notice what the figure leaves out: the milk and sugar for month four, the assistant's wages from month two, the slow week when takings halve. Those are working-capital needs, and they arrive on a different clock from the start-up outlay. The method is general: list what must be paid and when, add it up, and read the timing off the list. The total says how much finance is needed; the timing says what kind. Kamati's start-up outlay is met before trading, while her stock top-ups return every month - which is why the next block separates short-term needs from long-term ones.

### Short-term and long-term needs

A short-term finance need comes from the day-to-day running of the business and is expected to be met within about a year - topping up festive stock, bridging a slow month, covering wages. A long-term finance need is tied to something that lasts: buildings, vehicles, machines, product development. A hotel in Innsbruck repainting its dining room has a short-term need; the same hotel building a new wing has a long-term one.

The boundary between them is the purpose of the money and the length of time the business's money stays locked in it. The distinction matters because the timing of the need points to the timing of the source: matching the two keeps repayments in step with what the money earns, an idea 5.1.2 develops when it sorts sources into short-term and long-term. A business that borrows long-term money for a two-week stock gap usually pays for years of finance it does not need, whereas one that funds a workshop on an overdraft may face the facility being reviewed just as the workshop starts to pay.

## The main sources of finance

### Internal sources

An internal source of finance is money the business already has or generates itself. The examples to know: retained profit, the part of each year's profit kept in the business; selling unwanted assets, such as an old delivery van; the owner's own savings put in; and running down stock to release cash.

Because the money is already inside the business, using it tends to avoid interest charges, avoids approval waits and leaves ownership unchanged. The limits are just as real. The amount is capped by what the business has: a two-year-old firm has little retained profit to draw on, and selling too many assets can strip the business of what it needs to operate. A Dutch bicycle workshop that saved two years of profit to buy its repair van kept full ownership and paid no interest, but the same route is typically shut to a business that has not yet made any profit.

### External sources

An external source brings money in from outside the business. The examples to know: bank loans, overdrafts, trade credit from suppliers, debt factoring, leasing, hire purchase, debentures, issuing shares, micro-finance and crowd-funding.

External sources reach sums a business could not raise from its own pocket, and each carries a cost: interest on borrowed money, a discount on factored invoices, fees on a share issue. Some bring conditions as well - a lender may ask for security, an asset the lender may claim if repayments are missed, and a schedule of repayments the business must keep to. A Windhoek printing firm that borrows N$200 000 to buy a press gains a machine immediately and pays for it over years; the price of that reach is the interest bill and the pledge of security.

### Short-term sources

The short-term sources to know are the overdraft, trade credit and debt factoring.

An overdraft is an agreed limit the business may draw its account below; interest is charged on the amount used, day by day, and the facility is reviewed rather than fixed for years. It suits a temporary gap - a Walvis Bay fish shop covering stock bought on Monday from Friday's takings. Trade credit is simpler: a supplier allows an agreed number of days before the invoice must be paid, and the business sells the goods before paying for them. Debt factoring sells the invoices to a factor at a discount, turning credit sales into immediate cash at the price of part of the revenue.

Each is designed for weeks or months. Using one for a years-long need usually means renewing it repeatedly at rising cost, which is where the long-term sources come in.

### Long-term sources: debt and equity

Long-term sources run for years. A bank loan is repaid with interest over an agreed term and is often secured on an asset. Debentures are long-term loan certificates a company issues to investors. Leasing and hire purchase pay for a specific asset over its working life - the business uses the machine while paying for it. Issuing shares sells part of the ownership instead of borrowing.

Debt and equity are the poles here. Debt is borrowed money: it is repaid with interest, and the ownership of the business does not change. Equity is shares: no repayment is owed, but part of the ownership - and with it some control - passes to the new shareholders. Legal form decides who may use equity at all: a sole trader cannot issue shares, a private limited company may typically sell them to family, friends and invited investors, whereas a public limited company may offer them to the general public on a stock exchange. An Ongwediva logistics firm taking a five-year loan keeps every shareholder's stake intact but carries the interest; the same firm issuing new shares avoids the interest but its founders end up answering to more owners.

### Micro-finance and crowd-funding

Micro-finance lends very small sums - often a few thousand dollars - to entrepreneurs who lack the collateral and formal records a commercial lender usually asks for. Repayment is typically in frequent small instalments, and group schemes, where members guarantee one another, are common. A Katutura market trader borrowing N$3 000 to stock a stall and repaying weekly, or a Bangladeshi seamstress financing her first sewing machine, are the classic cases: the sums are small, but they buy the tools an income is built on.

Crowd-funding raises many small contributions from a large number of people, usually through an internet platform. Backers may receive the product first, a reward, or a small share of the business. A Canadian board-game designer raising the cost of a first print run from 1 400 backers is the typical shape: no bank involved, no collateral, but a marketing campaign run in public.

Both matter because they widen access. Small and start-up businesses - the ones a bank may be reluctant to lend to - can still test an idea, and the testing happens fast, at small scale, without collateral. The costs are real too: micro-loan interest can run high for the size of the sum, and a crowd-funding appeal that misses its target may leave a business with the campaign costs and none of the money.

### The factors in the financial choice

Which source fits is decided factor by factor.

Size and legal form first. A sole trader cannot issue shares, so equity is out of reach until the business becomes a company; a private limited company may typically sell shares to family, friends and invited investors; a public limited company may offer them to the general public. Small businesses are also held to smaller sums - a lender judging a one-person business weighs the person as much as the plan. Next, the amount required: a N$15 000 stock top-up is overdraft or trade-credit territory, whereas a N$500 000 workshop calls for a loan, debentures or a share issue. Then the length of time: the need's duration should match the source's, so a machine that earns for ten years may be financed over years, while a festive stock gap is met from a source cleared within months. Existing loans weigh in: a business already repaying large loans may find further borrowing harder, because a bank is likely to judge the added repayments as raising the risk of default, and may demand extra security or a higher rate. Finally, cost, control and risk: interest and fees against the sum, the ownership an equity issue surrenders, and the danger of missing repayments on secured debt.

### Two choices worked through

Short-term case. Gobabis Hardware needs N$15 000 of extra stock for the December rush and expects to sell it within two months. An overdraft or extra trade credit fits: the money is used for weeks, and interest runs only for those weeks. A two-year loan for the same stock would tie the business to years of repayments for a need that vanishes in January.

Long-term case. Keetmanshoop Precision, a private limited company, needs N$400 000 for a machine with a ten-year working life. Its existing loans stand at N$600 000, repaid at N$9 500 a month. A further loan of N$400 000 at 11 per cent would add 400 000 x 0.11 = 44 000 a year of interest and push monthly debt costs above N$13 000 before the machine earns a cent. Issuing shares to local investors avoids the interest and the extra fixed repayments, at the price of a slice of ownership. On the facts here the share issue is the more defensible route, provided the founders accept sharing decisions - the existing debt is the fact that tips the balance.

### Recommending a source

A recommendation is worth as much as the support behind it. The structure that works: name the source, tie it to the facts - the amount, the duration, the legal form, the debts already carried - weigh the case against it, and state the condition on which the advice stands.

Suppose Swakop Tours, a partnership of two, needs N$250 000 for a second catamaran. A secured loan over five years matches an asset that serves for years; the partners cannot issue shares, and neither partner wants a third owner. The case against: interest of roughly 250 000 x 0.10 = 25 000 a year, and the catamaran pledged as security. The recommendation reads: take the secured loan, because the asset's life and the loan's term line up, provided the winter season's takings cover the repayments - the condition the partners must check before signing.

### Confusions to keep apart

Needs and sources are different axes. A need is what the money is for - start-up, expansion, working capital; a source is where the money comes from - overdraft, loan, shares. Learners often answer a question about one with a list of the other; the test is to ask what the question is about: the purpose of the money or its origin.

The word capital is a homonym and needs its sense pinned: here it means money invested in or lent to the business, not capital goods, the machines and buildings of a factor of production, and not capital employed in a ratio. And an overdraft is not a loan: an overdraft is a reviewed facility for short, small gaps with interest on the balance used, whereas a loan is a fixed sum for a fixed term with interest on the whole of it.
