## Entrepreneurs, business plans and government support for start-ups

### What an entrepreneur is

An entrepreneur is a person who identifies a business opportunity and takes the risk of starting and running a business, hoping to earn profit. The entrepreneur organises the other factors of production - the premises, the workers, the equipment and the money the business starts with - and carries the loss if the idea fails. In Katutura, the woman who opens a hair salon with her savings is an entrepreneur; so is the graduate who writes a livestock app and sells it to cattle posts across the north.

The word names the role, not the scale of the business: an entrepreneur can run one market stall or a workshop with fifty workers.

### The characteristics of successful entrepreneurs

Successful entrepreneurs tend to share a recognisable cluster of characteristics. They work hard, because a young business demands long hours and there is nobody else to pick up the work. They are creative, spotting wants that others walk past and designing products or better ways of serving customers. They take risks - putting their own money and time behind an untested idea - though the better entrepreneurs take calculated risks, checking what customers want before committing. They are optimistic and self-confident, because a founder who expects failure tends to stop trying. And they communicate well, able to persuade a customer to try the product, a supplier to deliver on terms and a lender to listen.

No single characteristic makes success certain. The characteristics raise the odds, and hard work applied to a product nobody wants still fails. They are tendencies observed in successful founders rather than a recipe, and the surroundings - the demand, the competition, the timing - count alongside the person.

### What a business plan contains

A business plan is a written document that sets out a business idea and how it will work. What it usually includes: a description of the product or service; the entrepreneur's own details and the people involved; the aims the business will chase; a marketing plan naming the target customers and how they will be reached; an operations plan covering premises, equipment and suppliers; and financial forecasts - expected revenue, start-up costs and the likely profit. Strong plans also name the competition and the risks the founder can foresee.

The plan is normally written before trading starts, and it is a living document: a good one is revised as the business learns what its customers actually do.

### How a business plan assists the entrepreneur

A business plan helps in two broad ways. First, writing it forces the founder to think the idea through before money is committed: who exactly will buy, at what price, and whether the forecast costs leave anything behind. Weak ideas are often exposed on paper, which saves an expensive mistake. Second, a finished plan is evidence for other people. A bank is more likely to lend to a start-up that arrives with researched forecasts than to one with a spoken dream, because the plan shows the thinking behind the numbers; a grant officer can set the plan against the grant's purpose; a supplier asked for credit can see the orders expected.

The plan also gives the young business targets to measure itself against, so weak sales are noticed early rather than after the money is gone.

### Why and how governments support start-ups

Start-ups matter to a government because new businesses create jobs, produce goods and services people want, and pay tax as they trade. In a region where unemployment is high, each new firm also lifts household incomes and draws activity into the local economy. Governments therefore run support programmes aimed at the two barriers a start-up faces hardest: the cost of getting going and the skills the founder lacks.

The usual forms are practical rather than dramatic. A grant is money given to the start-up, often tied to a purpose such as equipment or training, and it does not have to be repaid. Training schemes are short courses in costing, marketing, record-keeping or the technical skills of the trade. Cheap premises in government small-business centres cut the rent bill in the hardest first years, and advisory services pair a founder with someone who has run a business before.

Support has limits, and a founder should read the small print: grants may be conditional on location or sector, training takes time the business also needs, and not every applicant qualifies.

### A grant or a training scheme?

Selma opens a tailoring workshop in Otjiwarongo with four second-hand machines and one skilled assistant. Two schemes are open to her, but taking one rules out the other this year: an equipment grant of N$15 000, or a free six-month training scheme covering costing, pricing and record-keeping.

Work the choice through. If her machines are the constraint - orders waiting while fabric sits unsewn - the grant buys capacity, and the grant is the better tool. If her constraint is skill - prices guessed, costs unrecorded, no view of which garments pay - then new machines would stand idle, and the training lifts the whole workshop. A useful discipline is to ask which barrier bites hardest this year, and take the scheme that answers it.

The same arithmetic appears elsewhere: a food-cart start-up in Sao Paulo, Brazil, can take a state-agency course in pricing and tax records or an equipment grant for kitchens in poorer districts, and the right answer there also depends on whether money or skill is the binding constraint.

### Support is not ownership

Government support is not government ownership, and confusing the two misreads what a grant or a training place is. When a start-up receives support, the state takes no shares, appoints no managers and claims none of the profit: Selma keeps every decision of her workshop and every dollar it earns. Involvement in running a company is a different arrangement altogether - the state owning or directing an enterprise itself. The test that tells the pair apart: ask who owns the assets and who makes the daily decisions. If both answers point to the entrepreneur, the government is a supporter standing outside the business, not an operator inside it.

## Measuring business size and the limits of each measure

### Three ways to measure size

The size of a business can be measured in three main ways: the number of people it employs, the value of its output over a year, and capital employed. Here 'capital' means the money invested in the business's assets - buildings, vehicles, machines and equipment - not the capital goods of Topic 1.1; capital employed is that investment counted in money.

Profit is not a way of measuring size. Profit measures how well a business is doing; the three measures above capture how big it is. A mine can be vast and loss-making; a one-chair barber can be tiny and profitable. Mixing the two ideas is the classic error in questions about size.

The measures are read with their units: people for employment, N$ for output value and N$ for capital employed.

### The same two firms, three different answers

Compare Oshakati Bread with Windhoek Precision Tools. The bakery employs 40 people, sells bread worth N$6 million a year and has capital employed of N$4 million. The toolmaker employs 15 people, produces output worth N$8 million and has capital employed of N$11 million - heavy machines, few staff.

By people employed, the bakery is the bigger business: 40 against 15. By value of output, the toolmaker leads: N$8 million against N$6 million. By capital employed, the toolmaker is far ahead: N$11 million against N$4 million. So which is bigger? The honest answer names its measure first. On two of the three, the toolmaker wins; on employment alone, the bakery does. A Dutch flower exporter makes the same point more sharply: twelve staff, yet output worth far more than a labour-intensive rival with hundreds of hands, because automated growing and packing flip every ranking that headcount suggests.

### What every measure misses

Every measure has blind spots, and knowing them is the point of the topic. The employment count is bent by how a business produces: an automated juice bottler may employ a dozen people and out-sell a workshop of sixty. The value of output is bent by how much bought-in material a business passes on: a car dealer's sales include the whole value of the vehicles it resells, so its output dwarfs a repair garage that employs more people. Capital employed is bent by the trade itself: a haulier's fleet costs a fortune, while a design studio may run on little more than laptops. The pattern in what these limits include: each measure sees one dimension of size and is silent about the other two.

### Why the limitations matter

Because the three measures can disagree, a question about which business is bigger is really a judgement question. A strong answer does three things: it states which measure it is using; it checks whether the comparison is fair - same trade, same production method, same units; and it says what a conflicting measure would show. A single number is evidence, not a verdict. A firm with high output value and low employment is probably capital-intensive - a fact about its production method, not a proof of true scale. Used together, the three measures give a fairer picture than any one of them alone.

### Capital employed is not working capital

Capital employed and working capital sound like cousins and measure different things. Capital employed is the total money tied up in the business's long-term assets - the scale of what has been built. Working capital is the short-term pool: cash, inventory and customers' unpaid bills, less the amounts due to suppliers in the near future - the money keeping the day-to-day wheels turning. The test that separates them: ask which assets are being counted and over what timescale. A business can be large in capital employed and still be short of working capital, which is one way big firms run into trouble.

So working capital cannot settle a question about size. A small joinery that has just been paid by a customer may hold more working capital this month than a sawmill ten times its scale, and the comparison says which has more ready money, not which is bigger.

## Growth: why owners expand, how businesses grow, the problems and the choice to stay small

### Why owners want to expand

Expansion means increasing the scale of what a business does - more output, more customers, more outlets, a wider product range. Owners usually hold several motives at once. Higher profit: more sales tend to bring more revenue, and provided costs behave, more is left over. Market share: a bigger slice of the customers weakens rivals and strengthens the business's hand with suppliers. Personal satisfaction and status: many owners take plain pride in a growing name and the jobs it creates. Spreading risk: selling to more markets or products means a downturn in one does not sink the whole business. Lower unit costs: as output grows, fixed costs such as rent spread across more units, so the cost per unit tends to fall - up to a point, past which diseconomies of scale begin to push it back up, as unpacked below.

The motives are hopes, not results: wanting the status of a bigger firm does not make the expansion profitable. The decision to grow is a weighing of expected gain against cost and risk, and different owners weigh it differently.

### From one shop to three

Trace the chain for a bakery that opens a second and third shop. Condition: the first shop is successful and the new sites sell to customers who already know the brand. Mechanism: sales rise across three shops, the head baker's salary and the delivery van's costs spread over more loaves, and bulk flour discounts arrive - internal economies of scale, from the firm's own growth, alongside external economies of scale when the industry around it grows: as more bakeries cluster in a town, specialist suppliers move in and every bakery's costs fall. Consequence: the cost per loaf tends to drop, prices can hold while margins hold, market share climbs and the owners' profit rises with it.

The chain holds up to a point. Beyond that point, supervision across three sites thins, communication lengthens, motivation falls among bakers who rarely see the owner, and coordination failures quietly raise costs again - the point at which diseconomies overtake the savings. Growth creates the conditions for profit, but the structure must grow with the size or the chain reverses.

### Internal and external growth

There are two routes to a bigger business, and they differ in almost everything but the destination. Internal growth - also called organic growth - uses the business's own resources: opening new outlets, extending the product range, winning customers through marketing, buying better equipment. It is slower, but ownership and control stay exactly where they were, and the business expands at a pace its people can absorb. External growth joins the business to another: a merger, in which two businesses agree to combine into one, or a takeover, in which one business buys control of another. It is faster - branches, trained staff and customers arrive at once - but it needs large finance, and two organisations with different ways of working must be made into one. Business growth, internal or external, changes more than size: whichever route is taken, the problems below travel with it. Many firms mix the two - organic growth in most years, a takeover when an opportunity appears.

### Two routes for Rundu Family Bakery

Rundu Family Bakery runs one shop and wants to grow. Option A is internal growth: fit out a second shop of her own, about N$220 000 of equipment and building work, expected to add sales of around N$340 000 a year - but the new shop starts with no customers and a year of building trade ahead of it. Option B is external growth: take over a struggling competitor for about N$400 000, bringing its equipment, its trained staff and its customers from day one - but costing more and leaving two ways of working to merge.

The comparison is honest about trade-offs, not a search for a right answer. The takeover buys speed and an operating trade; the second shop costs about N$180 000 less to finance and keeps every decision with the owner. Which suits this owner depends on her cash, her patience and her confidence in merging two teams into one way of working.

### The problems growth brings

Growth creates problems even where the demand to meet is real. Finance: expansion must be paid for before it pays - a loan, new owners or money kept back from profits - and the repayments arrive whether or not the new outlet succeeds. Staffing: more people must be found and trained, and their inexperience can dent quality in exactly the months when new customers are judging the business. Control and communication: as sites multiply, layers grow between the owner and the work, decisions slow, and instructions arrive late and altered. Diseconomies of scale: past the size a business can coordinate, average costs rise rather than fall - duplicated orders, small errors repeated at every site, workers who feel anonymous. Customers: the personal service that built the name can thin out as the business grows beyond the people who gave it. Cash: growth ties money up in stock, fittings and wages for the new sites before the extra revenue lands - and a business making profit on paper can still run short of cash, because profit and cash are not the same thing; the money arrives later than the spending, which Topic 1.3.4 takes further.

### How the problems can be overcome

The remedies cost planning rather than luck. Finance: expand in phases, each step funded and proven before the next, and match borrowing to what the business could service in a weak year. Staffing: train before opening, and promote experienced people to run new sites, because a known hand at the counter teaches faster than a manual. Control and communication: delegate real authority to local managers with clear limits, shorten the reporting lines while the business is still small enough to change them, and keep one short weekly meeting that every site attends. Diseconomies: if unit costs creep up as coordination thins, restructure before growing further - fewer layers, clearer responsibilities - so the structure catches up with the size. Customer drift: write the service standards down and train to them, so what won the first customers is reproducible rather than personal to the founder.

### Why some businesses stay small

Some businesses remain small because they want to; others because they cannot. The willing ones first. The market itself is small: a specialist bookbinder in a city the size of Windhoek has hundreds of possible customers, not thousands, and demand sets a ceiling no effort lifts. The owner's objectives are modest: a business that funds the family, keeps the craft and answers to no lender is meeting the aims its owner actually set. Personal service is the product: a groomer who knows every dog's name sells exactly what a second, staffed salon would destroy. And some trades gain little from size: a dressmaker's costs barely fall as output rises, so expansion adds risk without adding much return.

The constrained ones lack what growth needs: finance they cannot obtain, management skill a bigger operation would demand, or premises the trade allows. Smallness here is a ceiling imposed from outside, not a choice - and the difference matters, because chosen smallness can be a success while forced smallness is often one bad season from failure.

### Bigger is not automatically better

The error is treating expansion and rising profit as the same thing. In fact, growth is a change in the scale of operations, and profit is what remains after every cost is paid: the second shop must be fitted out, staffed and stocked long before it earns, so a growing business can be busier and poorer at once, and a badly chosen site can lose money for years. Growth and profit are linked only when the extra revenue outruns the extra costs. The test that separates the pair: ask whether a statement is about the size of operations or about the money left over after costs. Judging a business by size alone confuses the change with the outcome.

## Business failure and why new businesses are most at risk

### When a business fails

A business fails when it stops trading because it can no longer cover its costs. Failure has shades: an owner who closes a solvent shop to retire has made a choice, not a failure; insolvency - the state of being unable to pay debts as they fall due - is the harder edge. For a small business the consequences land on real people: the owner's savings and credit are gone, the workers lose their wages, suppliers are left unpaid and the local economy loses the trade.

### Why businesses fail

Three causes account for most failures, and they feed one another. Weak management: the owner can make the product but cannot price it, keep records or watch the money - costs leak for months before anyone notices. A changing environment: a competitor opens across the road, a supplier's prices jump, a new road moves the trade away from the old site, or customers' tastes shift - the business that does not notice and adapt is outrun. Liquidity problems: the bills falling due cannot be paid, even though each sale may be profitable on paper - and profit and cash are not the same thing: profit is earned across a period while cash arrives on particular days; when cash fails, suppliers stop delivering, wages go unpaid and the doors close.

The causes compound: weak records hide the environment change, the unnoticed change drains the money, and the empty till ends the business.

### How a liquid failure happens

Watch a young catering firm in Walvis Bay take a contract to feed a construction site. Condition: it must buy N$8 000 of meat, flour and fuel now, pay wages of N$3 500 each Friday, and wait until the client pays at the end of the month. Mechanism: the outgoings land weekly while the income lands monthly, so the firm's cash drains in the gap; the next contract's stock cannot be bought, because the money that would buy it is promised elsewhere. Consequence: suppliers suspend delivery, the client hears of it and delays payment further, and the firm stops trading - profitable on every contract it ever served, because profit is earned when the meals are cooked while cash arrives when the client chooses to settle. The stakeholder cost lands on the cooks' wages and the owner's savings. The remedy is timing - a deposit, staged payments or a reserve kept back - arranged before the gap opens, not after.

### Why new businesses fail more often

A new business starts without what an established one has accumulated. It has no customers who already buy, no trading record, no settled routines, thin reserves - and often a founder whose savings are all in. Its forecasts have never been tested against real customers, and it usually leans on one product, one location and one person's health, so a single shock hits everything at once. Suppliers typically want cash up front from a firm with no history, while an established rival enjoys a month to pay, so the young firm's costs arrive earlier than everyone else's. When the owner has staked personal savings, one bad month can end the venture, because there is no cushion behind the first mistake.

The pattern is not local: a two-person software start-up in Lisbon that lives on one client's late invoices fails by the same arithmetic. And the risk falls with each year survived: every year of trading builds the record, the reserves and the customer base that absorb the next shock - which is why the first years are the most dangerous.
