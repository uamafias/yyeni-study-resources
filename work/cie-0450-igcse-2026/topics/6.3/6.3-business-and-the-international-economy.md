## Globalisation, opportunities and threats, and trade protection

### What globalisation is

Globalisation is the growing integration of the world's economies. Businesses source, produce and sell across national borders; goods, services, money and people move between countries far more freely than they once did. A phone assembled in Vietnam from parts made in three countries sits on a shelf in a Windhoek shop; Namibian beef reaches European supermarket shelves; a designer in Swakopmund serves clients in Germany without leaving her desk.

Globalisation is a process, not a decision by one business. It changes the conditions every business works in: who its competitors are, where its customers can be found, and what its inputs cost. A business does not have to export to feel it - the foreign-made goods on local shelves are globalisation reaching into the home market.

### Why globalisation has grown

Three changes made the world one market. Transport became cheap and reliable: a container of tiles travels from China to Walvis Bay for a fraction of what such cargo cost a generation ago, and air freight carries fresh Namibian oysters to Europe overnight. Communication became close to free: a manager in Windhoek emails a supplier in Mumbai and tracks a shipment online in a single afternoon.

Barriers between countries came down: governments that once taxed imports heavily have signed trade agreements and cut the duties that guarded national markets. Multinational businesses added a fourth force: spreading production across countries, they pulled their suppliers and retailers into worldwide networks.

### One small business inside a world market

Kalahari Kilns, an imagined ceramics studio in Karibib, digs local clay, but its glazes use minerals imported from South Africa, its kiln elements come from Germany, and its online shop takes orders from Namibia and the Netherlands.

The studio never chose to compete worldwide - globalisation carried it there. The internet places its products beside every rival's, and cheap parcel freight makes distance almost invisible to a buyer in Rotterdam. Its suppliers, its market and its competition are no longer bounded by Namibia's borders.

### Opportunities globalisation offers

An opportunity of globalisation is a chance the growing world market gives a business to improve its position. The first is a larger market: a business that once sold only in its home town can reach customers in many countries. The second is cheaper or better inputs: buying from a wider range of suppliers tends to lower costs or raise quality. The third is access to ideas and technology: seeing how producers elsewhere work spreads better practice quickly.

Each opportunity is real but conditional. Foreign sales depend on demand abroad holding up; imported inputs depend on shipping routes behaving. A business that takes the opportunities takes on exposure to conditions it does not control.

### Threats globalisation brings

A threat of globalisation is an outside pressure the process creates that could damage a business. The sharpest is foreign competition arriving at home: imported goods can carry prices below what a local producer's costs allow, because foreign makers may work at far greater scale. A second is dependence: a business that imports key materials or relies on foreign customers is exposed to events abroad - a shipping disruption, a slump in another country's economy, a change in another government's trade rules - none of which it can influence.

Smaller businesses often feel these threats first, because they lack the reserves to absorb a price war and the buying power to negotiate with suppliers. Spotting a threat early is what makes a response possible - cutting costs, changing products or finding customers closer to home.

### Globalisation, exporting and multinationals

Three ideas are often treated as one. Globalisation is the economy-wide process of integration. Selling abroad - exporting - is one activity a single business chooses within that process. A multinational business is a particular kind of business, with operations it controls in several countries; most exporters are not multinationals, and globalisation's opportunities and threats reach firms that never leave home.

The tests that keep them apart: ask whether a statement is about a whole economy's conditions (globalisation), about one business's sales across borders (exporting), or about where a business owns its operations (a multinational). "Kalahari Kilns sells to the Netherlands" is exporting. "Shipping costs have fallen worldwide" is globalisation. "The German kiln maker owns plants on three continents" describes a multinational.

### Import tariffs and quotas

An import tariff is a tax a government places on goods entering the country. An import quota is a limit on the quantity of a good that may be brought in over a period. Both restrict trade deliberately, and both act at the border, on what crosses it.

The two work differently. A tariff works through price: it makes every imported unit dearer to land. A quota works through quantity: once the limit is filled, no more of the good may enter that period, whatever buyers would pay. A tariff of 25 per cent on imported tiles raises their landed price by a quarter; a quota of 40 000 tonnes of poultry simply stops imports at that tonnage for the year.

### Why governments introduce them

Governments that introduce tariffs and quotas usually have one of three aims. The first is to protect home producers: taxes or limits on imports make foreign goods dearer, giving local firms room to keep sales and jobs. The second is revenue: a tariff collects money for the government on every unit that crosses the border. The third is to answer trading abroad that is seen as unfair, or to pressure another government into changing a rule.

The protection motive matters most here. When cheap imports threaten a whole local industry and the jobs in it, a government may judge that the immediate cost to consumers - higher prices - is worth paying to keep the industry alive while it adjusts.

### How a tariff works through an economy

Start from the condition: a tariff is placed on imported furniture. The mechanism runs through price. Imported pieces land dearer; retailers who stock them pass the cost on or accept thinner margins; some buyers switch to local makers, whose prices now sit closer to the imported ones.

The consequences divide by stakeholder. Local producers gain sales and can take on workers - the jobs the policy was meant to protect. Consumers pay more for every table, whatever its origin. Retailers face frustrated customers and, if they import, higher costs. The story can also travel: the country whose furniture was taxed may tax this country's exports in reply, hurting a different set of workers entirely. Whether the tariff was worth it depends on whether the protected industry uses its breathing space to become competitive.

### Trade restrictions are not legal controls

Tariffs, quotas and legal controls all change what a business may do, but they act in different places. A tariff or a quota acts at the border: it limits what may enter the country and at what price. A legal control acts inside the economy: pollution limits, safety rules and employment law regulate how any business operates, wherever it comes from.

The test: ask where the rule bites. "Imported poultry beyond 40 000 tonnes may not enter" is a quota, acting at the border. "No plant may discharge untreated waste into a river" is a legal control, acting inside every business.

## Multinationals: benefits, stakeholder impact and effects on host countries

### What a multinational is

A multinational business has its headquarters in one country - its home country - and operations it controls in at least one other country, its host countries. The operations are real ones: factories, branches, mines or offices, not just a warehouse of exports. A German car maker with plants on four continents is a multinational; so is an imagined Namibian juice group that builds a second plant in Zambia.

The label is about control and location, not size. A 60-person firm with one workshop across the border qualifies; a giant exporter that sells to fifty countries from a single plant does not, because everything it owns sits in one country.

### Why a business becomes a multinational

Becoming a multinational is a growth strategy with specific gains. Producing inside a foreign market puts the business beside its customers there, cutting transport costs and avoiding the tariffs and quotas covered in 6.3.1. It can bring cheaper or better inputs: materials from where they are abundant, labour where it is skilled or lower-cost. Spreading operations across countries spreads risk - a drought, a strike or a slump in one country no longer threatens the whole business. And scale itself pays: internal economies of scale, such as bulk buying and specialised plants, tend to lower the cost per unit, provided the business does not become too large to coordinate, at which point diseconomies begin to outweigh the savings.

A business usually crosses a border in one of two ways: by internal growth, building its own plant abroad, or by external growth, buying or joining a business that already operates there - the same two routes Topic 1.3 covers for growth in general.

### What going multinational does to stakeholders

Going multinational shifts the position of every stakeholder group, in different directions.

Owners stand to gain the most: new markets and lower costs can lift profits, though they carry the cost and risk of the foreign venture. Managers gain wider careers. Home-country workers may see roles move to the new plant, while host-country workers gain the jobs it creates. Suppliers at home may win bigger orders - or lose them to suppliers near the new plant. Customers gain products made closer to them. The host government gains investment and jobs but answers to its citizens for what the plant does; the home government loses some activity it once taxed.

The pattern is the point: one decision, many directions of effect. Tracing it stakeholder by stakeholder is the reliable way to analyse it.

### Omaruru Dairy crosses a border

Omaruru Dairy makes long-life milk for the Namibian market. Demand from Angola is growing, but tariffs on imported dairy there make its product dearer than local snacks. The board considers a plant near Lubango: it would cut transport, avoid the tariff and employ 120 Angolans.

Set against that: the plant needs N$30 million up front, managers must run an operation in another language and legal system, and profits earned in Angola may be harder to bring home. Equipment suppliers in Omaruru may win contracts; workers there fear investment flowing away from home. The board's choice turns on whether Angolan demand is durable enough to repay the risk - the judgement a business makes every time it crosses a border.

### Stakeholders, shareholders and objectives

Stakeholders and shareholders sound alike and are not the same. Shareholders are the owners of a company: they hold shares, take dividends and vote. They are one stakeholder group. Stakeholders are every group affected by the business's decisions - workers, customers, suppliers, lenders, governments, the local community - whether or not they own a share. The test: ask whether the group holds a financial ownership stake or merely a stake in what the business does.

A second slip confuses objectives with roles. A stakeholder's objectives are what that group wants out of the business; the role of a stakeholder is the part the group plays in and around it. One is about wants, the other about positions. The test: ask whether the statement says what a group wants or what it does.

### Benefits to the host country

A country where a multinational locates can gain on four fronts. Jobs: the plant hires directly, and local suppliers hire to serve it. Exports: a plant built to serve a region sells abroad, earning foreign money for the country. Choice: consumers get products no local firm made before. Investment: the multinational spends on buildings, machinery, roads and training - money the country did not have to find itself - and the skills trained at the plant spread to other businesses.

Tax revenue often follows, and a successful plant can pull other businesses into the area around it.

### Drawbacks to the host country

The same arrival can cost the country. Local businesses can lose sales: the multinational's scale and brand can draw customers away, forcing smaller firms to cut output or close, and jobs created at the plant can be outweighed by jobs lost at the firms it displaces. Repatriation of profits drains the financial return: profits flow back to the home country rather than being reinvested locally, so the host keeps the jobs but much of the money leaves. Dependence follows: a plant that can locate in a country can relocate out of one, and a town built around a single foreign plant carries that risk indefinitely.

### Weighing the gains and losses

Whether a host country gains or loses is decided by conditions, not by the label "multinational". The share of the plant's spending that goes to local suppliers decides how much of the money stays. Whether profits are reinvested decides how much returns as tax and further jobs. Whether local businesses can withstand the competition decides whether the new jobs are net jobs.

A country that negotiates local-sourcing targets, trains its workers and keeps its own firms competitive collects the bulk of the gains and limits the losses; one that simply welcomes the plant and waits may find the benefits thinner than the brochure promised.

## Exchange rates: appreciation, depreciation and their effects on traders

### Exchange rates, appreciation and depreciation

An exchange rate is the price of one country's currency expressed in another's: how much of one currency a unit of the other buys. When a Namibian business orders machines priced in US dollars, the amount it pays in Namibian dollars depends on the exchange rate between the two currencies.

The rate moves in one of two directions. Appreciation means one unit of the home currency buys more foreign currency than before - the home currency has become stronger. Depreciation means one unit buys less - the home currency has become weaker. These are relative changes: the Namibian dollar can appreciate against one currency while depreciating against another in the same month.

### Telling appreciation from depreciation

The boundary between the two is the direction of the change, and each side is the other's mirror.

Appreciation: the home currency buys more foreign currency; foreign goods become cheaper to home buyers; home goods become dearer to foreign buyers. Depreciation: the home currency buys less foreign currency; foreign goods become dearer to home buyers; home goods become cheaper to foreign buyers.

The test in one question: what does one unit of the home currency now buy abroad? More than before is appreciation; less is depreciation. The view from the other currency is the opposite: when the Namibian dollar depreciates against the euro, the euro has appreciated against it. Both statements describe the same movement, seen from each side.

### How a depreciation works through

Start from the condition: the Namibian dollar has depreciated against the currencies of the countries Namibia imports from. The mechanism: each Namibian dollar now buys less foreign currency, so goods priced in those currencies cost more Namibian dollars than before. An importer of German machine parts faces a higher bill for the same order; a retailer of imported clothing pays more to restock the same rails.

The consequences split by stakeholder. A business importing materials must raise prices and accept thinner margins, or find cheaper suppliers. Consumers see imported goods dearer, and some switch to local alternatives. Meanwhile the same movement makes Namibian goods cheaper for foreign buyers, so a business selling abroad may find its export orders rising. A business that imports materials and exports products meets both at once; which effect dominates depends on which side of its trading is larger.

### An appreciation reverses the pattern

An appreciation turns every arrow around. Imported materials and goods cost less in the home currency, so an importer's margins widen or its prices can fall, and consumers find foreign products cheaper. Exports suffer the opposite: home goods cost foreign buyers more, so an exporter either raises its foreign prices and risks losing sales or holds them and accepts a smaller return on each sale.

What the exporter loses is competitiveness - its ability to win sales against rival products, which depends on the price it must charge and on everything else customers value, such as quality and brand. A currency movement shifts the price half of that ability without the business doing anything at all, which is why traders watch rates as closely as their own costs.

### A business on both sides of the border

Swakop Instruments assembles weather stations from components imported from Denmark and sells two-thirds of its output at home, the rest to buyers in Europe. The Namibian dollar appreciates against the euro. The imported components now cost the business fewer Namibian dollars, so its margin per station improves - but its European buyers face dearer Namibian prices, and two rivals in that market price in a currency that has moved differently.

The owner's reasoning: the appreciation helps where the business pays (imports) and hurts where it is paid (exports). With imports a large share of costs and exports a small share of sales, the net effect here is likely to help - and the owner can source more components at home if export prices abroad come under pressure.

### A weaker currency is not bad for every business

Learners often write that a depreciation is bad news, full stop. The error is stopping at the first consequence. In fact, a depreciation redistributes advantage rather than destroying it: importers pay more, but exporters gain customers, and a business with local costs and foreign sales can end up distinctly better off. The same holds in reverse for an appreciation.

The test: ask which way the payments flow. Money flowing out, to pay for imports, is hurt by a depreciation; money flowing in, from being paid for exports, is helped.
