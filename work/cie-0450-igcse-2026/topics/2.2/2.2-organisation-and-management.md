## Hierarchical structures and who does what

### Levels, spans and the chain of command

A hierarchical structure arranges the members of an organisation in levels, with each level answerable to the one above it. The number of these layers is the number of levels of hierarchy. At a Windhoek security company, the managing director, the area managers, the branch supervisors and the guards form four levels: each level has authority over the one below and answers to the one above.

The span of control is the number of subordinates one manager or supervisor is directly responsible for. If the managing director supervises four area managers, her span of control is four. A narrow span means few subordinates per manager and therefore more managers; a wide span means many subordinates and fewer levels.

The chain of command is the line of authority running from the top of the hierarchy to the bottom, along which instructions pass down and accountability passes back up. A guard with a roster complaint follows the chain of command: first the branch supervisor, then the area manager, then the managing director.

### Tall and short structures

A tall structure has many levels of hierarchy with narrow spans of control; a short structure has few levels with wide spans. The two trade closeness of supervision against speed. In a tall structure each manager watches few subordinates, so supervision is close, but a decision passes through many levels before the people who must carry it out receive it, so decisions tend to be slower and messages can be distorted on the way down the chain of command. In a short structure a decision travels through few levels, so it reaches the shop floor quickly, but each manager supervises many subordinates, so supervision is less close and managers can be overloaded.

A nine-level car plant in Japan, where every quality check is signed at a level, illustrates the tall form: precise supervision, slow instructions. A four-level Namibian bus company illustrates the short form: the owner's decision on a new route reaches the drivers the same afternoon, while the operations manager watches thirty staff at once.

### Directors, managers, supervisors and other employees

The directors of a company are chosen by the shareholders to set its overall direction; they take the key long-term decisions - which markets to enter, whether to build a new plant - and answer to the shareholders for the results. Managers run a department or function within the direction the directors set: a production manager plans output, allocates staff and reports upward. Supervisors oversee day-to-day work at the first level above the operatives: a shift supervisor checks attendance, sets the line running and deals with problems as they arise. Other employees carry out the operational tasks of the business, from packing orders to serving customers.

Each level depends on the others. Directors set objectives that managers turn into departmental targets; supervisors turn targets into daily instructions for other employees; and information about results travels back up the same chain. The inter-relationships are the structure working as intended: authority flows down, accountability flows up, and each level holds the authority its responsibilities require.

### One business, two structures

Otjiwarongo Beverages bottles juice with six levels of management between the managing director and the shop floor: plant manager, production manager, shift manager, line supervisor, line workers. Each line supervisor watches eight workers, so spans are narrow and supervision is close. When the managing director approves a new bottle design, the instruction passes through five levels before the first worker sees it, and each level adds its own explanation - so the version that reaches the shop floor can differ from the version that left the top.

A competing firm in the same town runs two levels: the owner-manager and team leaders, each coordinating fifteen workers. Its instructions arrive the same day, and its team leaders must be trusted with far more. Neither shape is better in itself; each fits a different size of business and a different need for supervision.

### A manager's functions are not a director's responsibilities

A learner writes: "The functions of management are what a director does." The error is confusing the general functions every manager performs with the specific responsibilities a particular role holds. Planning, organising, coordinating, commanding and controlling are the functions of management: a shift supervisor performs all five on a small scale, and so does a managing director on a large one. A director's responsibilities are specific: setting the company's overall direction, approving major decisions, answering to shareholders. The test that tells them apart: ask whether the statement names what any manager does in any role (the functions) or what this particular role is answerable for (the responsibilities).

## The role of management: functions and delegation

### The five functions of management

Whatever the business, a manager's work falls into five functions. Planning sets objectives and decides in advance how to reach them: the manager of a Walvis Bay fish cannery plans next month's production of 120 000 tins by fixing targets, timetables and budgets. Organising arranges people, tasks and resources to carry the plan out: assigning the night shift, booking fish deliveries, allocating machines. Coordinating makes the separate parts work together, so the packing line is staffed when the canning line has output to pack. Commanding directs and instructs staff - giving orders, explaining tasks, leading the shift. Controlling checks actual results against the plan and corrects deviations: comparing tins produced with the target, investigating the shortfall, adjusting.

The five functions are general, not a job description: a shift supervisor performs all five within one shift, and a managing director performs them for the whole business.

### Why planning comes first

Planning usually comes first among the functions, because the other four need a plan to work on. There is nothing to organise people towards, coordinate around, command staff to achieve or control results against until objectives and a method have been fixed. This is why a manager who skips planning spends the week fighting problems the plan would have prevented - and why control without a plan is empty, since a check needs a target to check against.

### Delegation: trust versus control

Delegation is the passing of authority from a manager to a subordinate to carry out a task or take decisions, while the manager remains answerable for the outcome. The depot manager who hands the daily scheduling to a senior clerk has delegated: the clerk now decides the timetable, but the manager still answers to the operations director when deliveries slip.

Trust and control are the two faces of delegation. Trust gives the subordinate freedom over how the work is done; control keeps the manager's check on progress and quality. A hotel group in Denmark that leaves weekend pricing to each hotel's reception lead is trusting method while keeping control through a monthly revenue review. Delegation without any control leaves the manager unaware of problems; control pressed so tight that every step needs approval reduces delegation to doing as told. The balance hangs on the subordinate's experience and the importance of the task.

### Delegating the schedule at Gobabis Freight

Condition: Naledi, depot manager at Gobabis Freight, has 14 staff and spends whole days on paperwork, leaving the planning and controlling functions squeezed out. Mechanism: she delegates daily scheduling to Tomas, her most experienced clerk. He builds the timetable, which frees her mornings for planning, and his scheduling skill grows with practice. Because the work is now his, he takes care over it, and his confidence and motivation rise with the trust shown. Consequence for the business: the depot runs on time, and Naledi controls the outcome through a weekly review instead of checking every sheet.

The chain runs the other way where monitoring is dropped. Given delegation with no check at all, an ordering error would travel unnoticed into the following week's runs, so mistakes a weekly review would catch would instead reach customers - and Naledi, as the delegating manager, is the one answerable for that outcome.

## Leadership styles and choosing between them

### Three leadership styles

An autocratic leadership style is one in which the leader makes decisions alone and instructs staff what to do. The leader sets the task, the method and the deadline, and staff carry out instructions without being consulted. It suits urgent or safety-critical situations and teams new to the work, because decisions are quick and nothing depends on the team's judgement yet.

A democratic leadership style is one in which the leader consults the team and draws on its views before deciding. The leader still decides, but the decision is shaped by the people who do the work. A laissez-faire style leaves the team to decide and organise the work itself, with minimal interference from the leader: the leader supplies broad goals and stays available, and the team arranges the rest. A software studio in Brazil runs on exactly this pattern - small, experienced, self-directed teams with a leader who steps in only when asked.

### What each style does well and badly

The three styles trade speed for commitment. Autocratic decisions are quick and consistent, and they work where the leader knows best and time is short - but closely instructed staff may follow orders while offering few ideas, and experienced staff can find constant instruction demotivating. Democratic consultation tends to produce better-supported decisions, because people usually commit to what they helped shape - though consulting takes time and can drift toward the loudest voice. Laissez-faire gives skilled, self-directed teams room to use their judgement, but without supervision the separate parts of the work can drift apart, and newer staff may be left without the direction they need.

A leadership style is not a fixed personality trait: one head teacher might instruct a new caretaker, consult the experienced heads of department and leave the school band to its own routine - three styles in one working week, each fitted to the team and the task.

### Choosing a style in given circumstances

Whether a style is appropriate depends on three things: the experience and skill of the team, the urgency of the task, and the leader's own knowledge of the work. Read the three together before recommending anything.

Worked case one: the head nurse at a rural clinic must orient eight newly arrived temporary helpers during a malaria season, and the helpers have never worked in a clinic. Team experience is low and errors could harm patients, so an autocratic style fits: clear instruction, one way of doing each task, and the nurse deciding everything until the helpers are trained. Worked case two: the owner of a Windhoek design studio with nine experienced designers must respond to a client's detailed hotel brief within 24 hours. The designers have done hotel work before, so their knowledge is the studio's best asset even under a tight deadline: a single structured consultation, then the owner directs the detail. Speed and commitment, each in its place.

### Recommending and justifying a style

A justified recommendation names the style, ties it to the circumstances of the case, weighs the case against it, and states the conditions that would change the choice. In the clinic case, the recommendation "instruct the helpers autocratically for the first weeks" is justified when it names why - no experience, real danger, no time to build skill - and when it admits the case against (helpers given no say may feel undervalued) and the condition (once trained, ease toward consultation). A recommendation without the circumstances behind it is a guess wearing a suit.

### Democratic leadership: three boundaries

A learner writes: "Delegating means leading democratically." The error is confusing a manager's act of passing authority for one task with a leader's general way of running decisions. Democratic leadership asks the team for views before the leader decides; delegation hands a defined task to a named subordinate, whoever the leader's style. An autocrat who hands the ordering to a storeman without asking anyone's opinion has delegated. The test that tells them apart: ask whether authority over a specific task moved to a named person (delegation) or whether the team was consulted about a decision (democratic leadership).

A second confusion sits next to it: "democratic" does not mean the team decides - the leader decides after listening, whereas in laissez-faire the team itself decides. Ask who has the final say, and the two come apart.

The third boundary runs the other way. Because the democratic leader also decides, "the leader made the final choice" cannot separate democratic from autocratic. What separates them is what came first: the autocrat decides alone and instructs; the democratic leader asks for views and uses them. The test: were the team's views sought before the decision?

## Trade unions

### What a trade union is

A trade union is an organisation of workers that aims to protect and improve its members' pay and working conditions, mainly by negotiating with employers on their behalf. Unions are funded by their members and run by elected officials; a bus driver who joins one gains a voice in negotiations she never had on her own.

Union members act together through collective bargaining: pay and conditions are negotiated between the employer and the union for the members jointly, rather than worker by worker. Where talks fail, members may support agreed measures, including industrial action - the collective strength is the whole point, because one driver asking alone carries little weight, while all the drivers asking together cannot be ignored as easily.

### What union membership changes

Condition: given that most workers at a business belong to a union and ask the employer to bargain with it. Mechanism: pay and conditions move from individual deals to collective bargaining, so the employer faces one negotiation backed by the combined strength of the members, because they act together rather than one at a time. Consequences for the employees: stronger negotiating power on pay, conditions and safety, and support in disputes with management. Consequences for the business: a single channel for negotiation instead of many separate ones, wage costs that tend upward toward the union's claim, and - where talks stall - the possibility of industrial action, which could stop output and lose customers.

At an engineering works in Germany, the recognised union negotiates one pay deal for the whole plant each year: management spends a single negotiation and accepts the agreed level for everyone, and works councils handle disputes before they grow. The effect an employer feels depends on what the union asks and on the business's ability to pay.
