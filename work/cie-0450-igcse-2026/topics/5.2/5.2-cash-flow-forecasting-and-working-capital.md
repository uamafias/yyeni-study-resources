## The importance of cash and of cash-flow forecasting

### Cash and why it matters

Cash is the money a business holds and can spend now: the notes and coins in the till and the balance in its bank account. Stock on the shelf and money a customer owes are resources, but until the stock is sold and the debt is collected they pay nothing.

Every payment a business must make - wages at month end, rent, supplier invoices, transport - falls due on a set date, and it is cash that settles each one. A business that cannot pay as the dates arrive loses supplier goodwill, then workers, and at worst the business itself, even while its products are popular and its order book full. Profit measures whether the business earns more than it spends over time; cash measures whether tomorrow's bill can be paid at all. Managers watch the two side by side, and the next section shows why they can point in different directions.

### Profit is not the same as cash

Windhoek Catering Supplies signs a contract in January worth N$60 000 of revenue and N$20 000 of profit. The client pays on 60-day credit, so the cash does not arrive until March. Meanwhile February's wages and supplier invoices still fall due. On paper January was the best month of the year; at the bank, February is the dangerous one. So a business that is profitable can still run short of cash, because a sale made on credit counts towards profit immediately while the cash follows weeks later - profit and cash are not the same measure, and their timing differs.

The same works in reverse. A cafe in Bergen, Norway, takes cash at the till from every customer, so its balance can look strong in a month when one heavy insurance bill drags profit below zero. Cash and profit answer different questions: cash asks whether the business can pay what falls due next; profit asks whether, over time, it earns more than it spends.

### What a cash-flow forecast is and how it is built

A cash-flow forecast is a table of the cash a business expects to receive and to pay out each month ahead, together with the cash balance it expects to hold at the end of each month. It does not measure profit; it tracks money in and money out.

A simple one is built month by month. The opening balance is the cash held as the month starts. Expected cash inflows follow: cash sales and payments expected from debtors. Then the expected cash outflows: wages, rent, materials, loan repayments. Net cash flow is the month's inflows less its outflows, and the closing balance is the opening balance plus the net cash flow. Each closing balance becomes the next month's opening balance, which is what turns the rows into one continuous picture.

The point of the exercise is timing. A forecast shows a shortage months before it lands, while an overdraft can still be arranged or spending re-timed, and it gives a lender figures to judge a request by rather than a promise.

### A three-month forecast, worked out

Otjiwarongo Bike Repairs opens October with N$2 000 in the bank. Expected cash inflows: October N$9 000, November N$8 000, December N$14 000, when the holiday repair rush arrives. Expected cash outflows: October N$7 500, November N$9 500, December N$11 000.

October's net cash flow is 9 000 - 7 500 = N$1 500, so the closing balance is 2 000 + 1 500 = N$3 500. November's net cash flow is 8 000 - 9 500 = -1 500 - outflows ahead of inflows by N$1 500 - so the closing balance is 3 500 - 1 500 = N$2 000. December recovers: net cash flow 14 000 - 11 000 = N$3 000, closing balance 2 000 + 3 000 = N$5 000.

Read as a whole, the business stays in funds all quarter, but November is the month to watch: the balance falls by N$1 500 because November's outflows run ahead of a thin month's inflows. The figures are estimates. If the December rush disappoints, the December surplus shrinks with it, so the forecast should be re-read against each month's actual cash as the quarter unfolds.

### Amending or completing a forecast

An amendment is a change to a figure the forecast already holds: a supplier raises a price, an extra order lands, wages rise. The change flows through in order. First change the month's inflow or outflow itself. Then recompute that month's net cash flow. Then recompute its closing balance - and every later month's opening balance with it, because each month hands its closing figure to the next.

Completing an unfinished forecast uses the same relationships the other way round. A missing closing balance is the opening balance plus net cash flow; a missing net cash flow is inflows less outflows; a missing opening balance is the previous month's closing balance. If a month shows inflows of N$6 400, outflows of N$7 100 and an opening balance of N$5 000, then net cash flow is 6 400 - 7 100 = -700 and the closing balance is 5 000 - 700 = N$4 300. Each missing cell is worked from the figures around it.

### Reading a forecast

Three readings carry the meaning. First, the sign of each closing balance. A negative closing balance is not an arithmetic slip; it is the forecast saying the business expects to hold less cash than its payments require that month, and it names the size of the gap and the month it lands in.

Second, the path. Balances compared across months tell the real story: a single dip in a rising forecast is a timing problem that will pass, while a slide that deepens month after month points to outflows running ahead of inflows structurally - a problem re-timing payments will not fix.

Third, the surpluses. Cash held beyond need is idle: it could pay down borrowing, earn interest, or buy stock ahead of a busy season, provided the later months stay healthy.

Because the figures are estimates, each closing balance deserves the question of how firm the assumptions behind it are, and the forecast earns its keep when it is re-read against actual cash every month.

### Overcoming a short-term shortage

Three routes are the standard ones. A bank overdraft lets the business spend beyond its balance up to an agreed limit, so it bridges a gap of a few weeks; interest runs on the amount used, and a bank is likely to set and review the limit, because the bank carries the repayment risk. Delaying supplier payments holds cash in the business longer - asking for longer terms, or paying at the end of the agreed window - but a supplier paid late can respond by withdrawing an early-payment discount or serving the business later when stock runs short, so the saving can cost more than it saves. Asking debtors to pay more quickly pulls cash in sooner: a deposit up front, cash-only terms, or a small discount for early settlement each shorten the wait, though a discount costs revenue and pressing too hard can strain customer relationships.

The same logic extends further: a purchase can be re-timed, hiring delayed, a promotion run for cash sales, unused equipment sold. Each route shifts the timing of cash rather than creating it.

### Matching the remedy to the cause

Condition: where the forecast shows one negative month inside a healthy year - a seasonal trough, a one-off bill - the gap is a matter of timing. Mechanism: an overdraft or a delayed payment carries the business across the trough, so suppliers and workers stay paid and goodwill takes no damage while the months around the trough restore the balance. Consequence for the business: the cost is a few weeks of interest or a lost discount, and the objective of trading continuously is met.

Where the closing balances sink month after month, the same remedies disguise rather than cure: borrowing against a permanent shortfall builds interest on a debt the business cannot lift, and the underlying gap keeps widening. There the flows themselves must change - prices, costs, credit terms - or longer-term finance must be raised. The test is the shape of the forecast: a trough is bridged, a slide is fixed.

### Two confusions to keep apart

Profit is not cash. A sale earns profit when it is made, but on credit the cash follows weeks later, so a profitable month can still end with too little cash to meet the payroll - the two differ in timing, and a cash shortage rather than a lack of profit is what closes a business. The test: ask which clock the figure is on - value earned over time, or money available today.

A cash-flow forecast is not a profit statement either. Its closing balance is the cash expected at a month's end, after inflows and outflows; it says nothing about what was earned. A loan received lifts the closing balance while earning the business nothing. The test: ask whether the figure came from money moving, or from value earned.

## The concept and importance of working capital

### Working capital

Working capital is the finance held for a business's day-to-day operations: its current assets - stock, money owed by debtors and cash - less its current liabilities, the supplier invoices, overdraft and other payments due within a year. It is a snapshot at a point in time, not a moving picture.

The level matters at both ends. Too little, and the business can struggle to pay wages, suppliers and rent as they fall due, whatever its long-term prospects. Too much, and resources sit idle - cash beyond need, stock beyond sales - where they could be earning a return elsewhere in the business. What a manager aims for is a level that keeps every near-term payment safe without parking more than that.

### Working capital in practice

Karibib Meats holds stock of N$40 000, debtors owing N$35 000 and cash of N$15 000, against current liabilities of N$54 000. Its working capital is 40 000 + 35 000 + 15 000 - 54 000 = N$36 000, so its near-term resources exceed its near-term bills by that much. A courier firm in Manchester with the same figure but wages due every week would need proportionally more, because what matters is the size and frequency of the bills falling due, not the number alone. Nor does the total tell the whole story: much of a working-capital figure can sit in slow-moving stock that must be sold and collected before it can pay anything.

The forecast of the first half and this snapshot are companions. The forecast shows whether the cash inside working capital will cover the months ahead; the working-capital figure shows the cushion held today. A business can hold comfortable working capital and still face a tight month if the cash within it is small and the bills cluster.
