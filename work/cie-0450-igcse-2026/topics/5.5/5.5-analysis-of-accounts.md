## Profitability

### What profitability means

Profitability is the ability of a business to earn profit: revenue that is greater than the total costs of earning it, measured over a trading period and expressed in a currency such as Namibian dollars. A jeweller in Windhoek with revenue of N$200 000 and total costs of N$168 000 is profitable by N$32 000 for that period. A business whose costs equal its revenue breaks even; one whose costs exceed its revenue makes a loss, however busy it looks.

### Why profitability matters

Profitability matters along a chain. Condition: a business earns profit when customers value its output above its full cost. Mechanism: the surplus rewards the owners for the risk they took, and it can fund expansion from the business's own resources instead of borrowing. Consequence: the business is more likely to survive hard trading periods, keep paying wages and suppliers, and attract further investment, because a steady profit signals that the business model works.

A family bakery in Lisbon that keeps its margins healthy can open a second shop from its own savings; one that trades at a loss year after year slowly eats its owners' funds and usually closes. The same chain runs through the stakeholders: workers keep a reliable employer and suppliers keep a customer worth trusting.

### Profit is not cash

Learners often treat profit and cash as one figure. They are not the same: profit is revenue less total costs over a period, while cash is the money actually available to pay bills on a given day. Because many sales are made on credit, the timing of receipts means a profitable business can still miss a payment date. The test: ask whether the statement is about the year's result, which is profit, or about money on hand today, which is cash.

## Liquidity

### What liquidity means

Liquidity is the ability of a business to pay its short-term debts as they fall due, out of the cash and near-cash assets it holds. A bicycle shop in Copenhagen that owes its suppliers this month and holds plenty of cash and receivables can meet the payment; one whose wealth sits in slow-selling frames may not, because stock must first be sold before the money to pay is there. Liquidity is therefore about timing as much as about size: the question is not only what the business is worth, but what it can lay its hands on when each due date arrives.

### Why liquidity matters

Liquidity failure is the fastest route to closure. Condition: a business lacks the cash to pay a debt on its due date. Mechanism: the unpaid supplier withholds further deliveries or demands payment up front; workers whose wages arrive late lose trust and may leave; the lender may treat the missed payment as a default. Consequence: the business loses the goods, people and credit it runs on, and may be forced to sell assets cheaply or close - even while it is profitable on paper, because profit and cash-flow are not the same thing and the timing of receipts decides what can be paid.

### Measuring liquidity

Liquidity is usually judged with two ratios drawn from the statement of financial position. The current ratio compares all current assets with current liabilities; the acid test removes inventory, the slowest asset to turn into cash. An acid test result below 1 suggests the business is counting on selling stock to pay near-term debts, so a firm with slow-moving stock may need to delay purchases, negotiate longer credit or arrange an overdraft before the due dates arrive. Sub-topic 5.5.3 works both ratios through with figures.

## The five ratios

### Gross profit margin

The gross profit margin measures trading performance: the share of revenue left after paying for the goods themselves. It is calculated as (gross profit divided by revenue) x 100, where gross profit is revenue less the cost of the goods sold. A margin of 40% means 40 cents of every Namibian dollar of sales remains after buying what was sold - the pool out of which every other cost must be paid. The figure earns its meaning in comparison: set against last year's margin and a rival's, it becomes evidence about whether the business is buying and selling better or worse than before.

### Gross profit margin worked out

Ongelo Tailors of Windhoek had revenue of N$450 000 last year and cost of sales of N$270 000. Gross profit is 450 000 - 270 000 = 180 000 in Namibian dollars, so the margin is 180 000 divided by 450 000, which is 0.4, or 40%. The year before it was 34%.

Interpretation: the margin rose by 6 percentage points, which usually means the tailors sold at better prices or bought fabric more cheaply. Compared against the earlier year, each sale now leaves a larger share of revenue as gross profit - a wider pool from which rent, wages and ultimately profit itself must be paid.

### Profit margin

The profit margin measures the share of revenue left after every cost - the goods, wages, rent, marketing, everything - has been paid. It is calculated as (profit divided by revenue) x 100. Where the gross profit margin watches the buying and selling side, the profit margin watches the whole business, so it is usually the lower of the two figures. A margin of 8% means 8 cents of every Namibian dollar of sales remains after every cost - the share that is truly the owner's.

### Profit margin worked out

Omaruru Hardware had revenue of N$600 000, gross profit of N$360 000 and operating expenses of N$150 000. Profit is 360 000 - 150 000 = 210 000 in Namibian dollars, so the profit margin is 210 000 divided by 600 000, which is 0.35, or 35%. Its gross profit margin is 360 000 divided by 600 000, which is 0.6, or 60%.

Interpretation: the 25-percentage-point gap between 60% and 35% shows how heavily expenses bear on the trade. The owner can lift the profit margin by selling at better prices, buying stock more cheaply, or trimming expenses such as rent and wages, because each route raises the share of revenue left as profit.

### Return on Capital Employed

Return on Capital Employed (ROCE) measures how hard the long-term funds work: profit as a percentage of capital employed, calculated as (profit divided by capital employed) x 100. Capital employed here names the long-term funds invested in the business - the owner's equity plus any non-current liabilities. The word capital does three jobs in business studies, and the senses must be kept apart: money capital is money placed into the business, capital goods are machines and buildings, and capital employed is the ratio input used here.

### ROCE worked out

Swakop Coastal Tours made a profit of N$84 000 on capital employed of N$600 000. ROCE is 84 000 divided by 600 000, which is 0.14, or 14%. A bank deposit in Windhoek might pay about 8% for the same period.

Interpretation: compared against that 8%, the tour company's 14% suggests its long-term funds are working productively - the owners are earning more from the business than the money would fetch in a savings account. Read the other way, a ROCE persistently below the interest rate suggests the funds might be better placed elsewhere, which is why the figure is usually set against interest rates and against what rival firms achieve.

### Current ratio

The current ratio is a liquidity measure: current assets divided by current liabilities, normally written in a form such as 2 to 1. Current assets are inventory, trade receivables and cash; current liabilities are the debts due within a year, such as amounts owed to suppliers and an overdraft. The figure says how many Namibian dollars of current assets stand behind each Namibian dollar of short-term debt.

### Current ratio worked out

Walvis Bay Cold Storage holds current assets of N$132 000 - inventory N$60 000, receivables N$48 000, cash N$24 000 - and current liabilities of N$66 000. The current ratio is 132 000 divided by 66 000, which is 2, written 2 to 1: for each N$1 of short-term debt the business holds N$2 of current assets.

Interpretation: a figure below 1 would suggest difficulty paying debts as they fall due, while a very high figure, say 4, suggests cash and stock sitting idle instead of earning. Note that this 2 includes N$60 000 of inventory, the slowest asset to turn into cash - which is exactly why the acid test exists.

### Acid test ratio

The acid test ratio is the stricter liquidity measure: current assets less inventory, divided by current liabilities. It counts only the assets that can be turned into cash quickly - receivables and cash itself - because a payment due this month usually cannot wait for stock to be sold first.

### Acid test worked out

Etosha Gate Lodge holds current assets of N$90 000, of which inventory is N$40 000, and current liabilities of N$50 000. Current assets less inventory are 90 000 - 40 000 = 50 000 in Namibian dollars, so the acid test is 50 000 divided by 50 000, which is 1, written 1 to 1. Its current ratio, for contrast, is 90 000 divided by 50 000, which is 1.8.

Interpretation: the lodge clears the current ratio comfortably but only just meets the acid test, because much of its wealth sits in stock. A result below 1 would suggest reliance on selling inventory to pay near-term debts, so a firm with slow-moving stock may need to delay purchases, negotiate longer credit or arrange an overdraft.

### Two confusions to keep apart

Two pairs are constantly swapped. Gross profit margin and profit margin differ in what they subtract: the first takes away only the cost of the goods sold, the second takes away every expense, so a strong gross margin can sit beside a loss. The test: ask which costs were taken out before the figure was reached. Current ratio and acid test differ in one input: the first counts inventory, the second removes it, so a business can pass one and fail the other. The test: ask whether stock is inside the figure, and whether that stock could become money before the next payment date.

### Reading a set of results

Ratios earn their keep in sets. A tour operator in Queenstown, New Zealand, judging a supplier, might read a gross profit margin of 30% (weak trading), a profit margin of 4% (expenses swallow almost the whole trading profit), a ROCE of 3% (long-term funds earn little), a current ratio of 2.6 and an acid test of 1.9 (short-term debts comfortably covered). Judged on liquidity alone the supplier looks sound; judged on profitability the same business looks fragile. That is the point: profitability ratios and liquidity ratios answer different questions, and a business can be strong on one side and weak on the other.

Read the trend as well as the level - a margin that slips a little every year tells a different story from one dip in an otherwise rising line - and compare against firms in the same trade, because a 4% margin may be normal for a food retailer and thin for a jeweller. An investor choosing between businesses weighs the return figures; a lender weighing the same set looks first at the liquidity pair, so the same numbers feed different decisions.

## Users of accounts and their decisions

### Who reads the accounts

Users of accounts are the people and organisations with an interest in a business's financial results. Owners want to know what their stake is earning; managers want the detail that steers day-to-day decisions; lenders want evidence that loans will be repaid; suppliers want to know whether credit is safe to give; employees want evidence their jobs and wages are secure; government wants the taxable profit and a view of the firm's health. Each user asks a different question, so the same accounts get read for different needs - a spice importer in Istanbul deciding whether to extend credit to a customer reads the liquidity lines first, while that customer's own manager reads the expense detail first.

### What each user weighs

The needs differ, and so do the ratios each user weighs. A lender, or a supplier asked for credit, is likely to weigh the liquidity ratios - the current ratio and above all the acid test - because they show whether short-term debts can be met on time. An owner, or an investor thinking of buying in, is likely to weigh profitability - profit margin and ROCE - because these show the return the business earns on its sales and on its long-term funds. A manager reads them all: the gross profit margin to watch buying and selling, the profit margin to watch overheads, the liquidity pair to watch the payment calendar. None of these users is misreading the accounts; they are answering different questions with the same figures.

### Why ratios rather than raw figures

Raw accounts are hard to compare: one year's revenue of N$450 000 means little on its own. Ratio analysis turns the raw figures into percentages and proportions that can be set against earlier years, against other businesses in the same trade, and against the threshold a user has decided on in advance - a bank may decline to lend below an acid test of 1, whatever the profit. The comparison, not the figure itself, is what makes a result usable for a decision.

### From ratio to lending decision

Condition: a bank reads an acid test of 0.7 in a loan application. Mechanism: the figure suggests the business would rely on selling inventory to keep up repayments, so the bank expects a higher risk of missed dates. Consequence: the bank is likely to refuse the loan, or to grant it only against security and at a higher interest rate, because the extra risk has to be paid for. The same chain runs the other way: an acid test comfortably above 1, alongside a healthy profit margin, is likely to move the same decision towards approval, because repayment then looks secure on the dates as well as over the years.

### One figure is not a verdict

A single ratio for a single year is a snapshot. It may reflect a one-off event, an accounting choice or an unusual season, so users usually weigh several ratios, the trend over several years, and what they know of the business's context before deciding. An investor comparing two firms should also ask what each return depends on: a ROCE of 18% built on steady local demand may be worth more than 22% won from a passing fashion. Two users can read the same results and still decide differently, because their needs - and their tolerance of risk - differ, and that is a strength of the method rather than a flaw in it.
