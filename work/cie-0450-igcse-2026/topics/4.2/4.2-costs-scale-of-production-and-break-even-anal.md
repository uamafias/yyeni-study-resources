## Classifying costs and cost-based decisions

### Fixed and variable costs

A fixed cost does not change with the level of output: the rent on a bakery in Otjiwarongo is the same whether it bakes 200 loaves or 2 000. A manager's salary and the licence fee on its delivery van are fixed too, because nothing about this week's baking changes them. A variable cost changes directly with output: flour, yeast, packaging and the electricity for the ovens all rise and fall with the number of loaves baked.

The classification depends on what causes the cost, not on the amount. A salary is fixed because output does not drive it; the same money paid per loaf to a packaging contractor would be variable, because output drives every dollar of it. Some costs are mixed - electricity has a fixed standing charge and a variable use charge - and for simple decisions the fixed part counts as fixed and the rest as variable.

### Total and average cost

Total cost is found by adding fixed costs to total variable costs, and total variable cost is the variable cost per unit multiplied by the number of units produced. The Otjiwarongo bakery pays fixed costs of N$6 000 a month and variable costs of N$4 per loaf. At 3 000 loaves: total variable cost is 3 000 x 4 = 12 000, so total cost = N$6 000 + N$12 000 = 18 000, and average cost per loaf is 18 000 / 3 000 = 6, that is N$6. At 6 000 loaves: total variable cost is 6 000 x 4 = 24 000, so total cost = N$6 000 + N$24 000 = 30 000, and 30 000 / 6 000 = 5, that is N$5 a loaf.

The pattern is worth noticing. The N$6 000 of fixed costs is spread across twice as many loaves, so the fixed cost per loaf falls from N$2 to N$1, and average cost falls even though the variable cost per unit has not changed at all. This fall is the arithmetic behind the idea of economies of scale.

### Direct and indirect costs

A second way to classify costs cuts across the first. A direct cost can be traced to one unit of output: the flour in a loaf, the leather in one pair of Okahandja school shoes. An indirect cost is shared across everything the business makes: rent, the manager's salary, the cleaning of the whole workshop. Direct costs are usually variable, but the match is not exact - the electricity used by one oven baking one batch is direct in cause yet is usually treated as an indirect overhead, because tracing it to single loaves is not worth the effort.

The test for the direct-versus-indirect boundary: ask whether the cost would disappear if that one unit were not made. The flour would; the rent would not. For the fixed-versus-variable boundary the test is different: ask whether output drives the cost.

### Using cost data: stop production or continue?

Cost data earns its keep in simple decisions. Karibib Print Shirts makes 900 shirts a month at N$40 each. Its monthly fixed costs are N$18 000 and its variable cost is N$26 a shirt, so at 900 shirts the total variable cost is 900 x 26 = 23 400 and total cost = N$18 000 + N$23 400 = 41 400, against revenue of 900 x 40 = 36 000. The business is making a loss: revenue of N$36 000 is N$5 400 below total cost.

Should it stop? The price of N$40 is above the variable cost of N$26, so every shirt contributes N$14 towards the fixed costs the business must pay in any case. At 900 shirts the contribution is 900 x 14 = 12 600, which pays N$12 600 of the N$18 000 fixed bill; the loss is 18 000 - 12 600 = N$5 400 a month. If the business stopped but kept its lease and manager, the loss could be the full N$18 000. Continuing is therefore likely to be the better choice while the price stays above variable cost per unit, revisited if the sales contract ends.

### Connections

Cost classification feeds directly into this topic's second half: the spread of fixed costs across more units is the arithmetic behind internal economies of scale, and the fixed-cost line is the first line drawn on a break-even diagram. Cost data is a starting point rather than the last word, because it does not include future price changes or demand shifts.

## Economies and diseconomies of scale

### Economies of scale: internal and external

Economies of scale are the falls in average cost per unit that come from producing on a larger scale. They come in two forms. Internal economies of scale arise inside the growing business itself: when Etosha Breweries doubles its output, the cost of brewing each litre falls because its own fixed costs are spread wider and its own buying power grows. External economies of scale arise from the industry or the area growing around the business: a cluster of marble cutters near Karibib attracts tool suppliers, trained workers and a repair service no single cutter paid for, and every cutter's average cost falls as a result.

The two forms differ in who can influence them. Internal economies are what a business earns through its own growth, so growing firms usually chase them deliberately. External economies arrive from outside and are shared by every producer in the area, so they reward location and timing as much as any single business's effort. Either way, the fall in average cost holds only up to a point: beyond a certain size the diseconomies of scale - the growing pains of managing a large workforce - begin to outweigh the savings, and average cost starts to rise again.

### The five internal economies

Internal economies of scale are usually grouped into five parts. Purchasing economies: bigger orders win bulk discounts, so a mill buying maize by the truckload pays less per bag than a small bakery does. Marketing economies: a national advertising campaign costs roughly the same whether the business sells 10 000 units or 100 000, so the promotion cost per unit falls as sales rise. Financial economies: banks usually lend to larger businesses at lower interest, because the risk is spread across more activity, which lowers the cost of funding growth. Managerial economies: only a larger business can afford full-time specialists - a production manager, a buyer, an accountant - and each specialist's salary is spread across more output. Technical economies: larger-scale machinery, such as an automated bottling line, costs heavily up front but produces each unit far more cheaply than hand work, so its cost per unit falls as volume rises. All five deliver their saving up to a point: once a business grows too large to manage well, the diseconomies of scale begin to outweigh them and average cost rises.

### Diseconomies of scale

Diseconomies of scale are the rises in average cost per unit that come when a business grows beyond the size it can manage well. They come in the same two forms. Internal diseconomies are caused by the business's own size: poor communication, weak coordination and a lack of commitment from employees. External diseconomies are caused by the industry or area around it growing crowded - a supplier cluster that drives up local wages for everyone, for instance.

Each internal diseconomy raises cost through the workforce. When head office writes messages that reach the floor late and garbled, decisions take longer and mistakes are made twice. When departments' work is weakly coordinated, batches wait for each other and idle time is paid for. When employees feel remote from decisions made above them, commitment falls, effort falls with it, and supervision costs rise to compensate - a German car plant that outgrew its management saw exactly this, with each extra assembly hall adding more coordination staff than the last.

### Where the gains of growth stop

The full chain runs: if a business grows, its fixed costs are spread across more units and its bulk buying and specialist management lower cost per unit - the internal economies of scale - so average cost falls. Mechanism: the fall continues only while the gains outweigh the growing pains of size. Consequence: beyond a certain size, poor communication, weak coordination and falling commitment push average cost back up - internal diseconomies of scale - and the business has grown past its cheapest point.

This is why growth lowers average cost only up to a point. A Namibian grain mill scaling from one mill to three probably rides purchasing and technical economies; the same mill scaled to twelve sites with one distant head office could find its coordination costs rising faster than its bulk discounts: cost per unit falls, flattens, then rises as size outgrows management.

## Break-even analysis

### The concept of break-even

Break-even is the level of output at which total revenue exactly equals total cost, so the business makes neither profit nor loss. Below it every unit sold leaves the business worse off; above it every extra unit adds profit. Break-even output is a number of units, not an amount of money.

Contribution is the idea that powers it. Contribution per unit is the selling price minus the variable cost per unit - the amount each unit sold contributes towards paying the fixed costs. When enough units have been sold for their combined contribution to cover fixed costs in total, the business has reached break-even; each unit after that adds its contribution to profit. A hairdresser in Tsumeb charging N$80 a cut with variable costs of N$20 per customer contributes N$60 per cut towards the salon's fixed costs - the N$80 price less the N$20 of products and power gives N$60 of contribution.

Knowing the break-even level matters before anything is sold: it tells the founders how many customers the salon must find each month before the business pays for itself, so the figure can be compared with the customers the town could realistically supply. A business whose break-even sits far above the customers available has a problem that no amount of effort behind the chair can fix, which is why the concept is checked early in any business plan rather than after the losses appear.

### The break-even formula

Break-even output is found using the formula fixed costs divided by contribution per unit, where contribution per unit is the selling price minus the variable cost per unit. Written out: break-even output = fixed costs / (price - variable cost per unit).

Worked example. Ondangwa Canvas Bags has fixed costs of N$36 000, sells each bag for N$90 and pays N$54 variable cost per bag. Contribution per unit: 90 - 54 = 36, that is N$36. Break-even output: 36 000 / 36 = 1 000 bags. At 1 000 bags the revenue is 1 000 x 90 = 90 000 and the total cost is the N$36 000 of fixed costs plus 1 000 x 54 = 54 000 of variable cost, giving 36 000 + 54 000 = 90 000 - revenue equals total cost, which is exactly what break-even means.

### Constructing, completing or amending a break-even diagram

A break-even diagram is drawn with output on the horizontal axis and money on the vertical axis. Constructing one follows the same steps whatever the figures. Step 1: draw the fixed-cost line - horizontal, because fixed costs do not change with output. Step 2: build the total-cost line from it - at each output, total cost is the fixed costs plus the variable cost of that output, so the line starts on the vertical axis at the level of fixed costs and rises with every unit made. Step 3: draw the total-revenue line from the origin, rising by the price for each unit. Step 4: mark the crossing point of the revenue line with the total-cost line - the break-even output sits directly below it on the horizontal axis.

Completing or amending a partly-drawn diagram uses the same four steps. To amend for a higher price, redraw the revenue line steeper - it still starts at the origin but rises by the new price per unit - and read the new crossing against the total-cost line. To amend for higher fixed costs, lift the fixed-cost line and redraw the total-cost line above it at every output; the crossing moves right. A table of revenue and total cost at several outputs is the safest way to place the lines: for Ondangwa Canvas Bags, at 500 bags revenue is 500 x 90 = 45 000 against total cost of 36 000 + 27 000 = 63 000, and at 1 500 bags revenue is 1 500 x 90 = 135 000 against total cost of 36 000 + 81 000 = 117 000 - the crossing sits between them, at 1 000 bags. Amending is not a new skill: each amendment changes one line only, so the safest routine is to ask which line the change touches - price touches the revenue line, variable cost and fixed costs touch the cost lines - then redraw that line and read the crossing afresh.

### Calculating break-even output

The calculation runs in three moves: find contribution per unit, then divide fixed costs by it, then state the answer in units. A second example with a profit target: Swakop Salt Co has fixed costs of N$90 000, sells each 10 kg bag for N$150 and pays N$90 variable cost per bag. Contribution per unit: 150 - 90 = 60, that is N$60. Break-even output: 90 000 / 60 = 1 500 bags. For a profit target of N$30 000, the units needed are (90 000 + 30 000) / 60 = 2 000 bags - the fixed costs plus the target profit, all divided by contribution per unit.

Reading the answer matters as much as reaching it. Break-even output of 1 500 bags means the business makes a loss at any output below 1 500 and a profit at any output above it, so what the calculation supplies is the threshold the business's sales must pass, not a forecast that they will. And the answer moves predictably when the inputs move: higher fixed costs raise it, a higher price lowers it, and a higher variable cost per unit raises it, provided nothing else changes - which is what makes the calculation reusable for decisions.

### The margin of safety

The margin of safety is the amount by which current output exceeds break-even output; it is measured in units of output. It is calculated as current output minus break-even output, and it can also be expressed as a percentage of current output. What it measures is protection: how far sales could fall before losses begin.

Ondangwa Canvas Bags sells 1 400 bags a month against a break-even output of 1 000: the margin of safety is 1 400 - 1 000 = 400 bags, which is 400 / 1 400 = 29 per cent of current output (0.29 rounded to two places). A wide margin protects the business from a demand fall, because 400 bags' worth of custom could be lost before losses began; a narrow one means a small fall in demand could push the business below break-even. Comparing the two businesses' margins is usually more informative than either number alone: a margin of 400 bags on 1 400 sold is comfortable, but a margin of 400 bags on 2 000 sold would be thinner protection against the same market.

### Interpreting a diagram and analysing the situation

Interpreting a break-even diagram means comparing what the lines show at the business's current output. The vertical gap between the revenue line and the total-cost line at that output is the profit or loss there; the horizontal gap between the break-even point and current output is the margin of safety. Below the crossing, the cost line sits above the revenue line and the vertical gap is a loss; above it, revenue sits above cost and the gap is profit.

The analysis is what the diagram says about the situation. A crossing close to current output signals little protection: a modest demand fall erases the profit and opens losses. A crossing far below current output signals a business that could lose many sales before losses begin. When a diagram is amended - a steeper revenue line after a price rise, a lifted total-cost line after new fixed costs - the meaning is read the same way: the new crossing says where the business's protection now sits, against which the current sales level must be judged.

Read with figures it is quicker still. On the Ondangwa Canvas Bags diagram, at 1 400 bags the revenue line stands at 1 400 x 90 = 126 000 while the total-cost line stands at 36 000 + 75 600 = 111 600, so the vertical gap between them is N$14 400 of profit - the same figure the margin of safety of 400 bags gives when multiplied by the N$36 contribution per bag. The diagram and the arithmetic must agree, because they are two readings of the same business.

### Using break-even analysis for decisions

Break-even analysis supports simple decisions because the effect of any change is a new calculation. Condition: if a business raises its price, with variable cost per unit and fixed costs unchanged. Mechanism: contribution per unit rises, so fixed costs are covered by fewer sales and break-even output falls. Consequence: the margin of safety widens at the same sales level - but only if the higher price does not cost too many sales, because the analysis itself says nothing about how demand responds to the new price.

Other changes run through the same arithmetic. Higher variable costs per unit shrink contribution, so break-even output rises. Higher fixed costs raise it directly. A business can also run the analysis backwards from a target: units needed for a given profit are fixed costs plus that profit, divided by contribution per unit. Used this way the analysis is a decision aid - it shows the threshold each choice must pass - rather than a forecast of the sales themselves. Run with figures: a business selling at N$60 against N$36 of variable cost, with fixed costs of N$24 000, breaks even at 24 000 / 24 = 1 000 units; raise the price to N$66 and contribution becomes 66 - 36 = 30, so the threshold falls to 24 000 / 30 = 800 units - the higher price lowers the threshold, provided the sales hold.

### The limitations of break-even analysis

Break-even analysis has real limitations. It assumes the price and the variable cost per unit stay the same at every output - but bulk discounts for the business's own buying and price cuts to shift larger volumes are both normal. It assumes fixed costs do not change as output rises - true over a small range, false once a second workshop or shift is needed. And it assumes everything produced is sold, with no inventory left over. Growth itself is part of the limit: internal economies of scale lower average cost as output expands, but only up to a point - once size outruns management, the diseconomies of scale push costs back up, and the straight lines on the analysis stop describing the business.

The deepest limitation is that the analysis assumes away demand: it finds the output needed to break even but says nothing about whether customers will buy that many. A lower break-even output does not by itself mean the business is safe, because if demand at the current price is below break-even output the business makes a loss however low the crossing sits. The diagram is also most dependable close to the output it was drawn for: further away, costs and prices usually change as the business grows, so the straight lines stop describing it. Used with those cautions, it remains a fast threshold calculation; used without them it can mislead.

The assumptions fail in ordinary ways. A retailer that cuts its price by 10 per cent to win volume changes the slope of its revenue line halfway through the year; a workshop whose rent rises mid-year lifts its whole cost line; a furniture maker that builds 400 chairs and sells 380 has revenue the line never drew. Each mismatch is small on its own, and together they mean the diagram is an estimate whose crossings should be re-checked whenever the figures move.
