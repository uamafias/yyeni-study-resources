## The main features of different forms of business organisation

### Sole traders and partnerships

A sole trader is a business owned and controlled by one person. The owner takes every decision, keeps the whole profit after tax, and is personally responsible for every debt the business takes on. Setting up is quick and needs little paperwork, which is why this form is common for small shops, repair trades and one-person services.

A partnership is a business owned jointly by two or more people. The partners share the decisions, the profits and responsibility for debts, and the split between them is usually set out in a partnership agreement. A partnership can raise more capital - the money invested in the business - than one person alone, because several pockets stand behind it, and a partner with skills the founder lacks can be admitted without forming a company.

Two examples. Ketil's Seafood, a Norwegian family firm, began when Ketil filleted and packed salmon on his own quay: one owner, one set of decisions, one wallet. In Windhoek, a repair workshop run by two cousins who share tools, customers and the rent is a partnership: the work, the risk and the reward spread across more than one owner.

### Private and public limited companies

A limited company is a business with its own legal identity, owned by shareholders who buy shares in it. Because the company exists separately from its owners, its shareholders risk the money they paid for their shares and their personal possessions are protected; liability is limited.

The two forms of the limited company differ in who may buy the shares. In a private limited company the shares move with the founders' approval and are not offered to the general public, so control tends to stay inside a small circle of family, friends and chosen investors. In a public limited company shares may be sold to the general public on a stock exchange, which can raise very large sums and spreads ownership across many shareholders, at the price of publishing results and answering to outside investors.

A Windhoek logistics firm whose shares are held by its founder and four neighbours is a private limited company; a German engineering group whose shares anyone may buy on the Frankfurt exchange is a public limited company.

### Franchises and joint ventures

A franchise is an arrangement in which a business that owns a trading name and a proven way of running it - the franchisor - sells the right to open an outlet under that name to a franchisee. The franchisee pays an initial fee and usually a continuing share of sales, follows the franchisor's rules on products, prices and layout, and in return trades under a brand customers already know, with training and support behind it. A Danish bakery chain that franchises every shop across Northern Europe is an international example: local owners bake to the chain's recipes while the head office collects fees and guards the brand.

A joint venture is a separate project or business formed when two or more businesses agree to work together on it. Each contributes resources - money, staff, equipment or expertise - and each shares the risks and the rewards of that one venture, while the parent businesses stay independent. A Namibian construction firm and an Indian road-building company might form a joint venture to deliver one highway section together: when the section is handed over, the venture ends and each firm carries on alone.

### Unincorporated businesses and companies

The law sorts these forms into two camps. An unincorporated business - a sole trader or a partnership - has no legal identity of its own: in law, the owner and the business are the same person, so the business's contracts, property and debts belong to its owners personally. A company is incorporated: it exists in law as a separate legal entity that can own property, sign contracts, sue and be sued in its own name, and keep existing when its owners sell up, retire or die.

The consequences reach past paperwork. Ownership of an incorporated company sits in shares that change hands without winding the business up, which gives it continuity an unincorporated form lacks. Risk and liability follow the same line: a company's debts belong to the company, while an unincorporated business's debts belong to its owners, out of their own pockets if the business cannot pay.

### Risk, ownership and liability

Three ideas run through every choice of business form. Business risk is the chance that the business performs worse than its owners expect - sales too low, costs too high - so that some or all of the money invested in it is lost. Ownership means holding the business and controlling its decisions: the sole trader owns and decides alone, the partners own jointly, shareholders own through shares and vote at meetings, and the state owns a public corporation.

Liability is the rule about who pays the business's debts when it cannot. With unlimited liability, which applies to sole traders and partners, the owner is personally responsible for all the business's debts, so a supplier who is owed money can claim the owner's car, house or savings. With limited liability, which applies to shareholders in a company, an owner's responsibility stops at the amount paid for the shares, so personal possessions are protected.

A bakery in Katutura run as a sole trader closes owing a supplier N$40 000; the owner must pay it out of personal savings. Had the bakery been a private limited company, the supplier could claim the company's assets and no further.

### How the forms differ

Set side by side, the forms differ along the same few lines. Ownership: one person, a few partners, or a crowd of shareholders. Liability: unlimited for the sole trader and the partners, limited for company shareholders. Capital, the money invested in the business: one wallet, several wallets, or a share issue - and a public limited company can raise more than a private one, because it may sell shares to the general public. Continuity: an incorporated company survives a change of owner, while a sole trader's business ends with its owner.

A franchise cuts across those lines rather than sitting beside them: the outlet's owner is the franchisee, but the brand and the system stay with the franchisor, so control is shared by contract. A joint venture shares ownership of one project between businesses that stay independent everywhere else. Public corporations sit outside the private-sector list altogether, owned by the state rather than by individuals.

### Choosing a form in practice

A recommendation about business organisation is made by matching forms to a situation, not by ranking forms in the abstract. A worked example. Two Windhoek graphic designers run a studio as a partnership. Orders have outgrown their two desks; serving the new clients needs N$300 000 of equipment and staff, and a single failed project could cost N$120 000 in refunds.

The criteria that matter here: risk - as partners they face unlimited liability, so their homes stand behind the N$120 000 of downside; capital - a bank is likely to lend around N$100 000 against two partners' security, and a private limited company could raise the rest by selling shares to a chosen investor; control - a shareholder with a large enough stake could outvote them together unless a shareholders' agreement reserves major decisions to the founders; continuity - a company keeps going if one founder leaves, which a partnership agreement must work hard to imitate.

The reasoned recommendation: form a private limited company, sell at most 30 per cent of the shares to one trusted investor, and keep a shareholders' agreement protecting the founders' control. The counterargument is real - they give up part of the profit and some privacy about results, and a misjudged investor brings day-to-day friction. The condition decides it: with N$120 000 of downside and homes at stake, limited liability is likely to be worth more than the share of profit sold, provided the stake stays small.

### The public sector and public corporations

Not every business organisation belongs to the private sector. A public corporation is a business organisation in the public sector, owned by the state, set up by the government to provide a service the nation relies on - water, electricity, railways, broadcasting. Its features: the government owns it and appoints its board; it is financed by state funds as well as its own revenue; it pursues service and national aims alongside any surplus it earns; and it often holds the sole right to supply its service, so it does not face the competition a private firm does. A national water supplier or a state railway corporation are the shapes learners meet in questions.

Why governments choose this form: where a service is essential but unprofitable under private ownership - rural pipelines, off-peak trains - the state can absorb losses and direct investment toward national goals. The limits sit beside the aims: without commercial pressure a public corporation can drift into inefficiency, and its losses fall on taxpayers rather than shareholders.

### Two confusions to keep apart

An entrepreneur and a sole trader are different ideas. An entrepreneur is a person who spots an opportunity, starts a venture and bears its risk - the word describes behaviour, not a legal form. A sole trader is one legal form such a person may choose; the same entrepreneur might instead found a company or run a franchised outlet. The test: ask whether the statement describes a person or a structure.

Unlimited and limited liability are opposites, and the boundary is what the owner loses. Unlimited liability reaches personal possessions, because the owner and the business are legally the same person; limited liability stops at the money paid for shares. The test: ask who pays the unpaid debts, and out of which pocket.

### In short

Sole traders and partnerships are unincorporated: their owners are the business, with unlimited liability and full control. Private and public limited companies are separate legal persons whose shareholders enjoy limited liability, at the cost of sharing ownership and results. Franchises buy a proven system; joint ventures share one project between independent businesses. Public corporations put an essential service in the state's hands. Recommending a form means matching those features against the owners' situation: liability, capital, control and continuity.
