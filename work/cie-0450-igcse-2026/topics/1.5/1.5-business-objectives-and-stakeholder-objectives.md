## Business objectives and social enterprises

### What a business objective is

A business objective is a target a business sets itself to reach within a period, such as a year. Objectives differ from wishes because they are stated in advance and the business can be judged against them: "keep trading through the low season" and "reach a profit of N$200 000 this year" are objectives, while "do well" is not, because nothing can be measured against it.

Most businesses hold several objectives at the same time. A bakery can aim to survive its first year, to make a profit and to win a bigger share of the bread market together, because those targets can support each other - though, as later sections show, they can also pull against each other.

### Why objectives matter

A business without objectives drifts: decisions get made by habit or by whoever argues hardest, and nobody can tell whether the year went well. Setting objectives matters for three practical reasons.

First, direction. An objective gives the owner and the workers one stated target to aim at, which guides everyday decisions - whether to spend on a new oven or hold money back, whether to chase tourist customers or local ones.

Second, measurement. A target such as "sell 4 000 loaves a month" lets the owner check progress and act when results fall short; a business that cannot measure progress cannot correct it.

Third, coordination and motivation. When everyone knows the target, the team pulls in the same direction, and reaching a stated objective gives the workforce something to celebrate.

### Objectives change with circumstances

Objectives are not fixed for life. A new business is likely to put survival first; once established, profit or market share usually moves to the front; a business whose sales are falling may return to survival. Circumstances change the order: in a recession customers spend less, and a business that keeps a growth target while its sales fall is aiming at the wrong thing. A sensible owner reviews objectives each year and reorders them to fit the situation the business is actually in.

### The main objectives: survival, growth, profit and market share

Survival means the business keeps trading. New businesses put it first because failure is most common in the early years, and an established business returns to it when sales fall. Survival matters because a business that stops trading achieves nothing else.

Profit is revenue less total costs over a period. It is the return owners get for the risk they took, and it funds what the business does next. Profit is not the same as cash: a profitable business can still run short of the cash it needs to pay suppliers this month, which is why a survival target is often about cash-flow, not just profit.

Growth means the business becomes larger - more sales, more branches, more products. Growth is internal when the business expands from its own resources, such as a taxi firm adding vehicles, and external when it joins with another business, such as a lodge buying a rival. A growing business usually gains market share.

Market share is the proportion of the market's total sales that one business makes. Here "the market" means the customers, actual and potential, for the product - not a physical place - so a bakery with a 20 per cent share sells one loaf in every five that the market buys.

### A bakery that reorders its objectives

Consider Osha Bakers, opened in Otjiwarongo in January. For its first year the owner sets one objective: survive to December with the business still trading. Every decision is tested against it - she keeps prices low to win regulars, spends little on advertising, and holds three months of costs in reserve. The business comes through its first low season still trading.

In its second year she reorders: the objectives become a profit of N$120 000 and, after that, a bigger share of the town's bread market. The survival year was not a failure because profit was small; judged against its own objective, the business succeeded. The example shows why the target must fit the situation: a profit objective in year one would have pushed her to raise prices and spend on marketing, which might have ended the business instead.

### Social enterprises

A social enterprise is a business with social aims that uses business activity - making and selling goods or services - to achieve them. Unlike a charity, it funds itself mainly by trading rather than by donations.

Social enterprises typically pursue objectives on three fronts together. Social objectives help people or a community, such as training unemployed young people or supplying affordable bread to a low-income area. Environmental objectives protect the natural world, such as using recycled packaging. Financial objectives keep the business itself sound: it needs revenue above costs, because a social enterprise that runs out of money stops helping anyone. Profit, for a social enterprise, is usually a means to keep the social aim running rather than the aim itself.

### When social aims and profit pull apart

The three fronts do not always point the same way, and the tension is the heart of running a social enterprise.

Suppose a social enterprise bakery in Katutura funds free training from its bread sales. A supermarket offers a large order at a price that would squeeze the training fund. The social objective argues for keeping the prices that fund training; the financial objective argues for the security a big, reliable customer brings. The manager must trade one against the other - accept the order and find a cheaper training model, or refuse it, keep the social aim intact, and accept slower growth. A London bicycle workshop that trains young mechanics faces the same choice with institutional contracts: a bigger contract means more trainees but thinner margins, and the manager has to decide where the balance sits.

### Objective or reason?

A common slip confuses the objective with the case for having one. When a question asks what an objective is, name the target - survival, profit, a share of sales. When it asks why objectives matter, give the reasons - direction, measurement, coordination. "Objectives help a business decide" is an explanation of importance, not an objective, and a learner who writes it in answer to a question asking for an objective has answered the wrong question, even though the sentence is true. The test: ask whether the statement names a target the business could reach, or a reason for setting targets.

## Stakeholders and their objectives

### Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any person or group with an interest in what a business does - anyone affected by its decisions, for better or worse. A lodge's stakeholders include its owners, its staff, the guests, the vegetable supplier, the bank that lent it money, the government that taxes it and the village that lives beside it. The word covers groups inside and outside the business, which is where the internal and external split begins.

### Internal and external stakeholders

Internal stakeholders are the people inside the business: its workers, its managers and its owners. External stakeholders are everyone with an interest from outside: customers, suppliers, lenders such as banks, the government and the wider community. The boundary is employment and ownership. A driver at a Windhoek courier firm is internal because the firm employs him; a regular customer is external because her link is a purchase, not a job. Owners are internal even when they never visit, because the business is theirs.

One caution: "internal" does not mean "more important". Both sides can decide a business's fate - workers can quit, customers can leave - so a manager has to weigh them all.

### What internal stakeholders want

Each internal group holds objectives that follow from its own position. Workers want fair pay, safe conditions, job security and work that is not unbearable - their interest is the living the business provides. Managers add objectives of their own: a rising career, a salary tied to results, and the standing that comes from running a growing department. Owners want the return on the money they put in: profit, and growth in what the business is worth. When the business does well, these aims usually move together; when it does badly, they pull apart, which is where conflict begins.

### What external stakeholders want

External groups want outcomes that match their own interest. Customers want quality, fair prices and reliable supply. Suppliers want a customer who pays on time and orders again. Lenders want the loan repaid with interest - a bank is likely to watch a business closely once its profits shrink, because the bank's money is at risk in it. Government wants the taxes, the jobs and the legal behaviour the country runs on. The community wants the jobs and the spending a business brings without the noise, the traffic or the pollution. A German carmaker felt the weight of that last objective when a town's objections to its test-track plans forced a redesign: neighbours are stakeholders too, and their objectives can hold up a project for years.

### One decision, many stakeholder effects

Most decisions a business makes help some stakeholders and work against others. Take a lodge that raises its room prices by 15 per cent.

The owner gains: revenue per room rises, which supports her profit objective. Guests pay more for the same stay, which works against their objective of value, and some may book elsewhere. Workers neither gain nor lose directly, but if the rise drives guests away and the lodge runs short of work, their security is threatened later. The village that sells crafts to guests gains if wealthier visitors spend more.

The manager cannot make the decision "good for stakeholders" in general; she has to judge which groups the business most depends on - and that judgement is what makes stakeholder conflict a live question rather than a definition.

### A wage claim at Tulonga

Tulonga Farm Supplies in Grootfontein made a profit of N$150 000 last year. Its 25 workers have asked for a 10 per cent wage rise, which would add about N$108 000 to yearly costs.

The workers' objective is fair pay, and the rise is small next to the whole wage bill. The owners' objective is profit, and N$108 000 is most of the N$150 000 they earned, so the two objectives cannot both be fully met. This is a stakeholder conflict: the objectives of the workers and the owners clash over one decision.

settlement exists - a staged rise of 5 per cent now and a further 5 per cent if sales grow, for example - because the objectives of the workers and the owners are not strictly incompatible. What no settlement can do is satisfy both groups in full this year; the owners pay, the workers wait, or both meet in the middle.

### Weighing and compromise

When stakeholder objectives conflict, the business has to weigh them. The usual starting points are: which groups can hurt the business most if they withdraw - workers who strike, customers who leave, a lender who calls in a loan; which objectives the law protects, such as a minimum wage; and how long each group can be kept waiting. Compromise is the normal result: a rise smaller than demanded, a price increase with a loyalty discount for regulars, a cut in hours instead of jobs. The judgement does not end, because the next decision starts the weighing again.

### Whose benefit?

Answers about stakeholder effects often say "this benefits the business" - but a business is not a person, and the phrase hides who gains. Profit goes to owners; a bigger wage bill goes to workers; lower prices go to customers. The discipline is to name the group: "the price rise helps the owners' profit objective but works against customers' objective of low cost" says something that can be checked against each group, while "it is good for the business" leaves the beneficiary unnamed.

## Private sector and public sector objectives

### Private and public sector objectives

Private sector businesses are owned by individuals or shareholders; public sector enterprises are owned and controlled by the state. Ownership drives the objectives. A private owner keeps the profit, so private businesses are led by profit, survival and growth - though a private business may add social aims, as social enterprises do. The public owner is the state, answerable to the public, so a public enterprise is judged on service: supplying water or transport to every town including remote ones a private firm would find unprofitable, keeping prices affordable, and covering its costs rather than maximising profit. A public enterprise may still earn a surplus from its trading, but the surplus is a means to keep the service running, not the measure of success.

### Two water businesses

Compare two ways of supplying water to a Namibian town. Kalahari Springs is a private company: it bottles water, sells it where the margin is best, and measures itself on profit and growth. The town's public water utility answers to the state: it must pipe water to every household, including distant settlements where each connection costs far more than it earns, and it is judged on whether the water flows and the price stays affordable.

The utility's objective - a reliable service for all - comes from who owns it; so does the bottler's objective of profit. The same difference appears with the French state railway, which keeps loss-making rural routes running because access, not profit, is its objective.
