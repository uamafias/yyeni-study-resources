## The business cycle, employment, inflation and GDP

### The business cycle and its stages

The business cycle is the repeated rise and fall in the total output of an economy over time. Economies do not move ahead steadily: output climbs for a period, peaks, falls back and bottoms out, and then the pattern runs again.

The four stages follow in order. In growth, output recovers from its last low point and rises, and businesses typically see orders strengthen and hiring pick up. In boom, output sits at its highest level; demand is strong and unemployment low, though wages and prices often climb too. In recession, output falls for a period; orders weaken and businesses cut back. In slump, output bottoms out at its lowest point; demand is weak and unemployment high. The cycle then turns: recovery begins the growth stage again, because the stages repeat rather than settle into one.

### One business through the cycle

Track Etosha Edge Tours, a safari lodge outside Tsumeb, through one turn of the cycle. In growth, families regain confidence and bookings rise, so the lodge adds two guides. In boom, the lodge is full every night and room rates stand at their highest. In recession, visitors grow careful, bookings fall, and the manager stops replacing guides who leave. In slump, the lodge runs at a fraction of its capacity. The lesson for planning is that the stages follow one another: a slump is followed by growth, so the lodge keeps its marketing and its core staff where it can, ready for the turn.

A European example shows the same rhythm: a German machine-tool maker hires extra assembly workers on boom contracts and releases them in the recessions that arrive with the same pattern.

### Employment, inflation and GDP

Three measures move with the cycle and tell a business what conditions it faces.

Employment levels: when employment rises, more households earn wages, so consumer spending tends to rise and the demand reaching businesses usually lifts with it. When employment falls, the reverse presses on sales.

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, so each unit of money buys less. For a business, inflation usually means bought-in materials and wage bills rising, and unless the increase can be passed on through higher prices, the margin between cost and price is squeezed.

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) measures the total value of the goods and services an economy produces in a year. Rising GDP usually signals rising incomes and stronger demand - growth or boom conditions - while falling GDP usually signals the reverse, a recession. A Namibian retailer watching GDP forecasts is watching its own future sales.

### How inflation squeezes a business

Follow one chain through a bakery. Inflation rises, at 5 per cent a year say. The flour supplier raises its price; the bakery's wage bill rises as workers ask for increases that keep pace. The cost of each loaf climbs, so the margin on every loaf narrows unless the bakery lifts its own price. If it does lift the price, some customers may switch to a cheaper bread, so volume may fall as well. Inflation therefore presses on a business from the cost side and the demand side at once, which is why businesses watch it closely.

### Recession and inflation are not the same

A common slip treats recession and inflation as one idea. They are different conditions, and the test that tells them apart is what is moving: a recession is total output falling over time, while inflation is the general price level rising. An economy can have one without the other - prices can keep rising while output falls, and output can rise while prices stay stable. A learner who writes "recession means prices fall" has confused the two; in fact the price level often keeps rising through a recession.

## Government objectives, taxes, spending, interest rates and business responses

### Government economic objectives

Governments typically pursue three economic objectives together: rising GDP, so that output and incomes grow; low unemployment, so that people who want work can find it; and low, stable inflation, so that money keeps its value and households can plan.

The objectives support each other in ordinary times: rising GDP tends to create jobs and tax revenue, while low inflation and low unemployment help households spend with confidence. A government announcing a budget is usually announcing moves toward these three - a tax change, a spending programme, or a signal about interest rates.

### Taxes and government spending

Two of the government's levers are taxes and its own spending.

Taxes come in two broad forms. An indirect tax is a charge on spending - fuel duty, sales tax on a restaurant bill - collected by the seller as part of the price. A direct tax is a charge on income or profits, such as income tax on wages. Both reach businesses: an indirect tax rise pushes the price of the taxed item up, which tends to reduce the quantity demanded and can leave affected businesses with lower sales; an income tax rise lowers the disposable income households have left, so spending on many consumer goods tends to fall.

Government spending is the money the state pays out on the goods, services and projects it provides - roads, schools, public works. A rise in spending tends to raise the demand reaching the businesses that supply those projects, and the workers the projects pay then spend their wages in the wider economy.

### One change traced

Trace an income tax rise. Households keep less of each pay cheque, so disposable income falls. Families postpone the purchases they can delay - a new lounge suite, a weekend away. Retailers see fewer customers and slower sales, so their revenue falls, and some respond by reducing orders to their own suppliers, passing the weakness upstream. One budget decision has reached from the pay cheque to a furniture workshop in Oshakati.

The same chain runs in reverse: Rehholtz Mobelhaus, a German furniture retailer, sees customers time large purchases to land after an announced tax cut, so its order book dips for a season before it lifts.

### Interest rates

An interest rate is the cost of borrowing money, charged as a percentage of the loan for each period of the loan, and at the same time the reward a saver receives for keeping money deposited.

When interest rates rise, two doors swing at once. For the business itself, borrowing becomes more expensive: a planned loan for new equipment carries higher repayments, so many businesses postpone investment and hiring. For its customers, saving looks better and borrowing dearer, so demand for expensive goods usually bought on credit - vehicles, furniture, fitted kitchens - often falls. A rate rise therefore presses on a business through its own loan costs and through its customers' pockets. When rates fall, both doors swing the other way and demand for credit-bought goods tends to recover.

### A rate rise at the car dealership

Ongoma Car Sales in Ongwediva sells most of its vehicles on monthly finance. The central bank raises rates from 8 per cent to 11 per cent. Within weeks, buyers who had placed deposits ask about longer loan terms, and the showroom empties at weekends: the repayments quoted on the forecourt have risen with the rate, so a buyer who could afford a pick-up now qualifies for a smaller one.

What can the dealership do? Hold prices and accept slower sales; push cheaper models with smaller loans; or trim its own running costs and wait for the turn. The choice depends on its objective - profit this year, or a stronger sales base for after the rate rise passes.

### Interest rates are not taxes

The two instruments get confused because both take money and both move with government policy. They are different things: an interest rate is the price of borrowing, charged by whoever lends the money, while a tax is a charge on income, spending or profits owed to the state. A rate rise makes a loan dearer; a tax rise takes a slice of what is earned or spent. The test that tells them apart: ask who charges it and on what - borrowed money, or an activity being taxed.

### How businesses respond

A business cannot set interest rates, taxes or the course of the economy, but it chooses its own response. The main elements of the response are prices, output, staffing plans, borrowing and marketing.

Prices: hold them steady to protect volume, trim them to win cautious customers, or lift them to cover cost increases - each choice fits a different situation. Output: produce less when demand weakens, so unsold stock does not pile up, and produce more when demand strengthens. Staffing plans: hire as orders build; freeze hiring or shorten hours when they fall. Borrowing: postpone new loans while rates are high, and bring them forward when rates fall. Marketing: promote harder in weak conditions to defend the sales base.

The right mix depends on the business's objective and situation. A business aiming for profit may respond to weaker demand by cutting its costs and conserving resources; one aiming to grow its market share may hold prices and accept a lower margin for a time, keeping customers it would be costly to win back.

### Choosing a response

When an economic change arrives, three questions order the response. First, what changed and for whom - a tax on spending, a rate rise on borrowing, a spending programme nearby? Second, how does it reach this business - through its costs, through its customers' incomes, or through its loans? Third, what is the objective - profit this year, or a stronger position when conditions turn? A response chosen against those questions is likely to fit the situation; one copied from a different business may not, because its customers, its costs and its loans sit in different places. The one thing a business cannot do is ignore the change and carry on as before: every economic shift reaches someone's order book eventually, and the business that has thought about which lever to move is the one that moves it in time.
