## Needs, wants, specialisation, purpose and added value

### Needs and wants

A need is a good or service a person must have to survive: food, water, shelter, basic clothing. A want is anything a person would like to have but can live without - a phone, a trip to Swakopmund, a second pair of shoes. Wants are effectively unlimited, while the resources to satisfy them are not.

Both needs and wants are met by goods and services. A good is a physical thing you can touch, such as bread or a brick. A service is work done for you, such as a haircut or a taxi ride across Windhoek. The difference between a need and a want is about survival, not about importance: a young musician in Katutura may want a guitar very badly, but she can survive without it, so the guitar is a want.

### Scarcity and opportunity cost

Scarcity means there are not enough resources to produce everything people want. The resources an economy can call on - land, labour, capital and enterprise - are limited in Namibia as everywhere else. Because resources are scarce, every choice about how to use them has an opportunity cost: the next-best alternative given up when the choice is made.

Suppose a school in Opuwo has N$20 000 spare and wants both a water tank and a set of extra textbooks. If it buys the tank, the textbooks are the opportunity cost of that decision. A person choosing between a course fee and a new phone, a business choosing between a delivery van and a second machine, and a government choosing between a road and a clinic all face the same logic. Scarcity forces choices, and a choice means the alternative is given up.

### Specialisation and the division of labour

Specialisation is when a worker, a business or a whole country concentrates on producing a limited range of goods or services instead of trying to make everything. A Karas lamb farmer specialises in meat; a Windhoek dental clinic offers one service; Namibia as a country specialises in mining and agriculture for export.

The division of labour is specialisation applied to the stages of one product: each worker carries out one narrow step of the work instead of the whole job. On a juice line at a Tsumeb bottler, one worker washes fruit, one fills bottles, one caps them and one packs crates. Specialisation is the broader idea - it can happen inside a business, between businesses or between countries - while the division of labour describes one specific form of it, at the level of the individual worker on one product's stages. The test that tells them apart: ask whether the change concerns the range of things produced (specialisation) or how the stages of one product are shared between workers (division of labour).

### Why specialisation matters

Specialisation tends to raise output per worker. Someone who caps bottles all day caps faster and makes fewer mistakes than someone who builds whole bottles from fruit to crate, because steady practice at one task builds speed and skill. When each worker takes one stage, the line as a whole produces more per day, so the cost of producing each bottle tends to fall and the business can price more competitively.

Specialisation also underpins exchange. Because each producer makes a narrow range of products, people swap their output through trade to obtain everything else, so consumers end up with far more variety than any of them could self-produce. This is why businesses exist as organisations: they are the vehicles through which specialised producers reach the people who want their output.

The costs sit alongside the gains. Narrow, repetitive work can bore workers and lower motivation, so some may leave. If a single specialist is off sick, a whole stage may stop until a replacement is trained. Sensible managers weigh the output gain against these human and operational costs, and many respond by rotating workers between stages or enriching the jobs, keeping most of the gain while limiting the harm.

### The purpose of business activity

Business activity exists to turn scarce resources into the goods and services that satisfy people's needs and wants. A business identifies what people need or want, organises land, labour, capital and enterprise to produce it, and exchanges the output for money. In doing so it creates jobs and wages for its workers, and it aims to earn profit for its owners by adding more value than the business spends.

Nobody in an economy makes everything they consume. The joiner, the farmer and the nurse each specialise and swap their output, and through that exchange everyone consumes far more than self-sufficiency would allow. Business activity is the organised form of that specialisation and exchange: it is how an economy gets its scarce resources to the needs and wants of the people who have them.

### Adding value

Added value is the difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials and other inputs used to make it. Omuthitu Potters in northern Namibia buys clay, glaze and firewood for N$40 per pot and sells each finished pot for N$180. Added value per pot: the N$180 selling price less the N$40 of inputs gives N$140.

That N$140 is not profit. It must first pay the potters' wages, the workshop rent, transport and every other business cost; whatever remains after all of those is profit. Added value measures the worth the business created by transforming the inputs; profit measures what is left once the whole cost of running the business has been paid. This is the distinction exam questions reach for when they ask why a business with high added value can still fail.

Value is added in more ways than physical transformation. A Norwegian jam maker buys berries and sugar cheaply in season, cooks them, and sells jars under a name customers trust for far more than the fruit cost - quality and brand do part of the work. A business increases added value along two broad routes: raise the price customers accept, through better quality, design, branding, convenience or service; or cut the cost of the bought-in inputs, perhaps by negotiating with suppliers or wasting less material. Cutting the selling price by as much as the input saving adds nothing to the gap between the two.

### Two confusions to keep apart

Added value is not profit, and specialisation is not the division of labour. Added value subtracts only the bought-in inputs; profit subtracts every cost of the business, so a potter with N$140 of added value per pot can still make a loss once wages and rent are paid. The test: ask which costs were taken away before the figure was reached. Specialisation covers any concentration on a limited range - a worker, a business or a country - while the division of labour is the specific arrangement in which each worker performs one stage of one product. The test: ask whether the statement is about what range is produced or about who does which stage.
