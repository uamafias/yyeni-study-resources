## The main classifications of assets and liabilities

### Assets: what the business owns

An asset is an item of value that a business owns and expects to use in its operations or turn into cash. Assets are sorted by how long the item is expected to stay. A non-current asset is kept for use for more than a year: the ovens and premises of a Windhoek bakery, a farmer's tractor, a delivery vehicle. A current asset is expected to be used, sold or turned into cash within a year: inventories such as flour waiting to be baked, trade receivables owed by credit customers, and cash itself. The same van shows the test: kept for deliveries it is a non-current asset; held for resale it is part of inventories.

### Liabilities: what the business owes

A liability is a debt the business owes to an outside person or business. Current liabilities fall due within a year: the trade payables owed to suppliers who sold on credit, wages owing at the end of the month, and a bank overdraft, which the bank may ask to be repaid at short notice. Non-current liabilities fall due after more than a year, most often a long-term bank loan repayable over several years. The one-year test that sorts assets sorts liabilities the same way, because debts paid soon need cash in the coming months, while a loan spread over years gives room to pay.

### Sorting items in practice

Keetmanshoop Tool Hire owns its workshop and a fleet of drills and ladders, holds fuel and spares for the coming weeks, is owed money by a builder who rented on credit, and owes money to its hardware supplier, on a five-year loan and on an overdraft. The workshop and fleet are non-current assets; the fuel, spares and the builder's debt are current assets; the supplier's invoice and the overdraft are current liabilities; the loan is a non-current liability. A bicycle repair shop in Copenhagen sorts the same way: its spanners are non-current assets, the inner tubes on its shelf are inventories.

## Interpreting a statement of financial position

### A snapshot on one date

A statement of financial position lists the assets a business owns, the liabilities it owes and the capital invested by its owners, at one point in time. It is a snapshot: it answers 'where does the business stand on this date?', whereas the income statement reports profit over a period, and profit is not the same as cash. Reading starts with the headings: non-current assets are the long-term resources the business works with; current assets and current liabilities show the short-term flow of goods, credit and cash; capital and liabilities together answer the financing question.

### Worked example: reading the finance mix

On 31 December 2025 the statement of Ongwediva Hardware, a tool retailer, shows non-current assets of N$40 000; current assets of inventories N$12 500, trade receivables N$9 400 and cash N$3 200; capital N$45 100; a long-term loan of N$10 000; and current liabilities of trade payables N$8 000 and an overdraft N$2 000. Each side totals 65 100.

Deductions. Financing: capital supplies about 69 per cent of the funds, the loan about 15 per cent and short-term credit about 15 per cent, so the business is financed mainly from its owners' money. Assets: current assets of N$25 100 against N$10 000 of current liabilities leave a margin of N$15 100, suggesting near-term debts are covered; a lender is likely to read the small overdraft as modest borrowing.

### Selling inventories to raise finance

When a business is short of cash it can sell part of its inventories. Condition: it holds goods buyers want. Mechanism: the goods pass to buyers and their value arrives as cash - finance raised from inside the business rather than borrowed. Consequence: the cash can clear a pressing overdraft or supplier, easing the strain on the business. The costs run the same chain in reverse: goods sold in a hurry may go for less than their usual price, and lines sold off are lines no longer earning future revenue, so the relief is one-off.
