## Economic sectors and their changing importance

### Primary, secondary and tertiary

Businesses are classified by what they do with materials. The primary sector takes materials from the natural world: farming, fishing, forestry and mining. The secondary sector turns materials into finished goods, through manufacturing or construction. The tertiary sector provides services rather than a physical product, such as transport, banking and retailing.

Place the classification in examples. A Karas lamb farm is primary. An Ongwediva furniture maker, turning planks into desks, is secondary. A Windhoek courier firm, delivering parcels, is tertiary. The basis also travels beyond the region: a Chilean copper mine is primary wherever it operates, and a Norwegian fish cannery is secondary wherever its tins are packed.

### The basis is the stage of the work

The test that places a business is the stage its work occupies between the natural world and the customer. Taking or growing the material: primary. Transforming the material into a product: secondary. Providing a service rather than a product: tertiary.

A bakery that mills its own flour straddles two stages - milling is secondary work, and selling bread over the counter is a retail service. Where a business spans stages, the sector given is the one describing the main stage of its activity: a bakery whose main work is baking is classified as secondary.

### Why the importance of the sectors changes

The importance of a sector means its share of a country's output and jobs. That share is not fixed; it shifts as an economy develops, and the shifts have names. Industrialisation is the earlier shift, in which manufacturing grows out of a mainly primary-sector economy. Deindustrialisation is the later shift, in which secondary-sector manufacturing tends to shrink as a share of the economy while tertiary services grow.

The reason behind the shifts is usually rising income and new technology. As households earn more, they spend a growing share of their income on services, so service businesses grow. As machines and automation let each factory worker produce far more, the same output needs fewer workers, so manufacturing's share of jobs falls even while its output stays high.

### How the shares look in a developed and a developing economy

In a developed economy such as Norway, most output and jobs are already tertiary. Banking, shipping services and tourism employ far more people than the fish cannery; the cannery itself runs automated lines needing few workers. Because household incomes are high, spending runs towards services, which is why the tertiary share tends to keep rising.

In Namibia the pattern differs. A large share of its people are still in primary work - farming, fishing and mining - and extractive industries such as uranium and diamonds make up much of the country's exports. Over time the pattern shifts: mechanisation on farms means fewer people are needed per hectare, so workers tend to move towards towns and service work such as taxis, security and retail - the same path the developed economies took, at a different stage.

### Reading the shares in a real case

Example worked through. A Ministry of Labour report counts workers this way: farm and fishing work 240 000 jobs, factory work 60 000 jobs, services 300 000 jobs. The services group is the largest, and the primary group still holds a far larger share than in a developed economy, which suggests an economy at a developing stage.

Now suppose a factory automation programme halves the factory jobs over a decade. The secondary share falls, and the workers who move tend to reappear in the services count. That single change explains how a country's classification shares can shift without any single business changing sector - the people move, and the totals follow.

### Keeping the two shifts apart

Deindustrialisation is not the same as a country becoming poorer. In fact, a shrinking secondary share of jobs often happens because factories produce more with fewer workers, while total output is still rising - the work moves to machines and to services, not to nowhere. The test that tells the ideas apart: ask whether the total amount being produced has fallen (a genuine decline) or only the share held by manufacturing (a shift in classification importance).

## Private sector and public sector in a mixed economy

### Private sector and public sector

The private sector is the part of a mixed economy owned and controlled by individuals or companies, usually run for profit. The public sector is the part owned and controlled by the state, usually run to provide services the population needs rather than to profit. A mixed economy is one that contains both, and Namibia's economy is mixed: Oshakati Premier Juice, a private juice processor, sits in the private sector; NamWater, the state-owned water utility, sits in the public sector.

### The features that separate the two sectors

Three features tell the sectors apart. Ownership: private-sector businesses belong to private owners or shareholders, whereas public-sector organisations belong to the state. Main aim: private-sector businesses are usually run for profit, whereas public-sector organisations usually aim to provide a service the population needs. Control: private owners and shareholders direct their own businesses, whereas government ministers and officials direct the public sector.

Applying the features settles any case. A Walvis Bay fishing company owned by a family and run for profit is private sector on all three features. A state school is public sector on all three. Outside the region the test works the same way: in the United Kingdom, the National Health Service is public sector on ownership, aim and control, while a family-run London restaurant is private sector. Ownership, aim and control decide the classification together, which is why sorting a business between the sectors gives one answer, not three.
