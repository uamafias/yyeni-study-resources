## Profit, its importance, and the difference between profit and cash

### How a profit is made

Profit is the surplus that remains when a business's total costs are subtracted from its revenue for a period. Revenue is the income earned from sales in that period; total costs are all the costs of earning it, from materials and wages to rent and power. A business makes a profit when revenue exceeds total costs, because every cost of the period has been covered and a surplus is left. When total costs run higher than revenue, the same calculation gives a negative figure, which is a loss.

Worked figures: Kuiseb Candles in Windhoek sold 4 000 candles at N$25 each in May, giving revenue of N$100 000. Its wax, wicks, wages, rent and power for the month came to N$83 500. Profit is revenue less total costs: 100 000 - 83 500 = 16 500, a profit of N$16 500 for May. The point of the figures is the comparison: a business does not make a profit by selling a lot, but by taking in more than it pays out.

### Why profit matters to private-sector businesses

Profit matters to the owners of a private-sector business for several connected reasons.

First, it is the reward for risk-taking and enterprise. Owners put their own money and effort into a business with no certainty of return, so profit is the payment that makes the risk worth taking; a private-sector business with no prospect of profit is unlikely to attract owners in the first place. Second, profit is a source of finance: the part of profit kept in the business, called retained profit, is an internal source of finance that can fund new equipment or stock without borrowing or giving away any ownership. Third, profit is a signal. A business earning profit is covering its costs, which tends to reassure customers that it will still be there next year, and it makes the business a safer prospect for anyone lending to it or supplying it. Fourth, profit supports survival: losses, sustained, drain the funds a business needs to operate, while profit rebuilds them.

### Profit kept in the business: a worked chain

Consider Ongeluli Guest House in Opuwo. In its first year it earned revenue of N$310 000 against total costs of N$280 000, giving a profit of N$30 000: 310 000 - 280 000 = 30 000. The owner takes N$10 000 out as drawings, leaving N$20 000 of retained profit inside the business.

That retained profit is an internal source of finance. If the guest house wants to add two rooms at a cost of N$20 000, it can fund the whole build from its own profit and avoid interest on a loan. The chain runs: profit is made, part is kept in, the kept part funds the next step of the plan, and the business advances without owing anyone. The condition is that the profit must actually be kept in. Profit taken out as drawings funds nothing, and how much to take out is itself a decision the profit figures inform.

### Profit and cash are not the same

Profit and cash are two different measures that answer different questions, and the difference is a matter of timing. Profit is the surplus of revenue over costs, earned across a period. Cash is the money the business actually holds - notes, coins and its bank balance - which it can spend at once.

The two come apart because of credit. A sale made today counts in revenue, and so in profit, the moment it is made, but if the customer pays in thirty days, the cash arrives thirty days later. Liquidity - having cash available when payments fall due - is therefore a separate matter from profitability. A business can be profitable and short of cash at the same time, and a business can hold cash while making a loss, because a loan or the owner's savings arrive as cash without any revenue being earned.

Profit answers "has the business earned more than it spent over this period?"; cash answers "what can the business pay today?".

### Two businesses that prove the point

Swakop Contracting completed a large job in March: revenue earned of N$180 000 against total costs of N$148 000, a profit of N$32 000. But the client settles invoices in sixty days, and the wages fall due every Friday. In April the business is profitable on paper and still cannot meet its payroll from its own money, because the cash is still in the client's hands. The remedy is cash-flow management - negotiating payment dates, arranging an overdraft - which is the subject of topic 5.2.

The reverse case is Tsumeb Tea Rooms in its opening month. The owner put N$50 000 of savings into the business bank account, so the business held plenty of cash, but revenue of N$9 000 against total costs of N$14 000 was a loss of N$5 000. Holding cash is not the same as earning profit, and a new business often holds the first without making the second.

## The income statement and its use in decision-making

### What an income statement shows

An income statement is a financial statement that records a business's trading performance over a period, usually a year. It answers one question: did the business earn a surplus, and where did the surplus go? Its main features, in order, are revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, profit and retained profit.

Revenue is the income from sales in the period. Cost of sales is the direct cost of the goods sold - for a retailer, the price paid to suppliers for the items sold; for a maker, the materials and direct labour that went into them. Gross profit is what remains when cost of sales is taken from revenue. Expenses, such as rent, administration and marketing, are then deducted, giving profit - the surplus for the period. Retained profit is the part of that profit kept in the business after the owners have taken their share out. Reading the statement is what matters here: assembling one from a blank page is not part of what this level assesses.

### The two subtractions

Gross profit = revenue - cost of sales. Profit = gross profit - expenses. Two steps, and the order is fixed: cost of sales first, because it belongs directly to the goods sold; expenses after, because they belong to running the business as a whole.

Worked figures for Walvis Bay Wholesale, year to December: revenue of N$60 000 and cost of sales of N$24 000 give gross profit of N$36 000, because 60 000 - 24 000 = 36 000. Expenses of N$21 500 then bring profit to N$14 500, because 36 000 - 21 500 = 14 500. The owners take N$4 500 out as drawings, giving retained profit of N$10 000, because 14 500 - 4 500 = 10 000.

### Reading the statement to decide

An income statement earns its place when its figures change a decision. A manager comparing two years asks why profit moved: if gross profit fell while revenue rose, the problem sits in buying or pricing, and if gross profit held while profit fell, the problem sits in expenses. Comparing options works the same way: two products, two branches or two prices can each be reduced to the profit each would produce, and the larger profit usually wins the argument.

The figures deserve caution, though. A statement describes one period, and the next period may not repeat it - demand can weaken, input prices can rise - so a decision based on profit figures carries the condition that the assumptions behind them hold. Profit is not the whole story either: a choice that maximises this year's profit while wearing out the machines or the staff may be the worse one over time.

### A decision worked through

Otavi Auto Spares sold parts worth N$82 000 last year at a cost of sales of N$49 200, giving a gross profit of N$32 800. Expenses of N$27 300 left a profit of N$5 500. The owner is weighing a Sunday opening that forecasts extra revenue of N$6 500, extra parts costs of N$3 900 and extra wages of N$1 500.

The forecast adds up: 6 500 - 3 900 - 1 500 = 1 100, an addition of about N$1 100 of profit a year, lifting profit from N$5 500 to roughly N$6 600, an increase of about a fifth. Judged on the statement's own logic, the opening earns its keep. The decision still depends on the forecast holding: if Sunday sales come in at half the forecast, the extra costs overtake the extra revenue and the opening loses money instead.

### An income statement is not a cash-flow forecast

An income statement usually looks back: it records the revenue, costs and profit of a period that has ended, counting a sale when it is made even if the customer pays later. A cash-flow forecast looks forward: it estimates the cash expected in and out of the bank month by month, including loans and the owner's capital. The test: is the document about profit earned in a past period, or cash expected in future months?
