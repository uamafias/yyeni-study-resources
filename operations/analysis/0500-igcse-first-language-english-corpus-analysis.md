## 1. The ten skills the paper assesses

0500 is not examined by topic. It is examined by **skill**: five reading skills and five writing skills, each tested in named tasks. This is the most important structural fact about the subject, and the plan must be built on it.

| skill | what the syllabus says it is | where it is tested |
|---|---|---|
| **R1** | understanding of explicit meanings | every reading task |
| **R2** | understanding of implicit meanings and attitudes | every reading task |
| **R3** | analyse, evaluate and develop facts, ideas and opinions, with support from the text | P1 Q3 (15 marks); P2 Directed Writing reading (15) |
| **R4** | understanding of how writers achieve effects and influence readers | P1 Q2 — the short questions and the language task (25) |
| **R5** | select and use information for specific purposes | P1 Q1 comprehension and summary (25); P2 Directed Writing |
| **W1** | articulate experience and express what is thought, felt and imagined | P1 Q3; P2 both sections |
| **W2** | organise and structure ideas and opinions for deliberate effect | P1 summary and Q3; P2 both sections |
| **W3** | a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context | P1 summary and Q3; P2 both sections |
| **W4** | register appropriate to context | P1 Q3; P2 both sections |
| **W5** | accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar | **Paper 2 only** since 2024 |

Reading and writing each carry 80 of the 160 marks: Paper 1 is 65 reading + 15 writing, Paper 2 is 15 reading + 65 writing.

Across all Paper 1 items, the commonest single skill tested is **R1, explicit meaning** (181 item-rows), then **R2, implicit meaning** (87). Almost every reading mark starts with getting the literal meaning right.

## 2. What a grade takes

Median minimum raw marks across the 17 series, for components 12 and 22 (thresholds are published per component):

| | Paper 1 /80 | Paper 2 /80 | overall /160 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **A\*** | — | — | 118 (74%) |
| **A** | 49 (61%) | 55 (69%) | 103 (64%) |
| **C** | 35 (44%) | 42 (53%) | 78 (49%) |
| **E** | 22 (28%) | 30 (38%) | — |

**Paper 1 is the harder paper**: a C there needs 44% of the marks, against 53% on Paper 2.

**November boundaries are higher than June in every year from 2021 to 2025**, and the 5 October 2026 sitting belongs to the November series. In the last three November series (2023–2025) an overall C needed 80–83/160, an A 107–110, and an A\* 121–124.

In band terms (our arithmetic, not Cambridge's): a **C on Paper 2** is roughly **Level 4 in all four level tables**, meaning "sometimes effective", "generally well sequenced", "begins to evaluate". An **A on Paper 2** is roughly **low Level 5 throughout**. On Paper 1 a C is roughly **Level 3 on the extended tasks plus about 60% of the short items**; an A is **Level 4 across the extended tasks**.

## 3. Paper 1 Reading, task by task

Three texts, 2 hours, 80 marks. Text A (700–750 words) for comprehension, Text B (700–750) for the summary, Text C (500–650) for Questions 2 and 3.

### Question 1(a)–(e) · Comprehension · 15 marks · R1, R2, R5

**How it is set and marked.** Seven point-marked items in a fixed layout: 1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3 marks. Each names the paragraph to use. Two-mark "using your own words, explain…" items need **both strands** of an idea explained for both marks.

**What examiners keep reporting (series raising each theme, of 17).** Copying instead of restating in one's own words (17); explaining only one part of a two-part idea (16); missing what the text implies (15); adding material beyond the point, which can undo a correct answer (14); answering from outside the paragraph the question names (9).

**The learner must be able to:** find the answer in the paragraph named, and only there; give just what the question asks; restate both parts of an idea in their own words; read what a writer implies as well as what they state.

### Question 1(f) · Summary · 15 marks · 10 reading (R1, R2, R5) + 5 writing (W2, W3)

**How it is set.** A selective summary of Text B on a stated focus (for example, the *positives* of something), in continuous writing of **no more than 120 words**, in the candidate's own words.

**How it is marked.** Two level tables.
- *Reading, out of 10, five levels.* The top band (9–10) requires understanding of a wide range of relevant ideas, consistent focus, and points selected skilfully enough to show an **overview**. The middle band (5–6) shows some loss of focus and may include excess material. The bottom bands are a list of unconnected ideas or indiscriminate selection.
- *Writing, out of 5, three levels.* The top band (4–5) is clear, fluent, mostly concise, well organised and in the candidate's own words, with well-chosen vocabulary. The bottom band (1) lacks concision, is too long or very brief, and may lift sections of the text.

**What examiners keep reporting (every theme in 16–17 of 17 series).**
- Lifting from the text. The single most repeated fault.
- Overlong explanations of a few ideas, instead of concise coverage of many.
- Examples and incidental detail included; stronger answers replace several specific details with one **umbrella phrase** that captures them.
- A list instead of an organised overview.
- Candidates adding opinion or comment. The summary is not the place for either.
- Missing the precise focus of the question.

**The learner must be able to:** find every point on the stated focus; group related details under one idea; restate each idea in their own words; write organised, continuous prose within 120 words; add nothing of their own.

### Question 2(a) · Word or phrase · 4 × 1 mark · R1

Given a sentence with an underlined idea, find the word or phrase in the text that means the same. **Examiners (16 of 16):** the most successful answers give the exact word or phrase and nothing else; weaker ones copy the whole sentence, or explain the meaning instead of finding the word.

### Question 2(b) · Meaning of single words · 3 × 1 mark · R1, R2

Explain in one's own words what the writer means by each of three words. **Examiners (16 of 16):** the most successful consider the word *as it is used in this context*; weaker ones confuse similar-looking words, or guess from a word's root without checking the context.

### Question 2(c) · One example, explain the effect · 3 marks · R2, R4

Choose **one** example from a short extract and explain how the writer uses it to suggest something (usually a feeling). Three marks go to an appropriate example with a full explanation. **Examiners (16 of 16):** answers that discuss more than one example waste time that 2(d) needs. The effective sequence is to explain the example's meaning in context first, then what that meaning suggests.

### Question 2(d) · Language task · 15 marks · R1, R2, R4

**How it is set.** Two paragraphs of Text C. The candidate explains how the writer uses language to convey meaning and create effect, in about 200–300 words.

**How it is marked (five levels).**
- *Top (13–15):* wide-ranging discussion of well-chosen language, with high-quality comments that add meaning and associations to the words in **both** paragraphs and show why the writer used them; imagery tackled with precision and imagination; a clear understanding of how language works.
- *Level 4 (10–12):* secure explanations of meaning in context, effects identified in both paragraphs, images recognised and partly explained.
- *Level 3 (7–9):* mostly gives meanings, and any comment on effect is basic or general. One paragraph may be handled better than the other.
- *Level 2 (4–6):* may name the device correctly without explaining why it is used; explanations few, general or repeating the text's own words.

**What examiners keep reporting (16 of 16 series).**
- Long quotations, where the best answers pick short, precise words and phrases. The strongest then examine the individual words within their choice.
- **Meaning, then effect.** Successful answers explain what the words mean in context, then what they make the reader see, feel or understand. Stopping at meaning caps the mark.
- **Naming a device is not analysis.** "The writer uses a simile" earns little on its own.
- Imagery handled literally first (what you would see happening), then figuratively.
- Both paragraphs covered evenly, with about three choices from each; the reports refer to this pattern explicitly.
- Specific comments, not general remarks about "atmosphere".

**The learner must be able to:** select about three short, rich choices per paragraph; say what each means in context; say what each suggests or makes the reader feel; explain images; cover both paragraphs.

### Question 3 · Extended response to reading · 25 marks · 15 reading (R1, R2, R3) + 10 writing (W1–W4)

**How it is set.** Text C again. The candidate writes about 250–350 words **in role**, in a given form: in the 33 papers where the form could be read, a letter (9), speech (7), interview (7), journal or diary entry (5), article (3) or report (2). **Every one of the 42 tasks has exactly three bullets** to address.

**How it is marked.**
- *Reading, five levels.* The top band (13–15) needs a thorough evaluation and analysis of the text; developed ideas sustained and related to it; a wide range of ideas; supporting detail well integrated; **all three bullets well covered**; a consistent, convincing voice. Level 3 (7–9) offers straightforward ideas, rarely develops them, uses the text mechanically, covers the bullets unevenly, and has a plain voice.
- *Writing, five levels.* The top band (9–10) has an effective register for audience and purpose, convincing and consistently appropriate language, and a sound structure. Level 3 (5–6) is clear but plain or factual, with some flaws in structure. Level 2 may copy from the text.

**What examiners keep reporting (16–17 of 17 series).** All three bullets covered evenly; ideas **developed and inferred**, not repeated; details from the text used in the candidate's own words, not copied; nothing invented that the text does not support; a voice, form and register that fit the role.

**The learner must be able to:** plan a route through all three bullets before writing; draw explicit and implicit ideas from the text; develop each one (why the person would think or feel this, what follows from it); stay inside the text; write in role, in the required form, with a consistent voice.

## 4. Paper 2 Directed Writing and Composition

2 hours, 80 marks. One compulsory Section A question and one Section B title.

### Section A · Directed Writing · 40 marks · 25 writing (W1–W5) + 15 reading (R1, R2, R3, R5)

**How it is set.** Two texts totalling 650–750 words, and a task to write a discursive, argumentative or persuasive **letter, speech or article** of about 250–350 words. In the 24 papers where the form could be read, it was a letter 13 times, a speech 6 and an article 5. **All 32 readable tasks open their first bullet with "evaluate"** — the ideas, views or attitudes in the texts. All 32 tell candidates to base their answer on both texts **and to use their own words**.

**How it is marked (two six-level tables, each descriptor tagged to its skill).**
- *Writing /25.* The top band (22–25) has a highly effective style conveying subtle meaning (W1); careful structure for the reader's benefit (W2); a wide range of sophisticated vocabulary, used precisely (W3); a highly effective register (W4); spelling, punctuation and grammar almost always accurate (W5). **Level 4 (14–17), roughly C territory:** a sometimes effective style, ideas generally well sequenced, adequate vocabulary, a sometimes effective register, generally accurate. **Level 3 (10–13): relies on the sequence of the original text**, with vocabulary simple or reliant on the original.
- *Reading /15.* The top band (13–15) **successfully evaluates** explicit and implicit ideas and opinions, and assimilates them into a developed, sophisticated response. Level 4 (7–9) begins to evaluate mainly explicit ideas. Level 3 (5–6) selects and comments on explicit ideas. Level 2 only identifies them.

**What examiners keep reporting (every theme in 17 of 17 series).**
- **Evaluate, don't summarise.** Weaker answers paraphrase and list the texts' ideas; answers marked low Level 3 and below contain much copied material. The strongest weigh conflicting views with confidence.
- **Use both texts, and use each to test the other.** Effective answers use one text's points to question or reinterpret the other's.
- **Own words.** Lifting is the most frequent single complaint on this task.
- **Organise by argument, not by text order.** Answers that follow the order of the source texts come out disconnected or contradictory.
- **Form and register.** The letter should read as a letter and the speech as a speech, written for the named audience.
- **Both bullets.** The second bullet (advice, a response, what should be done) is often thinly handled.
- Accuracy, which counts here.

**The learner must be able to:** read two texts for both stated and implied views; judge them — which are convincing and why, where they conflict, what they overlook; build an argument of their own, organised by idea; use the texts' ideas in their own words; write in the register and conventions of the required form; be accurate.

### Section B · Composition · 40 marks · 16 content and structure (W1, W2) + 24 style and accuracy (W3, W4, W5)

**How it is set.** One title from four: **two descriptive, two narrative**. About 350–450 words.

**How it is marked (two six-level tables).**
- *Content and structure /16.* The general top band (14–16) has content that is complex, engaging and effective, and a structure that is secure, well balanced and carefully managed for deliberate effect. For **description**, many well-defined, developed ideas and images create a convincing overall picture with variety of focus. For **narrative**, the plot is well-defined and strongly developed, using features of fiction — description, characterisation, an effective climax and convincing detail. At Level 4 (8–10) a description offers a relevant selection of ideas and images, *even where it tends toward narrative*, and a narrative is relevant and cohesive with some characterisation and setting.
- *Style and accuracy /24.* The top band (21–24) has precise, well-chosen vocabulary and varied sentence structures **chosen for effect**, a consistently apt register, and near-perfect accuracy. Level 4 (13–16) has some precise vocabulary, a range of structures sometimes used for effect, and is generally accurate with some errors. Level 3 (9–12) has simple vocabulary, straightforward sentences and frequent errors.

**What examiners keep reporting (16 of 16 series).** The key messages of almost every series tell candidates to know **the different kinds of content required for description and for narration**. The recurring failures are:
- A description that becomes a sequence of events (one report's example is a day-by-day holiday account).
- Cliché in place of observed detail.
- Narratives with too many events and no credible shape, or no real ending.

What succeeds:
- Specific, sensory detail that keeps a description descriptive.
- A structural idea that shapes the piece, such as one report's search through an overgrown garden that ends on a discovery.
- A credible, contained plot with character and a resolution.
- Simple and complex sentences mixed deliberately to create atmosphere.

**The learner must be able to:**
- *in description:* build a picture from precise sensory detail, shifting focus, and create atmosphere, with no story;
- *in narrative:* tell a credible, contained story with character, tension, a climax and a resolution;
- *in both:* choose words for precision rather than for show, vary sentences for effect, and write accurately.

## 5. The examiners' standing advice

The "key messages" at the head of each paper's report barely change from 2020 to 2025. For **Paper 1**, candidates did well when they:
- worked through the tasks in order and answered every part;
- based each answer on the right text and section;
- used their own words where required, without unselective copying;
- stayed with the text's ideas rather than inventing material or adding opinion;
- noted the marks and managed their time;
- planned the longer answers, and gave equal attention to every part;
- followed the task guidance: 120 words for the summary, one example in 2(c), both paragraphs in 2(d);
- checked and edited.

For **Paper 2**, high marks needed:
- the right form, style and register in both answers;
- writing structured to persuade or engage;
- clear knowledge of what description and narration each require;
- detailed, evocative description or a credible, engaging narrative;
- varied, accurate sentences;
- precise and wide-ranging vocabulary.

## 6. Command words and task forms

The syllabus lists four command words: **Describe, Explain, Give, Identify**. The papers use them like this:

| word | where it appears in the 84 papers |
|---|---|
| **Identify** | 132 Paper 1 stems, all 1 mark; 130 of them are the word-or-phrase items of Q2(a) |
| **Explain** | 244 Paper 1 stems: 119 one-mark "using your own words, explain" meanings of single words (Q2(b)); 84 two-mark own-words explanations (Q1(b)); 40 three-mark "explain how" items on one example (Q2(c)), plus one three-mark Q1 item |
| **Give** | 27 Paper 1 stems; 24 of them are one-mark retrievals of a reason or detail in Q1(a) |
| **Describe** | opens 48 of the 168 Paper 2 composition titles (the rest open with "Write") |

Counts are of the first command word in each mark-scheme stem, one count per question part.

**0500 is task-led, not command-word-led.** The extended tasks are instructions (write a letter, summarise, analyse the language of these paragraphs), not command words. So practice items should use the papers' own task forms and length limits, with the four command words where the papers use them.

## 7. What this means for the notes and flashcards

*Our reading of the evidence above, not a transcription.*

- **Organise the resource by task and skill, not by topic.** 0500 has no content syllabus; the tasks are the subject.
- **Technique cards**, one per move the examiners reward: grouping several details under an umbrella phrase; the meaning-then-effect sequence for language analysis; planning a route through three bullets; turning a source's view into an evaluated point; the content that belongs in a description and the content that belongs in a narrative.
- **Discrimination cards** for the confusions the reports name every year: lifting versus own words; summarising versus evaluating; naming a device versus explaining its effect; describing versus narrating; meaning versus effect.
- **Practice tasks on original short texts**, written for the purpose and set in Namibian and southern African contexts, each with a model answer and the band descriptors (paraphrased) to self-assess against. Cambridge's own texts cannot be reproduced.
- **Bloom's levels spread higher than in Business.** Comprehension is Understand; selecting for a summary is Analyse; language analysis is Analyse; Directed Writing's reading marks are Evaluate. Composition, Question 3 and Directed Writing's writing marks are genuinely **Create**, the level that was almost unused for Business.

## 8. Limits of this analysis

- The strong/weak tagging of 19,216 report sentences is automatic and noisy. The counts given here are **how many series raise each theme**, matched by keyword. Some theme patterns are broad, so a count of 16 or 17 shows the theme is discussed every series, not how prominent it is. The descriptions are ours, written after reading the example sentences.
- Paper 2's task form could not be read in 10 of 42 papers, and Question 3's form in 9 of 42. The form counts are for readable papers only.
- The band-to-grade translation in section 2 is our arithmetic, not Cambridge's.
